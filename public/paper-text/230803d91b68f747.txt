A B S T R A C T
Continuous human activity recognition (HAR) using millimeter-wave radar is a challenging task, primarily
due to issues such as activity transition ambiguity and over-segmentation. To address these challenges, we
propose CSARNet, a novel Cross-scale Adaptive Refinement Network that integrates frame-level classification
with explicit boundary prediction. The core of our approach lies in a dual-branch architecture: a Frame-level
Activity Recognition Branch (FARB) for per-frame classification and a dedicated Boundary Prediction Branch
(BPB) to accurately localize activity transitions. Furthermore, we introduce a Fusion Stage enhanced by a
cross-attention mechanism to effectively integrate features from both branches, thereby improving temporal
consistency and segmentation accuracy. To rigorously evaluate the generalization capability of CSARNet, we
conduct extensive experiments on a publicly available dataset comprising 40,000 continuous gesture sequences
from 25 participants across diverse environments and spatial locations. The results demonstrate that CSARNet
significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods, achieving an F1@50 score of 83.3%, an edit score of 75.2,
and a frame-wise accuracy of 95.9% on our private dataset. Critically, on the open-source benchmark, CSARNet
attains even higher performance with an F1@50 of 85.6%, an edit score of 76.9, and an accuracy of 96.2%,
establishing a new state of the art. These consistent gains across datasets underscore the effectiveness of our
boundary-aware design and its robustness in handling complex, real-world continuous HAR scenarios.
1. Introduction
In recent years, millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar-based human
activity recognition (HAR) has gained widespread adoption in ap-
plications such as smart homes, healthcare monitoring, and security
surveillance, owing to its unique sensing modality that leverages elec-
tromagnetic waves in the 60–100 GHz band. Compared to vision or
wearable sensors, mmWave radar operates reliably under low-light, oc-
clusion, and privacy-sensitive conditions, while providing fine-grained
micro-Doppler and range information through frequency-modulated
continuous wave (FMCW) signal processing. These inherent advantages
— non-contact operation, high precision, and strong robustness to
environmental interference — make it particularly suitable for real-
world HAR scenarios [1–3]. However, current research efforts have
predominantly focused on discrete activity classification [4], which
presents significant limitations when applied to real-world scenarios
where human activities naturally occur as continuous, temporally-
evolving sequences. The current fragmented recognition algorithm not

to suppress non-motion intervals, though misfires by this detector can
significantly hurt recognition performance [15]. Further improvements
include distributed multi-radar fusion [13], IMU-adar fusion [14], and
multi-perspective spectral graph fusion [9,15]. Nevertheless, all these
variants remain fundamentally limited by the fixed-window assump-
tion: when multiple activities fall within a single window, the method
inevitably suffers from boundary blurring and misclassification.
For multi-target detection algorithms, they present significant ad-
vantages over traditional sliding window algorithms, and they treat
each activity in a continuous sequence as an independent target for
detection and classification, avoiding the issue of blurred action bound-
aries caused by sliding window [17–21] as shown in Fig. 1(b). For
example, Xu et al. proposed a mmWave radar-based continuous motion
detection approach algorithm called mmCMD, which enhanced the
quality of micro-Doppler spectrums through dynamic feature visualiza-
tion (DFV) and designed an algorithm called You Only Look Once with
Micro-Doppler Matrix (YOLO-MDM) incorporating fusion squeeze-and-
excitation (FSE) modules and coordinate attention (CA), achieving 93%
mAP and 99% F1 score in the detection of 12 action classes [21]. Yin
et al. proposed a MOR-LNOD algorithm that uses a lightweight network
(LNet) and a one-dimensional (1D) boundary loss (ODBL) function, sim-
plifying action detection to regional division in the time dimension, and
achieving 96.5% accuracy in the recognition of 8 action classes with
only 1/10 of the parameters of ResNet50 [22]. Despite these advances,
multi-target detection methods often generate redundant detections for
a single action, miss short-duration activities due to coarse temporal
anchors or weak features, and require fixed-length input segments,
which conflicts with the inherently variable-length nature of real-world
radar streams.
Compared to both, frame-level HAR algorithms [23–27] assign pre-
dictions at every time step, enabling precise modeling of activity tran-
sitions and natural support for variable-length inputs through masking
as shown in Fig. 1(c). Examples include Bi-LSTM models that leverage
bidirectional context [23,26], Bayesian RNNs that quantify prediction
uncertainty [24,25], and attention-enhanced TCNs like R-ATCN that
capture long-range dependencies and optimize evaluation via Short-
Term Tolerance Scoring [27]. However, these methods suffer from
severe prediction jitter in transition zones — manifesting as unstable,
rapidly switching labels between adjacent frames — which undermines
segmentation reliability despite strong per-frame accuracy.
To address the aforementioned challenges particularly the severe
prediction jitter in transition regions that plagues existing frame-level
HAR methods—we propose a dual-branch cascaded network called
CSARNet for continuous HAR, as shown in Fig. 1(d). The core idea is to
leverage the strengths of frame-level modeling in capturing fine-grained
temporal dynamics and handling variable-length inputs, while simulta-
neously introducing a dedicated Boundary Prediction Branch (BPB) to
explicitly localize activity transitions. By integrating the outputs of the
Frame-level Activity Recognition Branch (FARB) and the BPB through
a confidence-weighted voting strategy. The key contributions of this
paper are summarized as follows:

2.1. Limitations of traditional algorithms
To illustrate common challenges in continuous HAR, consider the
Doppler-time spectrogram shown in Fig. 2, which depicts a continuous
HAR sequence comprising six distinct activities along with a stationary
state. Specifically, the dataset includes the following seven states:A0:
Standing still A1: Sitting down A2: Walking slowly A3: Walking For-
ward A4: Bending A5: Picking up A6: Fully squat walking. Next, the
activity transition ambiguity and input constraints are discussed in
detail.
Activity Transition Ambiguity Fig. 2 clearly demonstrates the activity
transition ambiguity phenomenon, where transitional periods between
activities exhibit overlapping characteristic features from multiple mo-
tion patterns. This inter-class feature mixing is particularly evident
during the A2-to-A3 transition, where the simultaneous presence of
running and sitting signatures creates significant challenges for precise
activity state determination.
Input Limitation Traditional sliding window algorithms divide radar
signals into fixed lengths such as 2 s, but they face two major problems:
first, the window length is difficult to adapt to the duration of different
activities for example, A1 requires a 2.5 s window, while A2 needs a
4 s window. Too short, and it truncates the action; too long, and it
introduces irrelevant noise. Second, the window boundaries may cut off
key features of the activity like the starting frame of a walking forward
being split into two windows. For multi-target detection algorithm, it
requires the input data to be of fixed length, while the spectrum in
practical applications is often variable in length. Moreover, this way
might allocate several distinct identification results to the same location
on the Doppler-Time spectrum, which is impractical in real-world
scenarios.
The adverse impact of these issues is quantitatively validated in
Section 4, where baseline methods exhibit elevated edit distances and
prediction jitter, while our CSARNet achieves significantly higher F1

3. Proposed mmWave-CSARNet
In this section, a two-branch cascaded staged mmWave continuous
HAR network called CSARNet is proposed, whose architecture is shown
in Fig. 3(c). The network takes the Doppler-Time spectrum 𝐼 ∈ R𝑇 ×𝐹
as the input, where 𝑇 is the number of temporal frames and 𝐹 is
the frequency dimension obtained by FFT. The two branches of the
proposed CSARNet are the FARB and the BPB. FARB realizes the
progressive enhancement of classification ability through multi-stage
feature distillation, and its output is the activity category probability
distribution 𝑆𝑖
𝑡 ∈ R𝑇 ×𝐴𝑐 , where 𝐴𝑐 is the total number of predefined
activity classes, and 𝑆𝑖
𝑡 is the prediction confidence of the 𝑖th stage.
BPB uses several 1D convolution kernels with residual structure to ex-
tract boundary-sensitive features and generates a boundary confidence
distribution 𝐵 ∈ R𝑇 ×1. The two branches achieve feature interaction
through proposed cross-attention mechanisms. Specifically, BPB injects
temporal constraint information into FARB’s feature learning process
to effectively align frame-level predictions with boundary confidence
scores and facilitate boundary identification. A cascaded loss 𝑐𝑠 and a
cross-attention loss 𝑐𝑟𝑜𝑠𝑠 are used to supervise this process. Finally,
a confidence-weighted dual-branch voting fusion strategy is adopted
to achieve collaborative optimization of spatiotemporal features and
boundary information, resulting in the final continuous HAR result.
Next, a detailed explanation of the proposed FARB, BPB, and voting
strategies, along with the associated loss functions, is provided.
3.1. Frame-level activity recognition branch
For FARB, it is a cascaded staged frame-level activity network with
an adaptive fusion mechanism, its input is the Doppler-Time spectrum
𝐼 ∈ R𝑇 ×𝐹 , and its main architecture consists of 𝑁𝑠 cascaded stages
and one fusion stage. For long-time feature extraction, the advanced
dual dilated layer (DDL) and dilated residual layer (DRL) in multi-
stage temporal convolutional network (MS-TCN) are used as the basic
units [29,30], as shown in Figs. 3(d) and 3(e). For DRL, the 1 × 1 con-
volution before and after adjusts the dimension of the input features,
then dilated convolution is used for time-domain feature extraction,
and the dilation factor of each layer is doubled, 𝑑𝑖𝑙𝑎𝑡𝑒𝑑 = 2𝑙, where 𝑙 is
the number of current layer. Unlike DRL, DDL enhances the receptive
field of the lower layers by combining two different dilated factors,
dilated = 2𝑙 and dilated = 2𝑁𝐷−𝑙. For each DDL and DRL block, they
are composed of 𝑁𝑅 DRL and 𝑁𝐷 DDL respectively. This design is able
to capture long-term dependencies and has stronger temporal modeling
capabilities in early stage, which is beneficial for continuous HAR.
For the cascaded staged network, it has been widely used in fields
such as object detection [31], pose estimation [32], and semantic seg-
mentation [33–36]. These studies show that the early cascaded stages
networks can extract explicit motion patterns, while the later stages
combine deep semantic representations to optimize the discriminability
of boundary-blurred frames. To better fuse features from different
stages, an AMFDM is used, giving higher attention to blurry frames,
thus achieving more accurate predictions. Additionally, drawing in-
spiration from densenet [37,38], the first 𝑁𝑠 stages in the cascade
use the Dopper-Time spectrum along with the concatenation of all
previous stages as input for the subsequent stage. This dense residual
structure effectively captures feature information at different stages and
prevents model overfitting. For the final fusion stage, proposed AMFDM
adaptively modifies the importance of frame-level HAR across previous
all stages. Specifically, each stage is allocated an initial weight denoted

3.4. Voting strategy
To achieve the final continuous HAR result, a confidence-weighted
dual-branch voting fusion strategy is employed. The voting strategy is
designed to combine the outputs of the FARB and the BPB to produce
a more accurate and stable prediction as shown in Algorithm 1. The
algorithm takes the frame-wise class prediction scores from FARB and
the boundary prediction scores from BPB as inputs. It first applies a
sigmoid function to normalize the boundary scores, then detects the
peaks in the boundary scores to identify potential activity boundaries.
For each detected segment, it performs a majority vote among the
predicted classes within that segment. If there is a tie, it uses the model
confidence to break the tie. Finally, it returns the refined segment-level
labels.

4. Experimental results
4.1. Experimental setup
During the model training, the time dimension of the input Doppler-
Time spectrum can be dynamically changed, and their FFT point num-
ber is set to 1024. For the obtained Doppler-Time spectrum, use a
time window length of 0.2 s, an overlap ratio of 80%, and a frequency
resolution of 1024. For the configuration of network hyperparameters,
𝑁𝑠 is also set to 3, in DDL and DRL, 𝑁𝐷 and 𝑁𝑅 are both set to 10,
which matches the FFT feature number of 210 in FARB. In BPB, 𝑁𝐵 is set
to 3. The value of 𝑁𝑠, 𝑁𝐷 and 𝑁𝑅 are selected based on experimental
validation, which is discussed in detail later. All 1D convolution kernels
have a size of 1 × 3, a stride of 1, and padding of 1. The optimizer
of model is Adam, with a learning rate of 0.0005 and a batch size of
1. The model underwent 40 epochs of training, and an early stopping
strategy is implemented to avoid overfitting. The proposed CSARNet
is trained on a GeForce RTX 4070Ti, using Python 3.8 and PyTorch
1.10 with CUDA 11.3. To ensure a fair and realistic evaluation of
generalization across different users, we adopt a subject-independent
leave-one-subject-out cross-validation (LOSO) protocol. Specifically, in
each fold, the model is trained on data from all subjects except one,
and tested on the held-out subject. The final performance metrics are
reported as the average over all folds.
4.2. Dataset collection
The radar data is collected in three different environments as shown
in Fig. 4(a–c), including a laboratory environment, a corridor, and an
apartment. For continuous HAR, data collection is extremely difficult,
especially for some rapid movements, which pose even greater chal-
lenges. To simplify the data collection process, spectrums of different
individual activities are concatenated to form the training spectrums.
4800 data samples are collected (8𝑝𝑎𝑟𝑡𝑖𝑐𝑖𝑝𝑎𝑛𝑡𝑠×6𝑎𝑐𝑡𝑖𝑣𝑖𝑡𝑖𝑒𝑠×100𝑠𝑎𝑚𝑝𝑙𝑒𝑠)
as shown in Fig. 3(a). The volunteers consist of four males and four
females, with heights ranging from 158 to 178 cm and ages ranging
from 22 to 26 years. For the concatenation of activity spectrums, a total
of 18,000 sets of continuous activity recognition data are generated,
with 15,000 for the training dataset and 3000 for the validation set.
During testing, five different activity sequences are collected, with 200
sets of data for each, namely: S1: A4, A6, A5, A1, A2, A3; S2: A5,
A4, A6, A1, A2, A3; S3: A1, A2, A3, A4, A6, A5; S4: A6, A4, A5, A1,
A2, A3; and S5: A1, A2, A3, A5, A6, A4, collected in three different
environments as shown in Fig. 4. In Fig. 2, we see that A0, being
stationary, causes the smallest energy fluctuations in the Doppler-Time
spectrum. A4 and A5 exhibit matching energy fluctuation traits, and
A2 and A6 also share similar movement patterns, leading to like-for-
like changes in Doppler energy. This similarity adds to the challenge of
continuous HAR.
4.3. Comparative experiments
In order to better quantify the performance of the model, this paper
adopts three metrics to evaluate the model’s performance, including
frame-level accuracy (Acc.), F1@10, 25, 50, and segmental edit dis-
tance (Edit) [42,43]. Accuracy refers to the proportion of correctly
classified samples at the frame level to the total number of samples, and
it reflects the average accuracy of predictions at the frame level. The
F1@10, 25, 50 metric serves as a quantitative measure of the F1-score
at temporal Intersection over Union (tIoU) thresholds of 10%, 25%, and
50%. The tIoU, calculated as the ratio of the overlapping area to the
combined area of predicted and ground-truth (GT) segments, evaluates
the model’s capability to manage over-segmentation effectively. The
segmental edit distance refers to the minimum number of operations
required to convert one sequence into another, and it can reflect the
global sequential consistency of the real activity sequence. To enable
a fair comparison of computational efficiency, we report the average
inference time per sample for all methods. This metric is obtained
by running each model on 1000 randomly selected test samples un-
der identical conditions (batch size = 1). The reported time includes
the full pipeline: radar data preprocessing, model forward pass, and
post-processing.
The performance of the proposed CSARNet is compared with three
different types of models, including: sliding window algorithms, multi-
target detection algorithms, and frame-level algorithms. For a con-
sistent comparison, the sliding window algorithm employs a uniform
window length and overlap ratio for the Doppler-Time spectrum input,
specifically a 3 s window length with a 90% overlap. WinBi-LSTM [4]
achieved continuous HAR by Bi-LSTM network. WinSFS [16] proposed
a Sequential Forward Selection (SFS) algorithm combined with SVM,
achieving a continuous HAR accuracy of 84.7%. Ding et al. intro-
duced a novel Dynamic Range-Doppler Trajectory (DRDT) algorithm,
coupled with a sliding window and machine learning, called DRDT-
HAR, which achieved a continuous HAR accuracy of 95.4% [12]. For
multi-target detection algorithms, a YOLO-MDM-based algorithm called
mmCMD [21] and an MOR-LNOD [22] algorithm with the proposed

4.4. Cross-environment experiments
To assess the robustness of the proposed CSARNet model under
various environmental conditions, a set of algorithms are trained in
environment (a) and then tested in environments (b) and (c). To ensure
a fair comparison, an equal number of samples are collected from each
environment, with all volunteers positioned at a constant distance of
3 m from the radar receiver. Environmental changes, such as variations
in background clutter, multipath effects, and changes in participant
locations, can significantly affect the Doppler-Time spectrum, thus
impacting continuous HAR performance. A quantitative comparison of
the performance of eight different algorithms, including the proposed
CSARNet, is presented in Table 3.
The results indicate that the sliding window algorithm is highly
sensitive to these changes because it depends on fixed window pa-
rameters, which causes its performance to drop in new environments.
Multi-object detection algorithms are somewhat adaptable but struggle
with defining boundaries when the environment changes. Frame-level
algorithms exhibit enhanced robustness for environmental variations.
However, they continue to face challenges in ensuring temporal consis-
tency. The cross-environmental experiment evaluates the performance
of CSARNet in environments (b) and (c), following its training in
environment (a). The findings show that CSARNet beats all the other
algorithms in every measure, like F1@10,25,50, edit distance, and
frame-level accuracy. In environment (b), CSARNet has an edit distance
of 72.4 and an accuracy of 91.5%, which is way better than the best
frame-level recognition algorithm, MS-TCN, that has an edit distance
of 66.1 and an accuracy of 86.1%. In environment (c), CSARNet also
does better with an edit distance of 70.4 and an accuracy of 91.1%,
compared to MS-TCN’s 64.6 edit distance and 84.8% accuracy.

5. Discussion
The proposed CSARNet demonstrates strong generalization and
scalability, which are critical for real-world deployment of radar-
based HAR systems. First, its generalization capability is validated
through cross-environment experiments, where CSARNet consistently
outperforms existing methods despite significant variations in back-
ground clutter, multipath effects, and participant positioning. This
suggests that the dual-branch architecture-particularly the boundary-
aware design-is robust to environmental perturbations that commonly
degrade Doppler-based features.
In terms of scalability, CSARNet supports variable-length input
sequences via temporal masking, enabling seamless processing of con-
tinuous radar streams without segmentation artifacts. With only 2.14M
parameters and an inference latency of 13.9 ms per sequence, it is
well-suited for edge deployment on resource-constrained platforms.
Moreover, the modular design allows straightforward extension to ad-
ditional activity classes by retraining the classification head, and the
framework can potentially incorporate multi-modal inputs through
feature-level fusion. Future work will explore long-term deployment in
uncontrolled home environments and adaptation to unseen users via
self-supervised pretraining.
6. Conclusion
In this work, we introduced mmWave-CSARNet, a dual-branch cas-
caded architecture tailored for continuous HAR using millimeter-wave
radar. By jointly modeling frame-level activity semantics and explicit
boundary dynamics through a cross-attention mechanism, our approach
effectively mitigates common challenges such as transition ambiguity,
prediction jitter, and temporal inconsistency. CSARNet achieves state-
of-the-art performance on multiple benchmarks demonstrating strong
results in F1@50, edit distance, and frame-wise accuracy, as well as
robust generalization across users and environments. A key strength
lies in its boundary-aware design, which decouples ‘‘what’’ is hap-
pening from ‘‘when’’ it changes, enabling more stable and temporally
coherent predictions. However, the method has limitations: it focuses
on short-duration micro-activities and lacks modeling of higher-level
behavioral structures; it requires precise boundary annotations, lim-
iting scalability in real-world settings; and its dual-branch structure
introduces moderate computational overhead compared to lightweight
alternatives. Despite these constraints, our work provides valuable
contributions to the mmWave-HAR community. The architecture offers
a modular framework that can be adapted to other sensing modalities
or integrated into existing systems to enhance temporal stability. Our
comprehensive evaluation protocol also sets a rigorous benchmark for
future research. For future work, we plan to extend CSARNet to hierar-
chical activity recognition, explore self-supervised or weakly supervised
learning to reduce annotation dependence, develop online adaptation
mechanisms for dynamic environments, and investigate multi-radar
fusion for improved spatial resolution. Overall, mmWave-CSARNet ad-
vances radar-based HAR by explicitly aligning semantic understanding
with temporal boundaries, offering both practical improvements and a
foundation for next-generation time-series activity models.
