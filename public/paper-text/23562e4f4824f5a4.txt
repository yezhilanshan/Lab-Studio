Abstract
A long-standing challenge for pushing sensor-based human activ-
ity recognition (HAR) to industrial usage is the distribution shift
between training data and testing data: significant variations in
data distribution lead to a notable decline in performance. Recently,
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated exceptional
generalization capability, which provides a new opportunity to
mitigate the distribution shift problem of HAR. However, since
LLMs are inherently designed and trained on textual data, their
potential to enhance generalization in HAR applications remains an
open question. In this paper, we introduce LLM4HAR, a novel LLM-
based model to improve cross-domain HAR. LLM4HAR consists of
three main modules: (i) the Sensor Data Adaptation module, which
aligns IMU signals with LLMs via sensor embedding, (ii) the Sensor
Knowledge Learning module, which injects sensor knowledge into
LLMs for activity recognition, and (iii) the Efficiency Enhancement
module, which employs a partial training strategy and reduces the
model size by more than 10 times. Extensive evaluations show that
LLM4HAR outperforms the existing methods by 13.82% in average
F1 score, demonstrating the feasibility and effectiveness of trans-
ferring knowledge from pretrained LLMs to enhance HAR. Further,
LLM4HAR has been adopted by JD Logistics to support downstream
applications such as Courier Welfare Improvement and Map Data
Generation.

1 Introduction
With the popularity of sensor-embedded mobile devices like smart-
phones and smartwatches, Human Activity Recognition (HAR) us-
ing Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) sensors has gained significant
attention. HAR means understanding fine-grained human activities
such as walking upstairs, as shown in Fig. 1. It has raised growing
interest in various data mining applications, such as healthcare [22]
and industrial production [58]. Among these applications, last-mile
delivery is a critical area where accurate HAR on delivery couriers
supports many applications, including fairness-aware workload
assignment, delivery efficiency improvement, and low-cost map
data mining [7, 18, 19, 42, 55]. However, achieving robust HAR
deployment in last-mile delivery is non-trivial due to the diverse
characteristics of couriers across multiple domains, including ac-
tivity habits, device types, and sensor placements. These domain
differences often lead to a significant performance drop in activity
recognition models, as the distribution of training data collected
from one group of couriers may deviate from the testing data of an-
other [2, 32]. Therefore, improving cross-domain HAR is critical
for real-world applications.
To improve cross-domain HAR, various approaches have been
designed. Techniques such as domain adaptation and few-shot
learning have been employed to mitigate the effects of domain
variance [21, 26, 57, 57]. The principle is learning domain-invariant
features by aligning the distribution among different domains. De-
spite these efforts, they often require domain-specific fine-tuning
or training data from the target domain, which is of high costs and
not always feasible in practice [32, 53]. For example, collecting full
target domain data is infeasible in last-mile delivery, as courier be-
haviors and device characters are diverse across tens of thousands
of couriers. Another technique, domain generalization aims to train
models only with data from source domains [27, 53]. However,
many of these models are easy to overfit on source domains due to
no observation of target data distributions, leading to suboptimal

performance when faced with unseen domains [39]. Additionally,

Recently, the emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs), such
as GPT2 [40] and LLama [51], presents an unprecedented opportu-
nity to overcome these limitations. With significantly more training
data and advanced model designs, LLMs have demonstrated impres-
sive generalization capabilities across a range of language tasks such
as generation [40] and reasoning [66]. Further, studies show that
the ability of LLMs pretrained with language data is not constrained
to language tasks, it also potentially enables broader applications
such as time series analysis [24, 28, 71]. However, few studies have
explored LLMs for cross-domain IoT sensor data [23]. Therefore,
we aim to answer the research question: whether and how LLMs can
benefit the generalization of HAR for industrial applications.
Despite its promise, it is not trivial to adapt LLMs pretrained
with language data for sensor-based HAR due to three main chal-
lenges. (i) Domain gap between sensor data and language data. Sensor
data collected from mobile devices is significantly different from
language data in format, information density, underlying seman-
tics, etc. Directly sending sensor data to LLMs might not achieve
satisfactory performance. (ii) Sensor knowledge gap of LLMs. Un-
derstanding sensor knowledge (such as multi-channel correlation
and sensor reading variation) is crucial for HAR. However, trained
with language data, such knowledge is often missing for LLMs. (iii)
On-device HAR requirement. In practice, HAR models are supposed
to run on mobile devices due to privacy concerns, data transmission
costs, and high latency for sending sensor data to the server [72].
However, commercial mobile devices have limited memory and
computational resources, making it challenging for them to run
LLMs directly on the edge.
To address these challenges, we design LLM4HAR, a novel system
that improves cross-domain HAR by adapting pretrained LLMs.
LLM4HAR comprises three main modules: (1) Sensor Adaptation
Module: This module effectively transforms raw IMU sensor data
for LLMs. It employs a segmentation strategy that treats IMU data
patches as analogous to textual tokens. Additionally, we design a
sensor embedding to bridge the gap between the sensor data and
the language. (2) Sensor Knowledge Learning Module: This
module enhances the LLMs’ understanding of sensor data by align-
ing sensor representation with pretrained knowledge from LLMs.
(3) Efficiency Enhancement Module: To enable real-time HAR
on mobile devices, we further optimize our system via a parameter-
cutting strategy and a partial training approach, which reduces
computation and memory costs while maintaining high effective-
ness. Our main contributions are summarized as follows.

• First, this study bridges the gap between IMU sensors and LLMs
and empirically demonstrates that the knowledge learned from
textual data benefits the generalization of HAR. We believe our

sensor data in the data mining community.
• Technically, we design a novel Sensor Adaptation Module to
transform IMU sensor data for LLMs. Moreover, we design a
Sensor Knowledge Learning Module by training partial LLMs

delivery couriers. After deployment, LLM4HAR has detected 72.6
million activities, supporting Courier Welfare Improvement and
low-cost Map Data Generation with an accuracy of 90.9% and
90.8%, respectively.
2 Background and Motivation
In this section, we first introduce the background of HAR in last-
mile delivery. Then, we discuss the connections between LLMs and
IMU data. Finally, we provide a formal problem definition.
2.1 HAR in Last-mile Delivery
The rapid growth of e-commerce has significantly increased the
demand for last-mile delivery services [20, 43]. As the final step in
the delivery process, where packages are transported from delivery
stations to customers, last-mile delivery is often the most complex
and costly segment [17]. In this context, the role of delivery couriers
becomes crucial as they navigate urban environments to provide
timely and accurate delivery services to citizens.
2.1.1 Sensor Choice for HAR. Many types of sensors can recog-
nize human activities, such as computer vision [50], acoustics [3],

2.1.2 Challenges of HAR. In the industrial deployment of sensor-
based HAR, the training data and testing data can come from differ-

cross-domain challenge is mainly brought by the distribution shift

models can drop up to 56.3% as shown in Fig. 2b. Therefore, we aim
to design a generalizable HAR model to recognize cross-domain
courier activities for industrial deployment.
2.2 Connection Between LLMs and IMU
Even though natural language and IMU are significantly different
in data modality, they are both sequence data and share many sim-
ilarities. LLMs are trained by predicting the next possible token,
which is similar to the next point or segment in IMU sequence data.
As shown in Fig. 3, we can utilize a similar tokenization method to
obtain tokens for LLMs as input. Recently, studies have explored
the generalization capability of LLMs, and shown advanced perfor-
mance in time series prediction [4, 13, 24, 71], which means that the
knowledge learned from language data enables LLMs to understand
sequential patterns in many domains beyond language. Therefore,
we can probably adapt LLMs to improve IMU-based HAR.
2.3 Problem Formulation
Given a pre-trained LLM G, a source dataset 𝐷𝑠𝑜𝑢𝑟𝑐𝑒 containing a
collection of IMU samples (𝑥𝑖) with corresponding activity labels
(𝑦𝑖), we aim to design and train a model M(.,𝜃, Φ): X → Y, which
can achieve good performance on unseen target dataset 𝐷𝑡𝑎𝑟𝑔𝑒𝑡 for
human activity recognition. 𝜃 represents the parameters we adapt
from the LLM G, Φ represents the parameters we customize.
3 System Design
To improve the generalization capability of HAR for real-world
applications, we design LLM4HAR, a novel framework to harness the
generalization capability of pretrained LLMs. As shown in Fig. 4,
LLM4HAR consists of three main components: (i) Sensor Data Adap-
tation to align sensor data with the LLM components, (ii) Sensor
Knowledge Learning to inject sensor knowledge to the LLM, and
(iii) Efficiency Enhancement to enable real-time inference on
low-resource mobile devices. Once LLM4HAR is trained, we evaluate
it with IMU data collected from different target domains. Finally,
we deploy the trained LLM4HAR for courier activity recognition in
JD Logistics (see Sec.5).
3.1 Sensor Data Adaptation
LLMs are designed to process natural language data in textual for-
mats, which is different from IMU sensor data significantly. There-
fore, we first convert the raw IMU sensor data into a representation
suitable for LLMs.
3.1.1 Adaptation Methods. Note that there are different ways to
represent IMU sensor data for LLMs as input.
• Sensor as strings. A straightforward approach to represent IMU
sensor data is transforming IMU readings into strings. While it
is easy to implement, the input length can be huge since every
digit of the floating-point number needs to be converted into a
string type.
• Sensor as statistics. To reduce the input length, we can represent
the IMU data by extracting statistical features such as the mean
acceleration. For example, we can calculate the mean value of
each channel of an IMU sample. While it reduces the length, it
loses detailed sensor information and might lead to a performance
decrease.
• Sensor as language. We can also describe the raw sensor data
with language, e.g., we can use "up 𝛼" and "down 𝛼" to describe
the trend of IMU sensor data and pattern [69]. Similar to sen-
sor statistics, this method might lose sensor information, which
might impact the model performance.
These methods rely on the embedding of LLMs to encode sensor
data. Due to the mentioned limitations, we design an IMU Embed-
ding component to encode sensor data instead of relying on the
original embedding component in LLMs.
3.1.2 Sensor Normalization. Given raw sensor data, we first apply
rotation-based sensor data augmentation [16, 60] to increase the
diversity of IMU sensor data. Then, we design a normalization
module to reduce the distribution gap among IMU data. Specifically,
in cross-domain HAR, one of the challenges is the distribution
shift brought by factors such as sensor placements and user habits
between the source and target domains. To reduce the distribution
shift and maintain the channel-specific sensor properties, we apply
instance normalization and normalize each channel separately for
each IMU sample.

makes Conv1D more efficient when dealing with high-dimensional
multi-channel IMU data.

3.2 Sensor Knowledge Learning
Once we get the adapted IMU embedding, the next step is to inject
sensor knowledge into LLMs. LLMs are pre-trained on a large cor-
pus of text data and can be fine-tuned for specific tasks, such as text
completion, translation, summarization, and question-answering [28,
40]. Originally, the input to LLMs is a sequence of tokens, first con-
verted into vectors using an embedding matrix. In our setting, the
input is an IMU sensor sample and cannot be enumerated to form
a vocabulary 𝐸. Therefore, to obtain embedding with the same size
required by pretrained LLMs, our Conv1D transforms the sensor
data into Xc ∈ R𝑁 ×768×1024.

for accurately distinguishing between different activities. To effec-
tively capture the temporal dependencies inherent in IMU sensor
data, we introduce a learnable positional embedding module. Unlike
fixed and predetermined positional encodings, learnable positional
embeddings allow the model to adaptively infer the optimal rep-
resentation of position information directly from the data. The
integration of positional embeddings is formalized as follows:

3.3 Efficiency Enhancement
While our LLM4HAR is promising for mitigating the domain gap
and improving the generalization performance, deploying LLM4HAR
on mobile devices is still challenging because of the high costs in
memory consumption and computation. As shown in Table 1, most
popular LLMs occupy more than 400 MB of memory, making it
impractical to directly deploy on commercial smartphones. Further,
according to the business team of JD Logistics, the maximum model
size they can accept is 100 MB to achieve large-scale deployment
on diverse types of mobile devices carried by couriers.
3.3.1 Model Size Reduction. Therefore, to enable practical deploy-
ment of LLM4HAR, we aim to improve the efficiency by reducing the
model size. First, we choose GPT-2 [40] as our backbone LLM due to
its small parameter size, widespread usage, and good performance.
Then, we remove the Embedding Component from the LLM be-
cause we have designed a new sensor embedding. Additionally, we
remove the Answer Generation Component, since we do not need
to generate results in textual formats. Finally, we remove partial
layers of the LLM to further reduce the parameter size. Existing
studies have shown that not all layers are useful for certain tasks
and dropping some layers will not impact the model performance
significantly [12, 45].
Compared to the original GPT-2 model, LLM4HAR has significantly
fewer parameters and requires less memory with a much faster
inference speed (see Table 3). These results highlight the potential

3.3.2 Selective Training. To preserve the generalization capabili-
ties of LLMs while adapting them to HAR, we design a selective
fine-tuning paradigm to fine-tune partial LLM parameters. Similar
fine-tuning strategies have also been studied by existing studies [71].
Specifically, we focus on fine-tuning the Positional Encoding and
Layer Normalization to better capture the temporal relationships
within the sensor data while preserving the generalization capa-
bilities of LLMs as shown in Fig. 5. We freeze the parameter in
self-attention. Freezing these parameters ensures that the com-
prehensive knowledge learned from textual data is not disrupted,
which benefits the generalization of HAR. We also freeze the pa-
rameters in Feedforward to maintain the generalization capability
of LLMs and reduce the training costs.

ancy between the predicted and actual activity labels:

4.3 Impact of Sensor Knowledge Learning
An important research question is whether the knowledge of LLMs
learned from the pretraining process on textual data benefits cross-
domain HAR. To answer this question, we compare LLM4HAR with
two variations: (i) randomly initialize the parameters in LLM blocks,
and (ii) remove the LLM blocks from our system. Other modules
remain the same.
• (i) Randomly Initialize the Parameters in LLM Blocks. From the
results shown in Fig. 6, we can observe that on all datasets, the
transferring performance decreases significantly after we remove
the pretraining process. On average, the accuracy decreases by
6.0% and the F1 decreases by 9.4%, which demonstrates that the
pretraining process on text data benefits sensor-based HAR.
• (ii) Remove the LLM Blocks from our System. The results are shown
in Fig. 7. From these figures, we can observe that removing the
LLM decoder blocks decreases the performance significantly,
especially on the Shoaib and Motion datasets. On average, the
accuracy decreases by 5.07% and the F1 decreases by 7.41%, which
demonstrates that the LLM decoder blocks pretrained on text
data are effective for our cross-domain HAR tasks.

4.5 Effect of LLM Size
4.5.1 Effect on Effectiveness. We evaluate the performance of HAR
with various parameter sizes of pretrained LLMs. Note that we
only replace the LLM block and keep other modules together with
the training process the same. From Fig. 9, we have an interest-
ing observation: with the increase in the parameter size of LLMs,
the transfer performance does not increase. This phenomenon is
counter-intuitive compared to the scaling law observed in natural
language tasks [51]. One of the possible reasons is that the training
data for HAR is very limited to fine-tune LLMs when the parameter
size goes up. The LLM with a smaller parameter size can learn the
IMU pattern well, and at the same time, LLMs utilize its transfer
capability to benefit cross-domain HAR.
4.5.2 Effect on Efficiency. We compare the efficiency of LLM4HAR
with three different sizes of LLM backbone models as shown in
Table 3. The inference time is calculated on a P40 GPU and a normal
CPU. Compared to the three sizes of the GPT-2 model, LLM4HAR
has significantly fewer parameters and requires less memory with
a much faster inference speed. These results highlight the potential
feasibility of deploying LLM4HAR for real-time activity sensing.

5.2 Real-world Applications
5.2.1 Application 1: Courier Welfare Improvement. Last-mile deliv-

for couriers. For example, many residential areas block delivery
vehicles, and couriers need to walk long distances to deliver parcels.
Also, some buildings are not equipped with elevators, and couriers
have to climb stairs every day. To enhance courier well-being, pre-
vent overwork, and ensure fair wages, accurately measuring daily
workload is essential. While measuring workload by the number
of finished orders seems straightforward, it does not work well in
complex urban environments. This is common in areas where deliv-

or require long walking distances, leading to significant workload
variations despite similar order numbers.
To enhance the welfare of couriers, we cooperate with JD Lo-
gistics to provide extra bonuses to couriers with high workloads.
Specifically, the business team identifies those couriers according
to the duration of walking and climbing stairs, the number of deliv-
ered orders, etc. We manually label the workload for 22 couriers
according to the field survey in the corresponding working areas.
The accuracy is 90.9% and the detailed results are shown in Table 4.
Therefore, the activities detected by our framework benefit the
practical application to improve courier welfare.

6 Discussion
In this section, we discuss the limitations of this study and potential
future directions. Also, we summarize three important lessons we
learned. Finally, we discuss how we ensure data ethics and privacy.
6.1 Limitations and Future Work
First, given the resource constraint, we currently only evaluate
three LLMs all from GPT-2 family. We will conduct experiments
4518
LLM4HAR: Generalizable On-device Human Activity Recognition with Pretrained LLMs KDD ’25, August 3–7, 2025, Toronto, ON, Canada
on other open-source LLMs such as LLama [51], to evaluate the
generalization of our observations. Secondly, we plan to improve
the efficiency by using quantization techniques[33] and increase
the deployment scale.
Also, incorporating multi-modal information might further en-
hance HAR. Intuitively, the context knowledge about the person
and devices is relevant to the HAR performance. For example, old
people and young people have different upstairs patterns. Therefore,
combining such context knowledge with original IMU sensor data
using LLMs could potentially improve the performance of HAR.
Further, with the increasing volume of IMU sensor data available,
it will be interesting to investigate whether the scaling law can be
observed for sensor-based human activity recognition. We leave
this as another future direction.
6.2 Lessons Learned
• (1) Transfer knowledge from pretrained LLMs benefits the
generalization of sensor-based HAR. Through extensive eval-
uations, we demonstrate that even though language and IMU data
are significantly different, the knowledge learned from language
data enhances cross-domain HAR (see Table 2). This observation
has broader applications for other IoT data, such as mmWave [56].
• (2) Aligning sensor data with LLMs. Our experiments have
shown that it is possible to align sensor data with text data and
utilize the generalization capability of LLMs trained on text data
to benefit sensor-based HAR. This discovery has significant im-
plications for future research and applications in mobile sensing.
• (3) On-device LLMs. Although most LLMs are computationally
intensive for edge deployment, our model compression approach
demonstrates that significant size reduction can be achieved with-
out compromising performance. Notably, our optimized models
enable efficient LLM execution on smartphones while maintain-
ing minimal energy consumption. On-device LLMs particularly
benefit privacy-critical domains such as healthcare.
6.3 Data Ethics
We take the following steps to ensure data ethics and privacy, and
mitigate potential risks such as revealing couriers’ working mode.
(i) We have obtained consent from participating couriers, who were
fully informed about the study’s purpose, methods, and potential
impacts. (ii) All user identifiers are removed or replaced with a
random number. (iii) We only collect data during the working hours.
The couriers can turn off the activity recognition module at any
time on their working devices. (iv) The data is stored securely, with
access restricted to authorized researchers only.
7 Related Work
In this section, we summarize related studies into two categories,
i.e., Human Activity Recognition and LLM for IoT Data Mining.
7.1 Human Activity Recognition
Sensor-based human activity recognition means predicting the ac-
tivities of a person by utilizing the sensor data collected from wear-
able devices such as smartphones and smartwatches [6, 68]. Due to
its great importance, there is increasing attention on sensor-based
HAR [15, 35]. Inoue et al. [22] integrates HAR with other records
to improve efficiency in smart nursing. Through a body-worn ac-
celerometer, Xia et al. [58] design an unsupervised HAR model
to improve production efficiency by detecting workers’ activities.
Due to the challenge of distribution shift between domains, such as
different user groups or different sensor placements, some studies
design transfer learning techniques [65] and focus on cross-domain
HAR [2, 54], i.e., models trained on one domain are expected to
generalize well to other domains. Qin et al. [39] design a repre-
sentation learning method for generalized HAR in low-resource
settings. Different from these studies, we explore the knowledge
in pretrained LLMs to improve the generalization performance of
HAR. Also, most studies focus on cross-domain HAR with small
distribution shifts, e.g., only the user is different or only the device
is different. On the contrary, we assume there are multiple types
of distribution shifts between source and target domains, which is
more practical and challenging.
7.2 LLM for IoT Data Mining
Recently, there has been a growing interest in integrating LLMs
with IoT applications [30]. Xu et al. [59] analyze how to harness
LLM to understand the physical world and provide a few concrete
examples. Kim et al. [28] utilize LLMs for health prediction based
on wearable sensor data. Li et al. [29] harness frozen LLM for ECG
signal classification. Specifically, this study incorporates a trainable
ECG encoder and a frozen LLM, which brings good performance
for zero-shot ECG classifications. Xu et al. [62] discuss the combi-
nation of LLM and 6G network. Also, there are emerging studies
explores LLMs for prediction tasks [4, 13, 24, 25, 48, 69, 71]. Jin et
al. [24] reprogram the pre-trained LLM for time series forecasting.
By reprogramming time series as text prototype representations,
the model achieves good performance in time series forecasting.
Chang et al. [4] design a two-stage fine-tuning strategy to fine-tune
LLMs for time series forecasting. Li et al. [30] fine-tuned LLMs
with sensor-language alignment for multiple sensor tasks on GPU
servers. Different from these studies, we aim to address the gener-
alization challenge in on-device HAR by harnessing the knowledge
in pretrained LLMs.
8 Conclusion
In this study, we aim to improve the generalization performance
of IMU-based human activity recognition by adapting pretrained
LLMs. Extensive evaluation results show that even though IMU data
is significantly different from text data, the knowledge of LLMs
learned from large-scale text still improves the performance of
activity recognition in the challenging cross-domain settings. This
observation potentially benefits related studies in IoT data mining
to address domain shifts from a new perspective.
