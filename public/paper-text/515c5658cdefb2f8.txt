MASTER: A Multi-modal Foundation Model for Human Activity
Recognition
GUANZHOU ZHU, DONG ZHAO, CHUNLIANG LI, MINGYUE ZHAO, ZHENGYUAN ZHANG,
HEFENG QUAN, and HUADONG MA, State Key Laboratory of Network and Switching Technology,
Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications, China
Multi-modal sensing has become crucial in Human Activity Recognition (HAR) due to its ability to combine data from diverse
sensors. However, challenges arise in recognizing various activities in different scenes using multi-modal data from different
positions and devices, due to dynamic combinations of modal inputs, data heterogeneity, and scarcity of labeled data. To tackle
these challenges, we propose MASTER, a multi-modal foundation model specifically designed for HAR. MASTER introduces a
masked-data modeling-based self-supervised pre-training method, enabling the model to learn from unlabeled data and adapt
to dynamic combinations of modal inputs. Moreover, it incorporates a few-shot alignment mechanism to facilitate adaptation
to different activities, scenes, positions, and devices. Through the pre-training and fine-tuning on 7 multi-modal HAR datasets,
MASTER currently supports, but is not limited to, 8 modalities (ACC, Gyro, mmWave, WiFi, Skeleton, Lidar, Infrared, and RGB)
and 45 human activities. The results demonstrate that MASTER achieves the highest accuracy with minimal labeled data across
various situations, surpassing alternative solutions.

1 INTRODUCTION
Human Activity Recognition (HAR) plays a significant role in our daily lives, enabling the monitoring of various
aspects such as living habits [37], stress levels [25], rehabilitation progress [33], abnormal behaviors [53], and
the early detection of Alzheimer’s disease [30]. In real-world scenarios, human activities tend to be complex
and dynamic. As a result, multi-modal sensing has become an important paradigm for HAR by fusing data from
diverse sources such as accelerometers (ACC), gyroscopes (Gyro), cameras, mmWave and WiFi radars, which has
attracted considerable research attention [17, 28, 30, 31, 46, 50].
However, there are three limitations of the existing HAR methods. First, they are primarily designed for fixed
modal inputs [20, 46, 47, 61] and cannot adapt to dynamic combinations of modal inputs. Second, most of them
[28, 50] heavily rely on a large amount of labeled data; while some studies [17, 31] have explored the use of
few-shot learning techniques in HAR, they still require a relatively high proportion of labeled data (e.g., 20% [31])

to achieve comparable accuracy to supervised methods that utilize 100% labeled data. Third, they only support a
limited range of activities, making it challenging to meet the requirements of diverse real-world scenarios. Due
to these limitations, current HAR methods fail to effectively address the following three real-world issues:
• Sensor aspect: Different scenes often involve varying sensor combinations, including differences in the
numbers and types of sensors used. When users transition between different rooms, they often encounter changes
in the combination of available sensing modalities. For instance, in indoor HAR, a combination of WiFi, a
smartphone held in hand, and a smartwatch may be employed. On the other hand, for outdoor HAR, a typical
configuration might consist of only a smartphone in the pocket and a smartwatch.
• Data aspect: First, even for a single modality, the diversity of adopted devices, their usage scenes, and
placement positions leads to heterogeneous sensing data, which severely impairs model performance, especially
for rare or unseen scenes, positions, and devices. This challenge becomes even more severe in multi-modal
settings. Second, labeling multi-modal data is challenging, especially for less intuitive modalities like mmWave
and WiFi, raising concerns about the availability of labeled training data.
• Task aspect: In HAR scenarios, there are numerous types of activities that need to be recognized. For
example, six commonly used public multi-modal datasets include 39 activities in total (detailed in Sect. 6.1). In
addition to classification tasks, mobility metrics are also needed to be estimated. For example, in the gait metrics
dataset [1], stride velocity and double support time were calculated for each participant to access walking quality.
To comprehensively address these challenges, it is significant to design a multi-modal foundation model for
HAR that can flexibly adapt to various combinations of sensor modalities (sensor aspect), across diverse scenes,
positions, and devices with minimal labeled data (data aspect), while maintaining high performance across diverse
HAR tasks (task aspect). In summary, this requires the model to possess two unique abilities:
• Dynamic combinations of modal inputs: The model can adapt to various combinations of data from
diverse sensors, such as ACC, Gyro, and mmWave radar. In other words, the model needs to support pre-training
and fine-tuning on all possible combinations of multi-modal HAR datasets.
• Few-shot learning: The model can achieve accurate HAR with minimal labeled data for training (e.g., 1% of
labeled data, which can be collected within only a few minutes), especially for new scenes, positions, and devices,
while supporting various tasks, such as recognizing different activity types and even performing mobility metric
regression.
However, two unique challenges must be addressed first:
(i) How to make the model adapt to the dynamic combinations of modal inputs? Most existing Multi-modal Large
Language Models (MLLMs) [16, 23, 32] only handle a small fixed number of input modalities (e.g., text, image, and
audio), which are highly interpretable. These models typically align the embeddings of non-textual modalities
with word embeddings in large language models or employ expert models to translate non-textual modalities
into natural language that large language models can comprehend. However, this restricts the model’s ability to
flexibly adapt to more modalities. This limitation arises from two main factors: i) the non-intuitive nature of most
sensing data, which makes data annotation challenging, let alone adding corresponding textual descriptions,
and ii) the absence of a single modality that can comprehensively describe all modalities like text does. Some
methods [7, 43] train and switch between multiple modality experts to reduce reliance on text. However, this
significantly increases the training overhead, requiring the training of multiple experts for various combinations
of modalities (e.g., 15 experts for all combinations of four modalities), and diminishes knowledge sharing across
different modalities.
(ii) How to make the model adapt to various activities, scenes, positions, and devices with only a small amount of
labeled data? Even for the same activity type with a single modality, notable heterogeneity exists in sensing data
across different scenes, positions, and devices [45]. This means that samples belonging to the same class cannot
be aligned into the same representation space. This heterogeneity exists independently within each individual
modality and further magnifies the overall differences in multi-modal data. Moreover, sensing modalities (e.g.,

mmWave and WiFi) are sensitive to changes in scenes and positions [13, 26], which further exacerbates the
challenge of adapting to new scenes and positions. Finally, the scarcity of labeled data makes it more difficult for
the model to adapt to various activities, scenes, positions, and devices.
To address these challenges, we design the Multi-modal Activity Sensing TransformER (MASTER), a multi-modal
foundation model for HAR, which has two key innovations:
First, to handle dynamic combinations of modal inputs, we propose a masked-data modeling-based self-supervised
pre-training method. Specifically, we concatenate all modal inputs into a single sentence and exploit the consistent
and complementary information implicitly present in other modalities by masking some modal inputs for recovery
based on a transformer architecture. It should be noted that standard loss functions designed for masked-text
modeling [14] or masked-image modeling [15, 19] are unsuitable for the heterogeneous sensing data. Sensing data
lacks the ability to generate unique representations akin to word embeddings, rendering the loss for masked-text
modeling inapplicable. Moreover, the dimensional variations in sensing data, such as 1D (ACC), 2D (RGB), and
3D (skeleton), make the loss for masked-image modeling unsuitable. Consequently, we design a recovery loss
specifically tailored for sensing data to effectively accomplish the pre-training tasks of masked-data modeling.
Second, we design a few-shot alignment mechanism to eliminate the data heterogeneity with only a small
amount of labeled data. Specifically, we treat the labeled data as anchor points of different distributions and then
minimize the distance between intra-class anchor points to align all intra-class samples to the same representation
space with the same classification boundary. Meanwhile, we maximize the distance between inter-class anchor
points to make the classification boundaries between classes more discriminative. In this way, we align samples
of the same activity class from the same modality and different modalities into the same representation space,
and thus the model can adapt to various activities, scenes, positions, and devices.
Existing public multi-modal HAR datasets typically collect multi-modal data in a single scene. Although it is
possible to simulate different modality combinations by simply dropping certain modalities, this approach fails to
reflect the dynamic nature of modality combinations, i.e., both the combinations and data distributions change
with scene variations. Therefore, a new multi-modal (mmWave, Skeleton, and 2 × IMU) HAR dataset called DCM
is specifically designed to capture Dynamic Combinations of Modalities in real-world settings, includes data from
two scenes, four types of modal input combinations (can be extended to 12 different combinations, detailed in
Sect. 5), 30 subjects, and 10 activities.
Moreover, the multi-modal foundation model for HAR should also have the ability for mobility-metric regression
[48], such as estimating stride velocity and support time. However, there is currently a lack of publicly available
multi-modal datasets suitable for evaluating the regression performance. Therefore, a new multi-modal stride
velocity dataset called MSV is specifically designed (detailed in Sect. 5). The dataset includes data from 11 subjects,
covering a stride velocity range from 0.65 m/s to 1.8 m/s, and comprises three modalities: mmWave, skeleton, and
2 × IMU.
We evaluate the classification performance of MASTER on the self-collected multi-modal HAR dataset DCM
and six public multi-modal datasets, including 8 sensor modalities (ACC, Gyro, mmWave, WiFi, Skeleton, Lidar,
Infrared, and RGB) and 45 human activities. The results show that: (i) With some modalities missing, MASTER
trained with only 2% of labeled data delivers an average accuracy improvement of at least 21.6% compared to other
baselines [11, 28, 29, 31, 38] with 100% labeled data. (ii) The ability of MASTER to handle dynamic modal inputs
allows collaborative training on seven datasets, improving average accuracy from 89.4% to 92.2% compared to
training on a single dataset. (iii) When applies to new scenes, positions or devices, MASTER only requires minimal
labeled data (e.g., 1%) to achieve comparable accuracy to the state-of-the-art (SOTA) supervised method AttnSense
[28] with 100% labeled data. (iv) MASTER utilizes only 1%-3% of labeled data to achieve comparable accuracy to
the SOTA self-supervised method COSMO [31] (which necessitates 10%-20% of labeled data), highlighting its
superior few-shot learning ability across various activities, scenes, positions, and devices. We further evaluate
the regression performance of MASTER on the self-collected multi-modal stride velocity dataset MSV. The results

In particular, the key contributions of this work include:
• To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work on a multi-modal foundation model for HAR called
MASTER. It features a novel masked-data modeling-based self-supervised pre-training method to adapt to
dynamic combinations of modal inputs, and a few-shot alignment mechanism to adapt to various activities,
scenes, positions, and devices.
• A new multi-modal HAR dataset with dynamic modality combinations in different scenes is collected by
ourselves to evaluate the abilities of MASTER on handling dynamic combinations of input modalities in
real-world scenarios. Additionally, a multi-modal stride velocity dataset is collected by ourselves to evaluate
the performance of MASTER on regression-based downstream tasks.
• Through the pre-training and fine-tuning on 7 multi-modal HAR datasets, our MASTER supports, but is
not limited to, 8 modalities and 45 activities. Experimental results show that MASTER achieves the highest
classification accuracy with minimal labeled data in various situations. Meanwhile, MASTER achieves the
best overall regression performance in stride velocity estimation.
• To benefit the IMWUT community, we have released our code and processed data. Specifically, the DCM
and MSV datasets are now publicly available at this link, and the code is available at this link.
2 BACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION
2.1 Why Multi-modal Foundation Models for HAR
In recent years, significant advancements have been made in foundation models for understanding human
intentions using text, image, and audio modalities. Notably, ChatGPT [8] has demonstrated exceptional capabilities
in language comprehension and generation, showcasing the power of transformer-based models in NLP. The
introduction of Vision Transformer (ViT) [15] has also bridged the gap between NLP and computer vision (CV),
leading to MLLMs like PaLM-E [16], a combination of language model PaLM [10] with visual model ViT. However,
it is worth noting that no existing multi-modal foundation model has the capability to handle inputs from all
sensing modalities. Existing models primarily focus on modalities that humans can intuitively understand, such
as images and audio. However, for less intuitive sensing data like ACC, Gyro, mmWave, and WiFi, current
multi-modal models struggle to comprehend the underlying meanings without accompanying text descriptions.
This limitation is evident in the fact that four representative MLLM learning paradigms require the assistance of
text [51].
On the other hand, it is significant to develop a multi-modal foundation model specifically designed to
understand the human activity information embedded in sensing data. First, the aforementioned multi-modal
sensing data are crucial for recognizing human intentions beyond the text, image, and audio, e.g., understanding
people’s stress levels [25, 27] by analyzing the frequency of certain actions, identifying signs of Parkinson’s
disease [54, 55], etc. Second, sensing modalities like mmWave and WiFi offer better privacy protection compared
to images and audio. Third, the complexity of human activities necessitates a more effective model. Lastly,
beyond the above three points, it is of great significance to equip the model with the ability to handle dynamic
combinations of sensing modalities and adapt effectively with minimal labeled data.
Based on the above considerations, we define a multi-modal foundation model for HAR as a model capable of
learning generalizable representations of human activities from diverse sensing modality combinations, across
varying scenes, positions, devices, and users. Such a model should exhibit strong cross-modal transferability and
few-shot adaptability to diverse downstream tasks, aligning with the key properties of foundation models in the
fields of NLP and CV. We believe this definition is essential for guiding the development of generalizable, scalable
HAR systems under real-world conditions.

2.2 Challenges in Multi-modal Foundation Models for HAR
To investigate the challenges encountered by multi-modal foundation models for HAR, i.e., dynamic combinations
of modal inputs and few-shot learning, we evaluate two SOTA multi-modal HAR methods, AttnSense [28] and
COSMO [31]. The former adopts a supervised learning paradigm with a large amount of labeled data, while the
latter leverages a pre-training + fine-tuning paradigm with limited labeled data. First, we evaluate these methods
on the multi-modal dataset MMFi [49], which comprises six modalities: mmWave, Lidar, Infrared, WiFi, Skeleton,
and RGB. This evaluation aims to investigate the impact of missing modalities and assess the models’ ability to
handle dynamic combinations of modal inputs. Second, we assess the few-shot learning ability of both methods
across different scenes, positions, and devices using the USC [56] dataset and Shoaib [35] dataset. The USC
dataset comprises ACC and Gyro data collected by MotionNode [2] (a 6-DOF inertial measurement unit) from
the waist position in both indoor and outdoor scenes. The Shoaib dataset encompasses the ACC and Gyro data
from smartphones at five body positions (waist, front left hip, front right hip, upper arm, wrist) in indoor scenes.
Both datasets are down-sampled to a sampling rate of 50 Hz.
2.2.1 Dynamic Combinations of Modal Inputs. With the development of sensing technologies and smart devices,
there are a wide range of sensors that can be utilized for HAR. However, many previous studies on multi-modal
HAR have been limited to specific modalities or sensors [47]. While some works support most modalities, they
assume that those modalities are available at all times [28, 31, 50]. However, this assumption may not hold true in
real-world scenarios, as certain sensors may be out of range, e.g., WiFi is typically limited to monitoring indoor
scenes. The issue of missing modalities poses significant challenges to HAR models, as they are typically designed
with fixed modalities as input. As shown in Fig. 1, even when any of the six modalities is missing1, the average
accuracy of AttnSense and COSMO significantly decreases from 66.9% and 87.4% to 51.4% and 73.9%, respectively.
Moreover, the accuracy drops sharply as more modalities are missing. For instance, when only one modality is
present in the input, the average accuracy of AttnSense and COSMO drops to 11.8% and 19.5%, respectively.
2.2.2 Few-shot Learning. Labeling multi-modal data for HAR is extremely difficult in practice, which greatly
affects the model performance. Fig. 2 demonstrates the impact of the amount of labeled data on AttnSense and
COSMO in the Shoaib dataset. We can observe that: (i) The accuracy of both AttnSense and COSMO drops as the
amount of labeled data decreases. (ii) The self-supervised method COSMO exhibits higher accuracy compared to
AttnSense. However, there remains a notable accuracy gap between COSMO and AttnSense that utilizes 100%
labeled data for supervised learning.

handle the heterogeneity of sensing data across different scenes, positions, and devices.
3 SYSTEM OVERVIEW
In this section, we present MASTER, a multi-modal foundation model for HAR. Our key idea is to capture consistent
and complementary information simultaneously from unlabeled multi-modal data and align the heterogeneous
embeddings of the same activity class across different scenes, positions, and devices. As shown in Fig. 5, MASTER
consists of two stages as follows:
• Self-supervised Pre-training. First, an unlabeled multi-modal pre-training dataset is created by merging publicly
available multi-modal datasets. Second, unimodal encoders extract high-dimensional feature embeddings for
each modality, which are then projected to the same dimension using an inner projection layer. By concatenating
the output embeddings of each modality in a sample into a sequence and padding it with zero embeddings to the
maximum length 𝑚, the embedding sequence length becomes consistent across all samples, regardless of the

number of modalities present. Third, a masked-data modeling mechanism is designed to capture consistent and
complementary information from unlabeled multi-modal data. Specifically, it randomly masks an embedding in
the embedding sequence and utilizes the transformer blocks to recover the masked embedding. To facilitate the
learning of both consistent information within modalities and complementary information between modalities
for effective recovery, a recovery loss is devised. This loss encourages the generated feature embeddings to closely
resemble the original feature embeddings while maximizing their dissimilarity from other feature embeddings in
the batch, thereby preserving more discriminative features.
• Few-shot Alignment and Fine-tuning. To facilitate the adaptation of the foundation model to new scenes,
positions, or devices with limited labeled data, we propose a few-shot alignment mechanism. It involves two steps:
i) We initialize the unimodal encoders and transformer blocks using parameters trained in the first stage and
freeze these parameters. ii) An adaptation layer is introduced to align samples belonging to the same activity class
via an alignment loss. By maximizing the similarity between intra-class samples while minimizing the similarity
between inter-class samples, the samples from the same class share a common classification boundary. After
that, we adopt an iterative fine-tuning mechanism [31] to fine-tune the pre-trained encoders, transformer blocks,
alignment adaption layers, and a newly added classifier with the same labeled data. This iterative process helps
refine the model’s representations and adapt them to the specific task.
4 DESIGN OF MASTER
4.1 Self-supervised Pre-training
4.1.1 Basic Idea. Human activities consist of a series of continuous sub-actions, such as the back-and-forth
movements of legs and arms during walking. These continuous sub-actions can be captured by different modalities,

such as vision and sound. For example, even when we temporarily close our eyes, we can still mentally picture
the actions of a person based on recent sub-action segments, along with the sounds we hear. This ability arises
from our comprehensive understanding of different activities, as well as the consistent and complementary
relationships between different modalities. Inspired by this observation, our goal is to design a self-supervised
pre-training method that learns the correlations between different modalities by reconstructing masked sub-
actions. To accomplish this, we segment the input of each modality into patches, representing sub-actions of the
current action. We then employ the attention mechanism in the transformer block to learn the consistent and
complementary relationships between different modalities to facilitate mask recovery.
4.1.2 Detailed Design. An overview of the self-supervised pre-training stage is depicted in Fig. 6. Briefly, the input
of each modality is divided into patches, which are then fed into the corresponding unimodal encoder to get Patch
Embeddings. Then, Position Embeddings and Modality Embeddings are added to the patch embeddings to retain
temporal and modality information, respectively. To learn the whole modality representation, an extra learnable
classification (CLS) token is added at the beginning of the whole patch embedding sequence. The CLS token play
a significant role in the subsequent few-shot alignment and fine-tuning stages. The other High-level Embeddings
learned through the transformer blocks are responsible for recovering masked patches during pre-training.

4.1.3 Model Pre-training. To construct the unlabeled pre-training dataset, we merge data from various multi-
modal datasets. For each input sample of MASTER, all modalities of a single dataset are included at a time. Due to
the presence of modality embedding, there is no need to restrict the order of different datasets for each input
sample. Before feeding the raw data into the MASTER encoder group, we split the raw data based on the duration,
e,g., 0.4 seconds. We first apply padding operations on data within a modality, as the data may include varying
lengths. Then we determine the length of each multi-modal input and pad all inputs with zeros to match the
length of the longest input.
Subsequently, we set attention masks to prevent transformer blocks from focusing on the padded sections.
Since direct recovery of the original patch signal requires an additional decoder and may compromise modality
alignment performance, the patch embedding output by the feature encoder is regarded as the recovery ground
truth for pre-training.
4.2 Few-shot Alignment and Fine-tuning
The objective of few-shot alignment and fine-tuning is to enable the foundation model to be easily adapted to new
scenes, positions, and devices with limited labeled data only. In this way, the trained model not only minimizes
the fine-tuning overhead but also enhances its knowledge transfer capability.

at aligning feature embeddings of the same activity from different scenes, positions, and devices into the same
classification boundary.

6 EVALUATION
6.1 Experimental Setup
6.1.1 Datasets. In this study, we first evaluate the classification performance of MASTER on a new self-collected
HAR dataset with real-world dynamic modalities setting (detailed in Sect. 5) and six public HAR datasets, which
contain almost all common modalities related to HAR. We then evaluate the regression performance of MASTER
on a new self-collected multi-modal stride velocity dataset (detailed in Sect. 5). The summary of the seven
multi-modal HAR datasets is shown in Table 1. The detailed reasons for using these public datasets are as follows:
• UCI [6], Shoaib [35], HHAR [36], and USC [56]. All of these datasets contain the ACC and Gyro data, which
are the most commonly used modalities for HAR. However, they exhibit data heterogeneity due to collection
from various scenes, positions, and devices. UCI dataset contains two streams of ACC data and one stream of
Gyro data, which are used for evaluating the dynamic modal inputs with similar modalities. Shoaib dataset
contains ACC and Gyro data from 5 positions and is used to evaluate the model’s cross-position few-shot
learning ability. Besides, the collection devices and sensing positions for one stream of ACC and Gyro data in
the UCI dataset are the same as that in the Shoaib dataset. Therefore, they can be combined to evaluate the
model’s cross-scene few-shot learning ability. HHAR dataset is used to evaluate the cross-device few-shot
Proc. ACM Interact. Mob. Wearable Ubiquitous Technol., Vol. 9, No. 3, Article 157. Publication date: September 2025.
157:14 • Zhu et al.
learning ability as it contains ACC and Gyro data simultaneously collected from 4 different smartphones
(Nexus 4, Galaxy S3, Galaxy S3 mini, and Galaxy S plus) with the same position. USC is a commonly used
dataset evaluated by most baselines we compare.
• MMFi [49]. It contains various common wireless sensing modalities, while some of them are not covered by
existing MLLMs, e.g., mmWave, Lidar, and WiFi. MMFi dataset is utilized to evaluate the model’s ability in
handling dynamic combinations of modal inputs.
• MRI [5]. It contains both wearable sensing modalities and wireless sensing modalities. MRI dataset is utilized
to further comprehensively evaluate the performance of MASTER.
6.1.2 Baselines. There are many multi-modal HAR methods, but most of them are designed for specific modalities,
e.g., Limu-bert [47] and UniHAR [46] are designed for a combination of ACC and Gyro only. For fair comparisons,
we compare the performance of MASTER with the following SOTA baselines that can easily adapt to other
modalities by changing the feature encoders or training strategy.
• AttnSense [28], an attention-based supervised multi-modal fusion method.
• STMAE [29], a transformer-based masked autoencoder for multi-device wearable HAR. Specifically, we
remove the pre-training stage for MMFi, MRI, and DCM datasets, as the pre-training strategy (recover raw
data) and loss (MSE) are unsuitable for wireless sensing modalities and demonstrate poor performance.
• CMC [38], a contrastive learning-based method designed for multi-view image data input.
• COSMO [31], a contrastive learning-based method for fusing multiple heterogeneous modalities for HAR.
• BABEL [11], a state-of-the-art multimodal foundation HAR model that follows the ImageBind paradigm,
aligning modalities from different datasets in an iterative pairwise manner.
6.1.3 Implementation Details. To ensure a consistent input format and fair comparisons across different methods
and modalities, we adopt a unified encoder architecture for all baselines and our proposed method. Specifically,
1D CNN is used for ACC, Gyro, and WiFi; 3D CNN is used for mmWave, Infrared, Lidar, and Skeleton. The
number of transformer layers is set as 4. 𝑁𝑖𝑡𝑒𝑟 is set as 3. The scaling parameter 𝛼 in Eq. 2 is set according to
[42], while the scaling parameter 𝛽 in Eq. 5 is set to 10. The learning rate and batch size are set as 3e-4 and 128,
respectively. We choose Adam as the optimizer. Each experiment is repeated five times.
Note that all models are trained using the same labeled set, and the subjects in the test set are different from
those in the training set. Unlike other baselines that can only train with one dataset, MASTER can pre-train and
fine-tune on all the multi-modal datasets together. However, to ensure a fair evaluation, MASTER is only pre-trained
on one dataset and does not utilize labeled data from other datasets for alignment, except for the cross-scene
few-shot learning case where labeled data from other datasets is used for alignment (detailed in Sect. 6.4.1). The
performance of MASTER pre-trained on all the multi-modal datasets is verified separately in Sect. 6.5.
6.1.4 Evaluation Metrics. To evaluate the classification performance, we follow prior works [48, 52] and adopt
three widely used metrics: Accuracy, F1-score, and Kappa. These metrics collectively reflect the overall correctness,
balance between precision and recall, and inter-rater agreement of the classification results.
For regression tasks, following prior studies [1, 48], we use five standard metrics: Mean Squared Error (MSE),
Standard Deviation of Squared Error (SDSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Standard Deviation of Absolute Error
(SDAE), and Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient (Corr). These metrics jointly capture the average and variance of
estimation errors, as well as the linear correlation between estimated and ground-truth values.

7 DISCUSSION
Generality. Our MASTER aims to address two real-world issues from the sensor aspect (dynamic sensor combina-
tions), data aspect (limited labeled data and heterogeneous data distribution), and task aspect (various recognition
tasks in different scenes). Specifically, (i) we propose a masked-data modeling-based self-supervised pre-training
method and a tailored recovery loss for sensing data to make the model harness various combinations of data
from diverse available sensors, meanwhile enabling the model to train simultaneously on datasets from different
scenarios and tasks; (ii) we propose a few-shot alignment and fine-tuning mechanism to enable the model to
adapt to different scenes, positions, and devices with only a small amount of labeled data. Furthermore, MASTER
can be compatible with other masked modeling-based methods, e.g., masked-text modeling. This implies that
MASTER has the potential to align with text and facilitate zero-shot HAR.
System Deployment. As a multi-modal foundation foundation model for HAR, MASTER is typically trained
on a cloud server. The limited amount of labeled data required for alignment and fine-tuning can be annotated
by volunteers. When it comes to a new device, if no new modalities are introduced, there is no need to retrain

157:22 • Zhu et al.
the model. In practical applications, the lightweight encoders of MASTER can be deployed on the sensors’ side to
perform feature extraction. The remaining components of the recognition model, such as transformer blocks and
the classifier, which are computationally intensive, can be deployed on smart devices with abundant computational
resources, such as smartphones and home hubs.
Potential Applications. In addition to the HAR application scenarios mentioned in Sect. 1, MASTER can be
further extended to other applications, such as handling multi-modal physiological data. This includes data from
sources like electrocardiography (ECG) and photoplethysmography (PPG). This extension would enable the
monitoring of physiological indicators and identification of potential diseases, as multi-modal physiological data
also contains consistent and complementary information.
Limitations. There are several limitations that need to be claimed. First, in multi-modal HAR, we currently
face a shortage of high-quality multi-modal data for pre-training, which hampers the performance of the model.
For instance, the UTD dataset [9] comprises 3 modalities and 27 categories but only contains 828 samples. This
data scarcity makes it challenging for the transformer in MASTER to be fully activated, resulting in the MASTER’s
performance being comparable with the SOTA baselines. This limitation also restricts the width and depth of
the MASTER model, preventing it from reaching the scale of other multi-modal foundation models in the field of
NLP or CV, which can stack up to over 96 transformer blocks. Although our experiments have demonstrated
that leveraging data generation/augmentation methods can enhance MASTER’s performance, the patterns in the
generated data are constrained and may diverge from the real-world data. Second, the pre-training overhead of
MASTER is higher than the SOTA baselines. Fortunately, MASTER offers the advantage of requiring only a single
model to be trained to apply to various modalities, scenarios, positions, devices, and tasks. In contrast, existing
methods require the training of multiple separate models for different situations. Third, the high computational
requirements during the inference stage of the model may necessitate the uploading of multi-modal sensing data
or patch embeddings [59] to the cloud or edge servers, resulting in privacy concerns and extra communication
overhead [57, 58].
8 RELATED WORKS
Multi-modal Sensing for HAR. Many studies [12, 28, 29, 31, 46, 47, 50, 54, 61] focus on using multi-modal data
to perform HAR. For example, PDLens [54] utilizes multiple built-in smartphone sensors to monitor human daily
activities; Zhu et al. [61] combine smart speakers and smart meters to recognize human electricity consumption
behaviors. However, these methods are designed for fixed modalities. Some deep learning-based multi-modal
fusion methods are designed for multiple combinations of modalities [12, 28, 31, 50]. For instance, Eldele et al. [17]
propose a self-supervised framework to fuse any two different modalities; Ouyang et al. [30] propose a contrastive
learning-based unified framework for multi-modal HAR with small labeled data. However, these methods still
have strict restrictions on the number of input modalities, which cannot adapt to real-world scenarios with
any combination of modalities. To make the model adapt to dynamic input modalities, Zheng et al. [60] use an
autoencoder-based data imputation method to fulfill the missing modality in pairs of modalities, enabling normal
model inference; Li et al. [24] train an independent sub-model for each modality using a model ensemble method.
However, the above works prove ineffective or entail significant training overhead when dealing with HAR data
that is rich in modalities.
Multi-modal Large Language Models. Many MLLMs [3, 16, 18, 23, 32, 43] have emerged to understand
human intentions from a multi-modal view. For instance, in the fields of NLP and CV, CLIP [32] employs
contrastive learning to align features from text and images, enabling the model to distinguish between unseen
classes; PaLM-E [16] projects image representations into the text representation space, allowing the model
to understand image information in a language processing manner; ImageBind [18] can handle data from six
modalities, including text, images, audio, depth, thermal, and IMU, by binding them with text or images. However,
Proc. ACM Interact. Mob. Wearable Ubiquitous Technol., Vol. 9, No. 3, Article 157. Publication date: September 2025.
MASTER: A Multi-modal Foundation Model for Human Activity Recognition • 157:23
the above MLLMs can only handle a small fixed number of input modalities (e.g., text, images, and audio). When
extended to some HAR-related sensing modalities (e.g., depth, thermal, and IMU), they still require alignment
with strong interpretable modalities like text, which are not commonly present in HAR scenarios.
Foundation Models for HAR. Some studies [44, 46–48, 52] build a uni-modal foundation model for HAR.
For example, Xu et al. [47] propose limu-bert, the first IMU-specific foundation model in the HAR field. Xu
et al. [48] propose RelCon, an accelerometer-specific foundation model. Similarly, Weng et al. [44] propose a
Radio-Frequency-specific foundation model for HAR. Although these models are limited to specific sensing
modalities, they share a similar objective with our MASTER, i.e., achieving superior performance across diverse
scenes, positions, and devices with limited labeled data. However, their pretraining strategies or backbone
architectures are difficult to generalize to multi-modal settings. For multi-modal HAR foundation models, a recent
representative work is BABEL [11], which builds on the ImageBind [18] architecture to perform pairwise modality
alignment. However, it faces several limitations during pretraining: i) It lacks the ability to learn discriminative
information during pre-training, as it focuses solely on aligning different modalities into the same representation,
without the capacity to distinguish between activities in an unsupervised manner, as shown in Figs. 7(a) and
8(b). ii) It fails to utilize the complementary information within heterogeneous modalities; specifically, aligning
modalities with rich information to those with less information leads to the loss of valuable complementary
information. iii) Its alignment strategy is restricted to modality pairs from a single dataset at a time, which
prevents comprehensive knowledge integration across multiple modalities and datasets. Different from the
aforementioned foundation models for HAR, MASTER is a multi-modal foundational model specifically designed
to tackle the practical challenges of HAR, i.e., dynamic modality combinations and few-shot adaptability, as
discussed in Sect. 1. Furthermore, our pre-training strategy facilitates simultaneous learning of both consistency
and complementary information across multiple datasets and heterogeneous modalities.
Few-shot Learning. Several studies [4, 22, 31, 34, 46] have investigated the application of few-shot learning
in HAR. These works use meta-learning or complex data augmentation techniques to pre-train a well-generalized
backbone model, and then fine-tune the model with small labeled data to adapt to the target users. However,
these approaches are designed for a fixed pair of specific sensor modalities and cannot be easily extended to
other heterogeneous sensor modalities. While some works [12, 31] explore multi-modal few-shot learning using
contrastive learning, they still face limitations in handling dynamic combinations of modalities.
9 CONCLUSION
This work proposes MASTER, a multi-modal foundation model specifically designed for HAR in real-world settings.
MASTER features a novel masked-data modeling-based self-supervised pre-training method to adapt to dynamic
combinations of modal inputs, and a few-shot alignment mechanism to adapt to various activities, scenes, positions,
and devices. Through pre-training and fine-tuning on 7 multi-modal HAR datasets, our MASTER supports at least
8 modalities and 45 activities. Extensive experimental results demonstrate that MASTER achieves the highest
accuracy with minimal labeled data in various situations, surpassing other SOTA baselines.
