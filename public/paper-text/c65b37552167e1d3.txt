Abstract
Wrist micro-behaviors during daily activities such as typing, hand-
writing, cooking, or carrying objects are valuable indicators for
early detection of wrist disorders like Carpal Tunnel Syndrome and
tendonitis. However, continuous personalized monitoring remains
challenging without intrusive setups or hand-crafted rules. We
present WristSense, a real-time, wrist-worn sensing system that in-
troduces: (i) a magnetometer-stabilized quaternion fusion pipeline
for orientation-agnostic tracking, (ii) a lightweight 1D-CNN + HMM
model to distinguish functional gestures from strain-related coping
behaviors, and (iii) an inertial tokenization scheme that converts
events into structured prompts for a pretrained LLM. This enables

1 Introduction
Wrist injuries from everyday activities are a growing but silent
epidemic. Modern routines involve prolonged typing, texting, lift-
ing, and cooking – actions that repetitively stress the wrist and
hand. Conditions like Carpal Tunnel Syndrome (CTS), tendonitis,
and De Quervain’s tenosynovitis often arise from subtle, repetitive
micro-movements like wrist flicking, finger cracking, or knuckle
crunching performed unconsciously during these activities [15, 33].
Yet, people rarely notice such poor habits, wrist posture, or strain
until pain becomes debilitating [31]. Early intervention is key, but
remains elusive due to the absence of continuous, personalized, and
unobtrusive monitoring tools.
Wearables, especially smartwatches, present an opportunity for
real-time, unobtrusive monitoring of musculoskeletal strain. Un-
like camera-based systems or glove-like wearables [4, 22], which
require controlled environments or specialized hardware, smart-
watches are widely adopted and integrated into daily routines. Their
9-axis IMUs, combining accelerometers, gyroscopes, and magne-
tometers, capture fine-grained motion data suitable for subtle wrist
and arm movements. Prior work has leveraged smartwatch IMUs
for gesture recognition, pose reconstruction, and interactive con-
trol [8, 13, 40, 41, 51, 53], but primarily targets deliberate gestures
in controlled settings. These frameworks overlook the spontaneous
micro-movements that naturally occur during daily activities and
reveal physical strain or fatigue. Although systems like Watch-
Guardian [27] address small, spontaneous actions using few-shot
intervention, they still capture coarse, intentional gestures per-
formed in isolation. In contrast, involuntary micro-behaviors such
as wrist clenching, finger flicking, or joint cracking arise during
routine functional tasks and serve as early, yet often unnoticed,
indicators of ergonomic strain.
In summary, despite advances in wearable sensing and gesture
recognition, existing wrist-worn systems lack a unified framework
that can: (1) continuously monitor wrist posture across common
daily activities such as typing, writing, lifting, and cooking; (2)
detect subtle, involuntary strain-induced behaviors such as flicking,
or joint cracking; and (3) translate these signals into personalized,
context-aware ergonomic feedback. Such a system could enable
early detection of musculoskeletal stress and deliver actionable
guidance for preventing injury. Furthermore, rule-based feedback
systems [5] lack personalization, and recent Large Language Model
(LLM) based approaches [52] are predominantly video-based and
not suited for real-time, wearable applications.
Research Challenges: Designing such a system introduces core
challenges stemming from the limitations of inertial sensing, user-
specific behavior patterns, and the difficulty of translating low-level
signals into high-level semantic feedback, as follows.
C1: Ambiguity in Single-Point IMU Sensing. Wrist-worn IMUs
capture a superimposed signal of both wrist and arm motion. Fine
finger gestures and wrist twitches often appear indistinguishable
in raw data, complicating fine-grained inference and animation.
C2: Orientation Drift and Frame Variability. Smartwatch data
in the local frame drifts with wrist rotation, body movement, and

magnetic interference (Figure 2). This prevents consistent posture
measurement across users, time, and sessions.
C3: Irregularity of Pain-Related Behaviors. Discomfort-
induced behaviors like clenching or cracking are brief, user-specific,
and unscripted. Unlike deliberate gestures, they are harder to detect
without contextual cues.
C4: Lack of Personalized, Understandable Feedback. Even
when strain is detected, most systems cannot explain what it means
or how to correct it. Designing ergonomic rules by hand is infeasible
across diverse users, making personalization challenging.
Our Core Idea. We present WristSense, a real-time ergonomic
sensing system that combines smartwatch IMU data with LLM
reasoning to detect and communicate wrist strain behaviors (Fig-
ure 1). WristSense addresses C1–C4 through three innovations: (i)
compass-aware posture encoding for orientation-invariant met-
rics, (ii) a hierarchical CNN–HMM pipeline for detecting functional
tasks and coping gestures, and (iii) inertial tokenization that con-
verts events into structured prompts for conversational LLM-based
coaching. Our contributions are as follows:
• Compass-Aware Posture Encoding. We introduce a novel
magnetometer-stabilized sensor fusion pipeline that computes
roll, pitch, and yaw in a world-fixed frame, resolving orientation
drift common in wrist-mounted IMUs. From this stable refer-
ence, we derive sign-corrected radial/ulnar deviation and prona-
tion/supination metrics (Figure 2) that are invariant to arm orien-
tation or body pose. This is, to our knowledge, the first IMU-based
method to enable consistent, user-agnostic wrist posture tracking
across diverse free-form activities without external references or
calibration. (Addresses C1, C2)
• Hierarchical Gesture Recognition via CNN–HMM. We pro-
pose a novel two-stage recognition architecture that separates
functional and strain-induced behaviors for improved accuracy
and interpretability. A lightweight CNN first detects high-level
daily activities (e.g., typing, cooking), while a second-stage HMM
is conditionally activated to detect subtle, transient coping be-
haviors (e.g., joint cracking, clenching) within those contexts.
This hierarchical structure allows for context-aware detection of
sparse micro-movements and reduces false positives, represent-
ing a significant departure from traditional flat classifiers that
treat all gestures uniformly. (Addresses C2, C3)
• Inertial Tokenization for LLM Feedback. We introduce a
novel semantic tokenization scheme that bridges low-level iner-
tial data with high-level language-based feedback. By encoding

guidance using only a smartwatch and LLM API, thus paving the
way for scalable, user-aware strain monitoring in daily life.
2 Pilot Study and Design Hypothesis
In clinical and ergonomic studies, repetitive strain disorders such
as CTS are often preceded by subtle, subconscious behaviors (like
wrist clenching, finger flicking, knuckle cracking, or palm press-
ing) performed to relieve early discomfort. Though not commu-
nicative, these actions act as early proxies of internal strain and
musculoskeletal fatigue [15, 33]. Hence, our gesture taxonomy was
informed by ergonomic and biomechanics studies [7, 43], which
describe these aforementioned coping behaviors as early compen-
satory responses to local muscular fatigue in hands. In contrast,
functional gestures (typing, scrolling, writing, lifting, carrying,
cooking, using tools) correspond to task-driven kinematics with
stable joint angles and low tremor variance [23, 26]. We distinguish
through joint-angle deviation and micro-motion features.
To develop our system architecture, we conducted a preliminary
signal exploration study with 8 participants (4M, 4F, aged 20–35).
Each participant wore a smartwatch (Apple Watch Ultra) while per-
forming daily tasks such as typing, lifting, cooking, or smartphone
use for 20–30 minutes in a sensor-instrumented testbed. They were
instructed to behave naturally, with no scripted actions. All ses-
sions were synchronized with video and IMU logging for offline
inspection. The goal was not to validate any detection pipeline
but to answer a simple question: What types of signals can a wrist-
mounted IMU capture in real-world strain contexts, and how should
these drive our system design?

a backpack, and (iv) writing on a notebook. All exhibit distinct
acceleration and angular velocity rhythms, confirming that a single
wrist IMU is sensitive enough to distinguish daily use functional

Insight 1: Static load tasks such as holding a bag or stabilizing a
dumbbell produced inertial plateaus, rather than distinct motion
bursts. Thus, we must combine dynamic gestures with posture-
based strain metrics (e.g., deviation angles).
Insight 2: Unlike structured functional gestures (e.g., typing),
strain-relief actions (e.g., knuckle cracking) lack clearly defined
start or end points. Instead, they manifest as brief spikes in angular
velocity, indicating the need for temporally aware classification.
Insight 3: Strain-relief behaviors were mostly observed during
functional activities, suggesting that detection can be efficiently
triggered only during these periods, reducing noise and focusing
analysis where it matters.
Insight 4: Wrist orientation angles, when derived solely from gy-
roscope and accelerometer data, are prone to drift over time and
may exhibit sign ambiguity due to inconsistent reference frames.
Without a stable global reference, such as magnetic north [41], the
same wrist posture can produce different angle readings. Incorpo-
rating compass orientation helps correct for this drift and ensures
consistent, interpretable measurements across sessions and users.
While prior sensor fusion methods such as Shen et al. [41] miti-
gate orientation drift, they rely on constrained motion or periodic
reference events that are unsuitable for free-hand, wrist-only sens-
ing, leaving orientation drift and sign ambiguity unresolved with-
out magnetometer input. Existing IMU-based wrist systems largely
emphasize activity recognition or motion reconstruction without
offering personalized feedback [2], relying instead on static, rule-
based alerts that fail to adapt to individual ergonomic differences.
WristSense addresses these limitations by computing radial/ulnar
deviation and pronation/supination in a magnetometer-stabilized,
world-aligned frame, providing anatomically consistent and robust
wrist posture metrics across diverse users and activities.

Hypothesis and Problem Formulation: Building on the observa-
tions from the pilot study and prior literature, we hypothesize that:
(H1) Wrist-worn IMU data can reliably capture wrist orientation
and hand-based functional gestures across diverse tasks; (H2) pain-

localized using multi-stage classification; and (H3) wrist posture

sensor fusion, and (H4) tokenizing detected behaviors into semanti-

or domain-specific heuristics.

3 System Design
WristSense, as shown in Figure 4 consists of three core modules: (1) a
streaming and sensing layer, (2) an on-device processing pipeline for
inertial tokenization, and (3) a feedback and visualization interface.
In the first stage, an Apple Watch Series 9/Ultra equipped with a
tri-axial accelerometer, gyroscope, and magnetometer samples nine-
axis data at 50 Hz and transmits it over Bluetooth to a companion
iOS app. All computation runs on the paired smartphone in order
to preserve battery life and conserve watch resources.
The processing pipeline begins by fusing accelerometer, gyro,
and magnetometer readings into a stable Earth-frame quaternion,
from which we derive Euler angles as well as sign-corrected ra-
dial/ulnar deviation and pronation/supination metrics. Overlapping
windows (1.5 seconds) of these fused orientations, raw signals and
spectral/statistical features feed into a compact 1D-CNN + HMM
model that continuously recognizes first functional gestures (typing
on a keyboard, typing/scrolling on a smartphone, lifting objects,
writing, cooking (simple tasks like stirring, chopping), using tools
(hammer/screwdriver), and carrying objects (bags/groceries)) and
then during the window of the functional gestures, the strain re-
lated coping behaviors (joint cracking, flicking, clenching, wrist
rotation), as well as directional circle motions, are monitored along
with their count of occurrences.

Prior methods often relied on uncorrected gyro integration or basic
accelerometer alignment, both of which are prone to drift and yield
unstable axes during unconstrained wrist motion. While Shen et
al. [41] propose drift mitigation through kinematic constraints and
periodic error resets, their approach assumes either structured full-
body motion or intermittent reference events (e.g., foot-ground
contact), which are not available in wrist-only, free-hand scenarios.
Our contribution lies in introducing a compass-stabilized quater-
nion framework specifically designed for wrist-mounted IMUs,
where neither structured motion nor global orientation anchors
exist. By leveraging the magnetometer to resolve yaw ambiguity
and projecting onto anatomically aligned planes, we obtain reliable,
user-agnostic deviation metrics that remain stable across postures,
sessions, and users, making this approach uniquely suited for real-
time ergonomic monitoring in everyday activities.
Feature Extraction and Windowing. After fusion, orientation,
and angle computations, all derived metrics and raw IMU streams
are resampled to a uniform 50 Hz and segmented into overlap-
ping windows of length (L=60) samples (1.2 s at 50 Hz) with 50%
overlap. Each window undergoes standardization (zero-mean, unit-
variance) and includes both first and second discrete differences,
capturing short-term velocity and acceleration cues. These tem-
poral derivatives, together with angular deviation and cross-axis
correlations, characterize rapid strain-relief motions (e.g., clench-
ing, flicking) that differ from slower, periodic functional gestures
(e.g., typing, lifting). Additional statistical and spectral features
(mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, RMS, interquartile range, zero-
crossing and peak counts, signal magnitude area, spectral band
energies, spectral centroid, entropy, discrete wavelet coefficients,
and autoregressive coefficients) further encode frequency-domain
cues linked to functional gesture movement and their intensity. The
resulting ≃150-dimensional, orientation-invariant feature vector
thus preserves both instantaneous motion signatures and short-
term temporal context, which are exploited by the CNN for spa-
tial–kinematic encoding and the subsequent HMM for temporal
sequencing and state smoothing in our two-stage pipeline.
3.2 Gesture and Behavior Classification
Our two-stage classification pipeline involves first detecting high-
level functional gestures and then monitoring specific strain-related
coping gestures during those detected functional activities, as
shown in Figure 5. This hierarchical design reduces computational
complexity and improves recognition accuracy by conditioning the
fine-grained gesture detection on the presence of broader activity.
We employ a CNN–HMM hybrid model for hierarchical recognition,
where the CNN extracts motion patterns from short data windows
and the HMM enforces temporal consistency. This design smooths
predictions, prevents invalid gesture transitions, and detects coping
behaviors only during functional activities, ensuring stable and
efficient recognition without additional counters or regressors.

3.4 Inertial Tokenization and LLM Integration
To bridge the gap between raw IMU gesture classification outputs
and intuitive, actionable user feedback, WristSense introduces a
structured inertial tokenization schema coupled with a large lan-
guage model via API integration (GPT-4.1). Rather than directly
feeding the continuous, noisy IMU stream into an LLM, we first
transform the gesture classification results into discrete, semanti-
cally rich tokens. These tokens encapsulate the functional gesture,
frequency of strain-related coping behaviors, and precise posture
angles over clearly defined time windows, enabling the LLM to
provide context-aware, ergonomic guidance.
Token Generation from Gesture Classification. Consider a
detected functional gesture event from Stage 1 (Sec. 3.2), such as
typing. For each such event, we define a structured token:

ACTIVITY, START, END,
COPING_COUNTS, PALM_ANGLE, FINGER_ANGLE

Here, each element is defined explicitly as follows:
• ACTIVITY: The recognized functional gesture class (e.g.,
Typing, Lifting, Tapping) identified by the Stage 1
CNN+HMM.
• START, END: Timestamps marking the exact beginning and
end of the detected functional gesture sequence, respectively,
obtained from the Viterbi decoding sequence of Stage 1.
• COPING_COUNTS: A vector indicating the number of occur-
rences of each strain-related coping gesture (e.g., {FLICK :
2, CLENCH : 1, JOINT_CRACK : 3}) detected within the
functional gesture’s timeframe by the Stage 2 model.
• PALM_ANGLE, FINGER_ANGLE: Peak palm tilt and finger
flex angles, respectively, recorded by the wrist visualization
module during the functional gesture period.

*

within the entire gesture window.

Token Formation and Semantic Mapping. Each inertial to-
ken summarizes an entire activity episode, spanning 20 minutes.
During this period, the CNN–HMM pipeline continuously classifies
functional gestures and counts strain-related coping behaviors. If
the user pauses briefly and resumes the same motion pattern within
one minute, the event is merged into the same token window using
an empirically tuned delay threshold. Each token thus records the
Activity label, its start–end timestamps, and the total counts of
coping gestures detected across that interval. Postural features such
as palm and finger angles are recorded as peak values observed
during the session, capturing worst-case wrist deviation and flexion
for ergonomic risk assessment. They are retained as raw degree
values, allowing the LLM to interpret magnitude directly within its
prompt context. The final token is therefore a structured activity
summary (e.g., [Typing, 12:03:10–12:15:20, JointCrack=3,
Flick=2, Palm=15°, Finger=22°]), preserving the semantics of
duration, intensity, and strain for contextual reasoning by the LLM.
Two-Stage Prompting for LLM Integration. To generate ac-
tionable ergonomic insights from these inertial tokens, we use a
structured two-stage prompting approach. First, we set a stable
system prompt to clearly define the role and expectations of LLM:
System Prompt:
You are an ergonomic assistant. You receive summarized to-
kens describing recent user wrist activities, durations, strain-
related coping behaviors (e.g., joint cracks, flicks, clenches),
and wrist posture angles. Provide (1) actionable ergonomic
advice to reduce wrist strain, and (2) a concise fatigue sum-
mary for the session. Keep responses clear and practical.
Next, at runtime, we dynamically compose a user prompt from
recent inertial tokens. For instance:
User Prompt:
Recent events:
- Activity: Typing, Start: 12:03:10, End: 12:15:20, JointCrack:
3, Flick: 2, PalmAngle: 15°, FingerAngle: 22°
- Activity: Lifting, Start: 12:17:30, End: 12:19:00, Clench: 2,
Flick: 1, PalmAngle: 25°, FingerAngle: 30°
Please provide ergonomic recommendations and a brief fa-
tigue summary based on these activities.
The combined system and user prompts are sent via the OpenAI
API (GPT-4.1, temp = 0 for deterministic output). The LLM returns
a natural-language response, such as “Your wrist angle reached 25°,
which suggests mild radial deviation. Try resting your forearm flat
and adjusting keyboard height to reduce strain.” which we parse
and display directly through notifications on the smartwatch. This
structured token interface allows the LLM to operate as a shared,
zero-shot feedback layer, generalizing across user styles and activi-
ties without increasing sensing or model complexity.
4 Implementation and Experiments
We conducted a multi-phase evaluation of WristSense to validate
its ability to detect and classify wrist postures and finger dynamics
in unconstrained settings. First, we developed a proof-of-concept
iOS application embedding our pretrained CNN-HMM pipeline,
and deployed it to 12 participants over a two-week period, during
which we recorded over 18 hours of wrist IMU streams across
everyday tasks, which are part of our primary set of gestures for
classification, categorised as functional states, specifically, (i) typing
on keyboard, (ii) typing/scrolling on a smartphone, (iii) lifting while
exercising, (iv) writing, (v) cooking (noodles), (vi) using tools, and
finally (vii) carrying stuff (grocery bags). Along with functional
states, we also asked the participants to record their non-functional
coping behaviors, to relieve their wrist pain, which are part of our
secondary set of gestures for classification, which are categorized
as (i) joint cracking, (ii) finger flicking, (iii) palm clenching, and
(iv) rotating the wrist in clockwise or counter clockwise manner,
while collecting sparse ground-truth via camera recordings and self-
reports. Next, we performed an in-the-wild usability study to assess
robustness across varied smartphone models, ambient conditions,
and user behaviors, measuring end-to-end latency, classification
accuracy, and perceived responsiveness.
Participants: We recruited 12 participants (6 female, 6 male,
aged 23-42, median age 29, 6 university students, 4 office work-
ers, and 2 homemakers) from the authors’ institute, with diverse
daily routines involving various aforementioned wrist-intensive
activities, across academic, professional, and household contexts,
providing a broad first validation of WristSense beyond a single
occupation group. All participants wore the smartwatch (Apple
Watch Series Ultra/Samsung Galaxy Watch 4 Classic) on their dom-
inant hand and reported no history of medically diagnosed wrist
injuries; however, several indicated frequent wrist discomfort fol-
lowing prolonged wrist-intensive activities. Informed consent was
obtained from all participants in accordance with our IRB-approved
protocol. Participants were compensated with a $10 gift card.
Experimental Setup: Data collection for evaluating WristSense
occurred in two distinct phases: (1) a semi-controlled environment
within a testbed hosted at the author’s institute, and (2) an in-the-
wild setting during participants’ regular daily routines.
(1) Testbed-based Semi-Controlled Setup: Participants first
completed structured sessions in a 3D-printed smart-home testbed
equipped with multimodal sensors (temperature, humidity, CO2,
light, mm-wave occupancy, ambient noise, furniture and water-
level sensors) to simulate realistic home environments with precise
context monitoring. They performed typical daily tasks, such as
typing, smartphone use, cooking, exercising, carrying objects, and
tool use, alongside natural discomfort-related behaviors such as
wrist rotations, hand clenching, finger tapping, palm pressing, and
joint cracking, as outlined in Section 2. Transitional actions like
short walks and seated browsing were also included to capture idle
and in-between wrist movements.
(2) In-the-Wild Data Collection: After completing the Sense-
pod sessions, participants continued to wear the smartwatch during
their everyday activities outside the laboratory over the subsequent
week. During this phase, participants independently recorded their
wrist-intensive behaviors and coping gestures through the provided
smartwatch and companion application, allowing us to capture nat-
urally occurring strain-related behaviors in unconstrained settings.
Annotation and Labeling: Three volunteer annotators inde-
pendently reviewed the synchronized video recordings, which were
manually annotated for functional gestures and corrective move-
ments. Inter-annotator agreement, measured using Cohen’s Kappa
[32], was 0.81, reflecting a high level of consistency across observers.
To capture individual variability and ensure a comprehensive range
of natural behaviors, each participant repeated every activity twice

Model Training: Model training was carried out using the anno-

convolutional block, and each input channel was normalized to

5 Results
We evaluate WristSense on its ability to recognize functional and
strain-related hand gestures from wrist-worn IMU data and to pro-
vide posture-aware ergonomic feedback in real-world conditions.
The evaluation focuses on three aspects: (1) Gesture Recognition
Accuracy, using macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1-score
across functional and coping classes; (2) Posture Tracking Ac-
curacy, comparing estimated wrist angles (radial/ulnar deviation
and pronation/supination) against ground truth from MediaPipe
Hands [47]; and (3) User-Centered Feedback Quality, measured
through SUS [28] scores, qualitative feedback, and BERTScore-
based similarity [50] between LLM-generated and orthopedist ad-
vice. Vision-based comparisons use the MediaPipe implementation2,
which estimates wrist and finger joint angles from RGB video.
5.1 Performance over In-House Dataset
5.1.1 Gesture Classification Performance. We compare WristSense
against two recent smartwatch-based IMU systems: PrISM-Q&A [2]
and WatchGuardian [27]. While neither system is designed specifi-
cally for ergonomic strain detection, both represent strong baselines
for fine-grained activity recognition using smartwatch IMUs. To
ensure fair comparison, we re-implemented and retrained their
inertial pipelines on our dataset (available under our source-code
repository), excluding auxiliary sensors such as microphones or
user-defined prompts.
As summarized in Table 1, WristSense achieves a macro F1 score
of 0.91, outperforming PrISM-Q&A (0.78) and WatchGuardian
(0.74). Unlike WatchGuardian’s user-specific classifiers trained on
short sessions or PrISM’s coarse segmentation optimized for proce-
dural tasks, our two-stage CNN-HMM pipeline separates functional
and coping gestures explicitly, improving the classification of subtle
strain-related behaviors such as joint cracking and wrist clenching
by up to 25% in true-positive rate. Figures 6 and 7 visualize the
confusion matrices. Functional gestures such as Keyboard, Smart-
phone, and Carrying exceed 80% accuracy, with some confusion in
semantically similar activities like Cooking and Tool Use. Coping
gestures also show high separability, with Joint Crack reaching
95.8% true positives and minimal misclassification. These results
validate our architecture’s ability to distinguish macro and micro
hand activities using only wrist-mounted IMUs.

5.1.4 Generalization: LOSO and LOTO Analysis. To evaluate the
generalization capability of WristSense, we conducted two standard
cross-validation experiments: Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) and
Leave-One-Task-Out (LOTO). In LOSO, we trained the model on
𝑁 −1 participants and tested on the held-out user, iterating across all
participants. In LOTO, we trained on all but one functional activity
(e.g., excluding Cooking) and evaluated performance on that un-
seen task. As shown in Table 3, the two-stage CNN+HMM pipeline
generalizes significantly better than a flat CNN baseline across both
unseen users and unseen activities. In particular, WristSense retains
a macro F1 score of 81.6% in the LOSO setting, compared to 74.2%
for CNN-only, demonstrating robustness to inter-user variability.
5.2 UX Studies (In-the-Wild Experiments)

5.2.2 Utility of LLM Feedback Layer. We conducted a comparison
study with the participants to validate the use of the LLM-based

substantially influence user compliance and perceived system value.

6 Discussion and Ablation Studies
6.1 LLM vs. Rule-Based Feedback
To further justify the use of an LLM, we compared its contextual
reasoning against rule-based feedback. A static template provided
the output “Warning: wrist bent beyond 20 for ≥10 min”, whereas
GPT-4.1 interprets multi-sensor cues to produce richer, task-aware
advice such as “Your wrist was bent outward for over 10 minutes
while typing; consider lowering your keyboard height or taking
a short break.” This demonstrates the LLM’s ability to merge pos-
ture metrics with activity context, enabling naturalistic ergonomic
coaching unattainable with fixed thresholds. Nonetheless, halluci-
nation risks persist, so we constrained prompts, restricted action
vocabularies (e.g., rest, adjust, stretch), and recommend optional
human oversight in clinical use.
6.2 Cloud and Local LLMs: Tradeoffs
To reduce cloud dependence and enable private, always-available
inference, recent quantized local LLMs (e.g., Mistral [14], Phi-3
Mini [1]) show promise for on-device use. However, real-time
deployment on wearables remains constrained by high memory
(>4 GB), energy demands, and limited processing throughput. Run-
ning models like LLaMA-3 8B [48] or TinyLlama [49] on paired
smartphones lowered latency (~3000-6000 ms to ~300 ms) and en-
abled offline use, but often caused hallucinated or misaligned feed-
back. Thus, current lightweight LLMs lack sufficient semantic reli-
ability for ergonomic reasoning and introduce trade-offs such as
battery drain, memory pressure, thermal throttling, and constrained
prompt windows (Table 6).
6.3 Model Selection: Beyond GPT-4.1
Integrating LLMs into wearable sensing pipelines like WristSense
enables intuitive, personalized feedback. We used GPT-4.1 via API
for its strong performance, accessibility, and consistent outputs,
making it a reliable baseline for ergonomic coaching. LLM selec-
tion remains a key design choice: alternatives such as GPT-3.5 [44],
Claude 3 [37], and open-source models like Mistral [21], LLaMA-
3 [48], and Command-R [11] offer varying strengths in coherence,
safety, and formatting. In testing, GPT-3.5 generated generic feed-
back on long or repetitive logs, while GPT-4.1 produced more pre-
cise summaries and reasoning. Latency comparisons (Figure 11)
showed smaller models were faster but less accurate. Overall, GPT-
4.1 provided the best balance of quality, reliability, and contextual
validity without hallucinations.

6.4 Orientation Estimation Strategies
We conducted an ablation study to evaluate the effectiveness of our
approach for wrist orientation tracking. Specifically, we compared
it against two common IMU-based methods: (i) gyroscope-only
integration, which accumulates drift over time, and (ii) a comple-
mentary filter that blends gyroscope and accelerometer data. As
shown in Table 7, gyroscope-only integration yields substantial
angular error (MAE: 14.21°), while the complementary filter offers
moderate improvement (MAE: 7.84°). In contrast, our method re-
duces the mean absolute error to 4.78°, highlighting the value of
magnetometer-guided yaw correction for achieving stable, drift-
resistant orientation estimates in free-form wrist movements.
6.5 Impact of Hierarchical Design
To assess the impact of temporal modeling and architectural hierar-
chy in WristSense, we conducted a two-part study comparing three
model configurations. (i) CNN-only Baseline: A single 1D-CNN
trained on windowed features performs well on high-level func-
tional gestures (F1: 0.87) but struggles with temporal alignment and
produces jittery outputs for sparse coping gestures due to indepen-
dent window-wise predictions (Table 8). (ii) CNN + HMM (Single-
stage): Adding a discrete HMM layer improves temporal consis-
tency and boosts coping gesture F1 from 0.52 to 0.71 by smoothing
fluctuations and enabling natural event counting through state
transitions (Table 9). (iii) Two-Stage CNN + HMM: Activating
coping gesture recognition only within functional windows further
improves macro F1 to 0.91 (Table 8) and enhances separation of
overlapping gesture types. t-SNE visualizations (Figure 12) confirm
that this hierarchical structure leads to better clustering of cop-
ing behaviors, demonstrating that functional context serves as a
valuable inductive bias for fine-grained behavior recognition.

6.8 Limitations and Future Directions
WristSense shows strong performance; nevertheless, it has a few
limitations. Its modular CNN-HMM pipeline depends on prede-
fined gesture classes, limiting the system’s ability to detect new or
unlabeled behaviors. The method is currently restricted to wrist-
centric strain detection and does not extend to the elbow, shoul-
der, or full-body ergonomics, which are important in clinical and
industrial contexts. Some misclassifications occurred during upper-
body stretches or postural adjustments, revealing challenges in
distinguishing coping gestures from broader movements. Clinical
validation on larger, more diverse populations, including patients
with musculoskeletal disorders and high-risk occupational groups,
is essential before healthcare deployment. Although the LLM feed-
back aligns closely with orthopedist guidance (BERTScore = 0.89),
it should serve as supportive coaching, not a medical diagnosis.
Safe adoption will require clear disclaimers, optional human-in-the-
loop review, and integration with certified clinical decision-support
systems. Future longitudinal studies will evaluate not only accu-
racy but also real-world health outcomes, such as reduced strain
symptoms and injury prevention.
While WristSense currently relies on inertial sensing for wrist
strain analysis, the same tokenization principle can generalize to
multi-sensor wearables such as smartwatches, rings, earables, and
smart ornaments. By extending inertial tokens into multi-sensor to-
kens that fuse motion with physiological and contextual signals (e.g.,
heart rate, temperature, EDA, ambient context), the framework can
support broader health and ergonomic monitoring beyond the wrist.
Additionally, future work could also explore end-to-end sensor-to-
language models (e.g., TimeLLM, GPT-4o) to bypass handcrafted
tokenization. LLMs could also support richer applications, such
as generating clinician-friendly reports, summarizing behavioral
trends, facilitating dialogue-based troubleshooting, and enabling
conversational personalization of feedback. While early results
show promising user engagement, ensuring the safety, reliability,
and clinical relevance of LLM-generated advice requires long-term
deployment with human-in-the-loop validation. Finally, while the
magnetometer-based fusion handles minor disturbances, strong
local interference can still affect yaw stability; future work will add
disturbance detection and adaptive weighting to mitigate this.
7 Related Work
We summarize prior work (Table 11) across three areas: (1) IMU-
based wrist sensing, (2) wearable ergonomic monitoring, and (3)
language models for feedback generation.
Wrist-Worn IMU Systems. Prior work has explored the use of
wrist-worn IMUs for physical activity tracking [3, 17, 19, 20], fall
detection [12], and coarse-grained gesture recognition [25]. Other
works like Zhang et al. [46] recognize fine finger gestures but ig-
nore strain-related behaviors. The most closely related work to our
system is MUSE [41], which also employs magnetometer readings
from a wrist-mounted smartwatch IMU to identify hand motion
trajectories. MUSE fuses IMU data with a magnetometer-corrected
orientation pipeline and leverages particle filters and motion di-
rection vectors to estimate hand displacement in 2D space during
object interaction. However, their focus is on recognizing object
manipulation gestures by tracking motion traces over time. Simi-
larly, another work, ArmTrak [40] reconstructs full arm poses via
HMMs but focuses on upper limb kinematics using only the gyro
and accelerometer data. In contrast, our system does not rely on
trajectory reconstruction or displacement estimation. Instead, we
use magnetometer-stabilized quaternions to extract stable, sign-
corrected wrist posture angles, specifically radial/ulnar deviation
and pronation/supination, from a fixed forearm-aligned frame.
Wearables for Ergonomic Monitoring. Prior work has explored
gloves [36], EMG [9], and textile-based sensors [18] for posture
and strain detection. While accurate, these setups are intrusive
and unsuitable for everyday use. However, recent systems like
WatchGuardian [27] demonstrate smartwatch-based detection of
smaller, habitual micro-actions such as face scratching, nail biting,
and lip picking, providing personalized, just-in-time interventions.
While closely related in motivation, their gestures are voluntary
and context-isolated, whereas WristSense targets involuntary strain-
relief behaviors that emerge spontaneously during functional tasks
like typing or lifting. Therefore, smartwatch-based sensing remains
underexplored for ergonomic analysis. WristSense bridges this gap
by detecting both high-level tasks and transient discomfort behav-
iors passively and in real time using commodity wrist IMUs.
Language Models for Motion Feedback. Recent vision-language
models [16, 24] show promise for activity captioning but rely on

video input and high compute budgets. IMU–LLM pipelines are
emerging, yet prior work lacks temporal segmentation or struc-
tured tokenization. Frameworks like SensorLLM [30] and Sensor-
Chat [45] align long IMU sequences (5–10 s) with text using trans-
former encoders and large paired datasets, effectively modeling
macro-activities but require continuous motion context and ex-
tensive training to capture brief, non-repetitive micro-behaviors.
Mojito [39] uses a VQ-VAE-based inertial tokenizer trained on syn-
thetic data, but its latent tokens lack interpretability. In contrast,
WristSense performs event-level inertial tokenization, segmenting
short IMU windows into interpretable, biomechanically grounded
tokens for zero-shot, low-latency feedback on commodity devices,
offering higher temporal resolution, interpretability, and safety
control than global alignment approaches.
8 Conclusion
In this paper, we presented WristSense, a real-time, wrist-worn sens-
ing system that combines inertial data with large language model
reasoning to deliver personalized ergonomic feedback. Using only
smartwatch IMU signals, WristSense fuses compass-stabilized orien-
tation, sign-corrected wrist angles, and a hierarchical CNN–HMM
architecture to recognize functional and strain-related behaviors.
These are converted into structured tokens for GPT-4.1, enabling
ergonomic insights without handcrafted rules or retraining. The sys-
tem, deployed on both Apple and Samsung smartwatches, achieved
a macro F1 score of 0.91, surpassed prior baselines, and main-
tained posture estimation errors under 10◦. LLM-generated feed-
back aligned closely with clinical references (BERTScore: 0.89) and
drove significantly higher user compliance (76%) compared to rule-
based (23%) and no-feedback (5%) setups. A usability score of 85.2
further supports its real-world potential. Overall, WristSense demon-
strates the viability of integrating LLMs into real-time sensing
pipelines for contextual, personalized ergonomic monitoring.
