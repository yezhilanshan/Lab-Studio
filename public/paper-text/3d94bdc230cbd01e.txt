Spiking-UNet: Spiking Neural Networks for
Spectrum Occupancy Monitoring
Kosta Dakic†, Bassel Al Homssi⋆, and Akram Al-Hourani†
† School of Engineering, RMIT University, Melbourne, Australia
⋆ School of Engineering & Technology, UNSW Canberra, Canberra, Australia

Abstract—With the exponential growth of the Internet of
Things (IoT) landscape and the resulting spectrum congestion,
innovative techniques for spectrum monitoring are crucial. This
paper presents an approach to spectrum monitoring harnessing
the power of spiking neural networks (SNNs) with a focus on
image segmentation using the UNet architecture. Traditional
methods, including energy detection, have been widely used
but are not without challenges, especially in environments with
varying signal-to-noise ratios. In contrast, the presented SNN
approach in this paper demonstrates through simulations per-
formance metrics that significantly surpass energy detection
methods and closely align with conventional convolutional neu-
ral network techniques while also exhibiting favorable energy
efficiency. Future explorations will delve into enhancing the
framework using machine learning techniques for advanced
feature extraction and multi-class segmentation.
Index Terms—Spiking Neural Networks, Spectrum Sensing,
Machine Learning, Neuromorphic Computing, and Image Seg-
mentation
I. INTRODUCTION
The Internet of Things (IoT) has experienced rapid ex-
pansion, connecting billions of devices and enabling a wide
range of applications in numerous industries, including smart
cities, healthcare, and industrial automation [1]. Due to the
IoT networks’ quick proliferation, radio frequency spectrum
resources have further decreased in supply and are increasingly
congested [2]. With its dynamic spectrum access capabilities,
cognitive radio (CR) technology offers an attractive answer
to the problem of spectrum scarcity and enhances spectrum
usage in IoT environments.
To alleviate spectrum scarcity through CR, it is imperative
to sense the spectrum. This not only facilitates the optimal
utilization of resources by pinpointing unused or underutilized
spectrum bands but also plays a pivotal role in detecting
potential interferences. By doing so, it ensures uninterrupted
and seamless communication, preventing overlaps with other
active signals [3]. However, The cost of implementing spec-
trum sensing technologies can be high, especially for wideband
sensing due to the computational power needed. The algo-
rithms required for spectrum sensing can be complex, requir-
ing advanced signal processing techniques. Another challenge
is the possibility of false detection, i.e., sensing a channel as
free when it is actually occupied, or vice versa [3].
The main conventional methods of spectrum sensing that
are demonstrated in the literature include matched filter-based
sensing, spectral correlation-based sensing (cyclo-stationery),
radio identification-based sensing, and waveform-based sens-
ing [4]. Nevertheless, these conventional methods require
additional information about the signals such as the bandwidth,
modulation type, or the presence of a known pilot signal or
preamble. Energy detection-based sensing is the most common
approach due to its low computational complexity and ease
of implementation. However, its accuracy is heavily degraded
at low signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) [4]. This challenge has
encouraged researchers to look into machine learning and deep
learning to improve their performance for spectral sensing
relative to conventional methods.
Machine and deep learning have revolutionized fields such
as computer vision by enabling the creation of sophisticated
models that can recognize complex patterns and classify
objects [5]. These advances have been facilitated by the
power of data-driven approaches, neural networks (NN), and
various learning algorithms. With the success in these do-
mains, researchers have started to explore the potential of
machine learning to cluster signals in wideband spectrograms
for clearer signal boundaries [6], drawing bounding boxes to
localize user signals in the time-frequency domain [7], and
utilizing deep learning in the area of spectrum sensing [8]. By
employing similar techniques, it is possible to develop models
that can efficiently detect and classify signals, adapt to changes
in the radio environment, and handle the challenges associated
with radio spectrum analysis.
Nonetheless, the energy efficiency of these algorithms needs
to be further improved, particularly for low-power IoT devices
which must be energy-efficient to optimize battery life and
operational time [9]. The energy consumption of machine and
deep learning algorithms is primarily due to the high computa-
tional requirements associated with the training and inference
of the models. These algorithms often require a large number
of mathematical operations, memory access, and data move-
ment, all of which contribute to high energy consumption.
Enhancing energy efficiency is crucial for facilitating larger
networks and becomes increasingly vital in edge computing
environments, which include satellites, IoT devices, UAVs, etc.
Edge computing involves processing data closer to its source,
which is essential for real-time applications and reducing the
load on central servers. However, many edge devices, such
as satellites and UAVs, have strict energy constraints due to
their remote locations and the difficulty in replenishing their
energy sources [10]. To address the requirement of energy-

SNNs present an alternative approach to conventional deep

Mimicking the behavior of biological neurons, SNNs transmit
information using spikes or events, allowing for increased
energy efficiency and real-time processing capabilities, making
them a promising option for radio spectrum analysis applica-

integrates the principles of spiking neurons with the
robustness of the UNet model.
• Our approach showcases the Spiking-UNet architecture’s
efficacy, which not only maintains similar accuracy per-
formance to the conventional CNN UNet architecture but
also outperforms traditional energy detection methods.
• We showcase the power efficiency compared to the con-

II. SPIKING NEURAL NETWORKS
In recent years, deep learning has been extensively em-
ployed in wireless systems for various tasks such as signal
detection [12], [13], modulation classification [14], and nu-
merous other applications. However, these methods come at
the expense of increased power consumption, hardware costs,
and system complexity. SNNs have emerged as a valuable and
energy-efficient alternative. SNNs offer a distinct advantage
over traditional NNs due to their incorporation of spatiotem-
poral aspects in spike trains rather than solely utilizing single-
element vectors to convey weights and biases. This feature
enables SNNs to transmit significant amounts of information
using fewer neurons than generic NNs [15]. Consequently,
SNNs excel at handling real-time, continuous, and temporally
rich data streams, such as wireless signals [10]. Moreover,
SNNs are only activated upon spike generation or reception,
in contrast to conventional NNs, which require continuous
operation for every new input.
SNNs have been applied in numerous prior research endeav-
ors, such as in [16] for image segmentation. However, research
with SNNs related to wireless communication is in its infancy.
Nevertheless, some research work has been conducted for
implementing SNNs in telecommunications including mod-
ulation classification [17], [18], signal detection [19], [20],
and resource management [21]. In the aforementioned re-
search works relating to wireless communications, researchers
propose using SNNs to leverage the network’s low power
consumption and superior spatiotemporal data processing ca-
pabilities. A key advantage of SNN-based spectrum analysis is
to provide robust packet localization in the spectrum with in-
terference, anomalies, and high AWGN, all while maintaining
low power consumption.
The spiking neuron is heavily influenced by biologi-
cally plausible neuron models, such as the Hodgkin-Huxley
model [22]. Nonetheless, while the model offers detailed
biophysical mechanisms including ion channel dynamics, is
computationally intensive due to its intricate differential equa-
tions and multiple state variables. As a consequence of its
computational complexity, the more straightforward leaky
integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron model is often used instead
for SNN architectures. The LIF neuron model calculates the
sum of weighted input pulses, which are then integrated over
time with a specific exponential decay, or leakage. Fig.1
demonstrates that a membrane potential accumulates from the
input current Iin, and if the integrated value surpasses a chosen
threshold Vth, the LIF neuron emits a voltage spike κ(t). The
output κ and voltage V (t) relationship can be expressed as,
κ(t) = u (V (t) − Vth) , (1)
where u(.) represents the Heaviside step function. Upon
triggering a spike, the membrane potential should be reset.
One major issue of spiking-based networks is the non-
differentiability of spikes. The minimization of the loss func-
tion is not immediately solvable using conventional gradient-
based techniques because the spiking mechanism in (1) is
non-differentiable due to the inclusion of the threshold Vth.
To address this issue, surrogate gradients (SG) techniques
substitute the derivative of the threshold with an appropriate
differentiable estimate. For a more comprehensive understand-
ing of this method, we recommend the readers consult the
research work [23].

B. Proposed Spiking-Based Architecture
In this research, we adapt the renowned UNet architecture,
incorporating LIF neurons for our spiking-based approach.
Originally conceptualized as a CNN for image segmentation
tasks, the UNet has found significant application in biomedical
image segmentation [11]. The UNet architecture consists of
an encoder (downsampling) path and a decoder (upsampling)
path, forming its characteristic U-shape. This unique design
enables the network to harness both local and global contextual
information, ensuring precise image segmentation. The encod-
ing path features a sequence of convolutional and max-pooling
layers. Each block in this path integrates two convolutional
layers with rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation functions,
succeeded by a max-pooling layer to downsample. This design
extracts features from the input image, systematically reducing
its spatial dimensions.
The decoding path is in charge of upsampling the feature
maps acquired from the encoding path and merging them
with the high-resolution features from the encoding path
through skip connections. Every block in the decoding path
features an upsampling operation, succeeded by concatenation
with the corresponding feature map from the encoding path.
Subsequently, two convolutional layers with ReLU activation
functions are applied. The number of feature channels is
reduced by half at each upsampling step. Lastly, a 1×1
convolution is utilized at the final layer of the decoding path to
produce the segmentation map, which has the same number of
channels as the target classes. To convert the UNet architecture
to use LIF neurons, in addition to the convolutional layers for
feature extraction interleaved by a max pooling layer, we add
a LIF neuron layer, The architecture is illustrated in Fig. 2.

A. Energy Efficiency
One of the predominant merits of SNNs over conventional
neural networks is their potential for improved energy ef-
ficiency. This is relevant in applications requiring real-time
processing on resource-constrained devices or for minimizing
the power draw on large-scale deployments.
In Table I, we provide an analysis of the energy consump-
tion per inference between the CNN UNet and the proposed
SNN UNet. The energy is recorded using the KerasSpiking
Python power profiling package from Nengo [25]. The CNN
UNet is executed on a general-purpose GPU, whereas the SNN
UNet is run on the Loihi neuromorphic chip.
As can be observed, the SNN UNet yields substantial energy
savings, consuming an order of magnitude less energy per in-
ference than its CNN counterpart. Specifically, the SNN UNet
achieves a remarkable 10× reduction in energy consumption.
This underscores the energy-efficient nature of neuromorphic
hardware when coupled with SNNs, paving the way for their
potential deployment in scenarios where power efficiency is
paramount.

V. CONCLUSION
This paper presented a novel approach to spectrum monitor-
ing using image segmentation with SNNs, where it showcases
a notable image segmentation architecture called the UNet
network, adapted to an SNN architecture using LIF neurons.
The performance of the proposed method far outperforms
the ED method in terms of accuracy and precision metrics.
Furthermore, our method also closely follows the conventional
CNN UNet performance all while maintaining the inherent
benefits of the SNN. The advantages of the SNN relative to
the CNN include a much higher power efficiency, which we
experimentally demonstrated in this paper, real-time inference,
and advanced parallel processing. For future work, we plan
to extend our proposed framework to real-world data and
use machine learning techniques to extract relevant features
from the spectrum data, which can then be fed into the SNN
for better accuracy and multi-class segmentation for spectrum
sensing.
