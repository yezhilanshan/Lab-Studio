Learning Using Millimeter Waves

Action recognition using millimeter waves (mmWave) has shown great potential in various fields. However, the state-of-the-art
still falls short in terms of multi-person action recognition inclusive interactive action recognition. In this paper, we propose
mmMulti, a multi-person action recognition method based on multi-task learning using millimeter waves. To this end, we
first segregate the mmWave data and assign them to each of multiple persons, and propose two new input data—compressed
Doppler map (CDM) and point trajectory segments (PTS)—to represent the patterns and sequential characteristics of actions.
Next, we leverage ConvNeXt to extract pattern features from CDM and leverage Transformer to extract sequential features
from PTS, and fuse them by a cross-attention mechanism. Finally, we custom-design a multi-task learning model to recognize
independent and interactive actions from multiple concurrent persons, enabling mmMulti to recognize single-person actions,
multi-person independent actions and multi-person interactive actions. We implement mmMulti on a commercial mmWave
radar and conduct extensive experiments. mmMulti achieves single-person action recognition accuracy of 99.64%, independent
action recognition accuracy of 91.03% for two persons, 72.38% for three persons, 64.75% for four persons, and interactive
action recognition accuracy of 100% for two persons. To the best of our knowledge, mmMulti is the first work in the field of
mmWave sensing to differentiate both independent and interactive actions in multi-person scenarios, based on a multi-task
learning model to accomplish multiple tasks simultaneously.

1 Introduction
Millimeter-wave (mmWave) radars have been widely used in various fields due to their rich spectrum, large
bandwidth, and minimal interference. Applications include autonomous driving [16], explosive detection [24],
human detection [32], activity recognition [23], gesture recognition [10, 11], gait recognition [17], and vital sign
monitoring [27]. While vision-based methods [1, 20] provide higher granularity, they face privacy, lighting, and
shielding issues. WiFi sensing offers a low-cost, non-intrusive solution [12, 15], but struggles with subtle and
multi-person sensing due to its coarse resolution. In contrast, mmWave radars, with their high resolution and
minimal privacy concerns, show great potential for accurate sensing [10, 11, 17, 27].
Current RF sensing research focuses primarily on single-person sensing, while multi-person sensing remains a
challenge. Although mmWave radars provide new opportunities for multi-person sensing, action recognition
in such scenarios is limited. Several solutions have been proposed for localization [6] and tracking [30], but
multi-person action recognition is still unexplored. There are three primary challenges:
Challenge 1: The number of people and action modes (independent or interactive) are unknown in advance.
We define interactive actions as those involving mutual physical interactions between two or more individuals,
such as “high-five” and “shake hands”. Traditional methods rely on a two-stage process: counting individuals,
identifying the action mode, and then classifying the action, which results in increased computational complexity
and lower accuracy.
Challenge 2: In multi-person scenarios, mmWave signals overlap, causing difficulties in accurately segregating
data (e.g., point clouds and range Doppler maps) and assigning it to the correct individual.
Challenge 3: The limited number of points in mmWave point cloud data can lead to sparsity in multi-person
scenarios. Insufficient points hinder the accurate capture of action features, causing mis-recognition.
To address these challenges, we propose mmMulti, a method that recognizes simultaneous actions from
multiple persons using mmWave signals, with no prior knowledge of person count or action mode. Specifically,
mmMulti can automatically identify the number of people and the action mode, and recognize single-person
actions, multi-person independent actions, and interactive actions in one model.
Solution to challenge 1: We design a multi-task learning (MTL) model that performs three tasks: action mode
discrimination (including people counting), independent action recognition, and interactive action recognition.
The MTL model enables multi-person action recognition within a single model without prior knowledge of the
person count or action mode.
Solution to challenge 2: Accurate segregation of mmWave data is key to independent action recognition.
We accumulate point clouds from multiple frames to increase point density, then use the k-Means algorithm
to cluster the point clouds. Each cluster corresponds to a person, and we use these clusters to segment range
Doppler maps and assign sub-maps to individuals.
Solution to challenge 3: To handle sparse data, we fuse trajectory and Doppler features. We track points frame
by frame to create point trajectory segments (PTS), and compress 3D range Doppler maps into 2D compressed
Doppler maps. The fusion of these two features using a cross-attention mechanism improves recognition accuracy.
We evaluate mmMulti using a real-world dataset collected in our office building. Our self-collected dataset
shows that mmMulti achieves 98.49% accuracy in action mode discrimination, 99.64% in single-person action
recognition, 91.03% in two-person, 72.38% in three-person, 64.75% in four-person, and 100% in two-person
interactive action recognition. Our dataset is shared on GitHub1.
The main contributions of our paper are summarized as follows:
(1) Introduce point trajectory segments (PTS) as input, addressing the discontinuity of target trajectories and
retaining detailed trajectory information. Experiments show that PTS outperforms target trajectory.

2 Related work
2.1 Action recognition with mmWave
Action recognition is one of the most common applications of mmWave sensing. Wang et al. [29] built the
first mmWave gesture recognition system on Google’s Soli sensor. They used range Doppler images as the
features and constructed CNN-LSTM models to recognize dynamic gestures. Utilizing a pulsed mmWave radar,
Fhager et al. [5] leveraged range-time envelope images for hand gesture classification by tuning a pretrained
ResNet50. mHomeGes [10] achieved continuous arm gesture recognition in real-time using mmWave point
cloud data, via a hidden Markov model-based voting mechanism and a CNN, M-Gesture [11] focused on person-
independence, automatic segmentation and real-time recognition of gestures from continuous mmWave point
clouds, mmASL [21] could recognize American Sign Language (ASL). Using spatial spectrograms and a multi-
task deep learning model, it could learn the phonological properties of ASL signs and use them to accurately
recognize the ASL signs. Yu et al. [33] created spatial–temporal point clouds through a modified voxelization
approach and recognized human activities by a dual-view CNN. Zhang et al. [35] leveraged the Histogram of
Oriented Gradients (HOG) algorithm to extract features from range-profile images and achieved long-range
mmWave gesture recognition. Kang et al. [9] proposed a framework of spatial–temporal point cloud transformer
(ST-PCT) to realize activity recognition based on sequential point cloud from mmWave radar. Rodar [8] proposed
a multi-view de-interference Transformer network to achieve accurate recognition of similar gestures under
high-strength human activity interference. These works worked in single-person scenarios and did not consider
the multi-person issue.
2.2 Multi-person mmWave sensing
Compared to single-person sensing, researches on multi-person mmWave sensing are much less. mID [36, 37]
proved that by forming temporally associated trajectories from point clouds and integrating sophisticated neural
networks, it is possible to track and identify multiple individuals using mmWave. mmTrack [30] was the first
system that localized and tracked multiple users simultaneously using an mmWave radar. It first promoted
the spatial resolution by digital beamforming and then constructed human trajectories through the k-Means
clustering algorithm and the Hungarian matching algorithm. Chen et al. [3] developed a geometric model that
estimated the location of multipath ghosts and generated a reflection map that allowed for eliminating the
multipath and shadow ghost interference as well as augmenting weakly reflected human subjects in occluded
areas, thus achieved accurate multi-person tracking. To achieve multi-person gesture recognition, Wang et al. [28]
proposed a multidimensional analysis method to separate the gesture feature of each person from mixed signals,
by jointly leveraging the range, the angle, and the Doppler information. Multi-HAR [34] exploited the extended
Kalman filter-based group tracking algorithm to cluster and track each target in a multi-person scenario, and
combined 3D-CNN with LSTM to classify human activities in multi-person scenarios. MARS [22] employed an
intelligent model to sense both macro and micro activities and used a dynamic spatial time-sharing approach to
achieve continuous multi-user activity recognition. MARS relied on the point cloud data to localize the subjects
and used range Doppler images to recognize activities. These works have made progress for multi-person sensing.

However, they can not handle interactive actions and the recognition accuracy still needs improvement for
multiple persons.
2.3 Novelty of our work
Existing works on RF action recognition predominantly focus on single-person scenarios. Only a small num-
ber address multi-person scenarios. Even so, almost all of them neglect interactive actions, considering only
independent actions. Different from the previous, our work distinguishes itself in the following aspects.
(1) Existing methods are unable to distinguish between interactive actions and multi-person independent
actions, and rely on DBSCAN for people counting, which may lead to decrease in accuracy. mmMulti
takes a multi-task learning approach. It performs action mode discrimination (inclusive people counting),
single-person action recognition, multi-person independent action recognition and multi-person interactive
action recognition within the same framework. Through a gate-controlled multi-expert neural network,
mmMulti can achieve multi-person action recognition without knowing the count of individuals and the
action mode in advance.
(2) Existing methods either focus on Doppler map features or trajectory features, whereas our approach
combines both types of features. To enhance recognition accuracy while simultaneously reduce computa-
tional complexity, mmMulti introduces two data preprocessing techniques. One involves compressing 3D
sequential range Doppler maps into 2D compressed Doppler maps, and the other entails constructing point
trajectory segments instead of target trajectories to track actions.
3 Method overview
3.1 Overall structure
The objective of our method is to achieve multi-person action recognition, capable of recognizing both independent
and interactive actions. To achieve the goal, we design a multi-task learning framework, integrating the tasks of
action mode discrimination, people counting, independent action recognition, and interactive action recognition.
The overall structure of mmMulti is illustrated in Fig. 1.
Data acquisition: During the process of actions, the mmWave radar emits Frequency Modulated Continuous
Waves (FMCW) and captures the reflections. The emitted and the reflected signals are processed to produce frames
of point clouds and range Doppler maps. Each frame of point clouds contains the reflection points’ positions,
velocities, distances to the radar, and reflection intensities. Each range Doppler map depicts the targets’ dynamics
with respect to the distance and the velocity.
Data segregation: The point cloud data is first denoised by the Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications
with Noise (DBSCAN) algorithm to eliminate non-target reflections. In a multi-person independent scenario, the

Data preprocessing: The sequential range Doppler sub-maps of each individual are compressed on the velocity

individual undergoes preprocessing to obtain the point trajectory segments (PTS). CDM provides an overview of
the action pattern, while PTS details the action path.
Feature extraction and fusion: We employ the pretrained ConvNeXt [13] model to learn the spatial features
from compressed Doppler maps, and employ the Transformer [26] model to learn the temporal features from
point trajectory segments. The spatial and temporal features are fused by the cross-attention mechanism [7] and
passed to the multi-task recognition model.
Multi-task action recognition: To achieve multiple recognition tasks in one framework, we custom-design
a multi-task learning model, comprising a shared expert, multiple task-specific experts, multiple task-specific
classifiers, and multiple gates. Controlled by the gates, the features learned by the experts are filtered, weighted
and fused to accommodate the task-specific classifiers. In this way, the MTL model can realize specific action
recognition tasks according to the inputs and predict one of the three results: action mode with people count,
independent action type, or interactive action type.

sequences. The CDM and the PTS undergo feature extraction by ConvNeXt and Transformer respectively and are
fused by the cross-attention mechanism. The fused features are input to the multi-task learning model for action

4 Preprocessing of mmWave data
The signals emitted by the mmWave radar are FMCW. When the emitted FMCW signals reach an object, they are
reflected and received by the mmWave radar. By measuring the frequency difference between the emitted and the
received signals, the distance from the radar to the object can be calculated. Due to the movement of the object,
the frequency of the received signals may change, known as the Doppler effect. Analyzing the Doppler frequency
shifts allows for the calculation of the object’s velocity relative to the radar. This capability enables mmWave
radars to monitor not only the distance to the object but also the movement speed of the object. In this work, we
propose two data preprocessing methods to derive point trajectory segments and compressed Doppler maps.
4.1 Point trajectory segments
4.1.1 Point cloud. A point cloud contains a collection of points reflecting radar signals from objects, providing
detailed spatial information. Millimeter-wave radars capture surrounding information by converting raw signals

5 Multi-person action recognition
Multi-person action recognition methods typically require to discriminate the action mode and people count,
then recognize the actions through specialized neural networks according to the action mode. These two-step
multi-model approaches not only lack efficiency but also fail to share information between tasks. To mitigate
these problems, we propose a multi-task learning framework that can perform action mode discrimination and
action recognition simultaneously within one framework. By sharing one network, the key common information
is shared between different tasks.
The structure of the MTL framework is illustrated in Fig. 6. The framework takes Compressed Doppler Maps
(CDM) and Point Trajectory Segments (PTS) as input, extracts the features through the pretrained ConvNeXt [13]
and Transformer [26], and fuses the features through the cross-attention mechanism [26]. The fused features are
then used for multi-task learning and action recognition. The MTL model contains multiple experts, which are
controlled by the gates to determine the feature flows to the classifiers for decision-making.
5.1 Feature extraction and fusion
5.1.1 Design of feature extractors. To process compressed Doppler maps (CDMs) and point trajectory segments
(PTS), we design two feature extractors. For CDMs, treated as image data, we employ ConvNeXt [13] to extract
visual features for action recognition. As an advanced CNN integrating Transformer-like structures, ConvNeXt

5.2 Multi-task learning model
Multi-person action recognition involves multiple scenarios. To achieve high efficiency and high accuracy for
multi-scenario recognition, we leverage a multi-task learning model. We custom-design the tasks as action mode
discrimination, independent action recognition, and interactive action recognition. Through the task-flows in
Fig. 2, multi-person action recognition in different scenarios can be achieved. The key components in the MTL
model are mixture of experts and customized gate control.
5.2.1 Mixture of experts. We adopt a gate-controlled mixture of experts [25], comprising a shared expert and
multiple task-specific experts, each regulated by a gate. The shared expert captures common knowledge, while
task-specific experts focus on specialized knowledge. Gates adaptively select and weight expert contributions
based on input data, preventing conflicts and parameter interference [25]. As shown in Fig. 6(c), the shared expert
consists of 𝑚𝑠 sub-experts, each a neural network, to extract essential features across tasks. For multi-person
action recognition, we design task-specific experts for action mode discrimination, independent action recognition,
and interactive action recognition. Each consists of 𝑚𝑘 sub-experts, also structured as neural networks.
5.2.2 Customized gate control. Each task is regulated by a gate that selects and weights features from shared and
task-specific experts based on input, ensuring only relevant features reach the task-specific classifier. We employ
Customized Gate Control (CGC) [25], as shown in Fig. 6(d), where each classifier integrates knowledge from both
the shared and its corresponding task-specific expert. Since all experts contain multiple sub-experts, the gate
selectively combines them to refine feature selection. The gate is a single-layer feed-forward neural network

5.2.3 Multi-task recognition. After the features are selected and weighted through the gate control, they are
fused and fed into the corresponding task-specific classifier. Each classifier is a multi-layer neural network. Three
task-specific classifiers are designed to classify the action mode (classifier 1 in Fig. 6(a)), the independent action
(classifier 2), and the interactive action (classifier 3), respectively. The prediction of the action mode classifier
(classifier 1) controls the output of the other two classifiers. If it is single-person action, the independent action
classifier (classifier 2) outputs the single-person action type simultaneously. If it is multi-person interactive
action, the interactive action classifier (classifier 3) outputs the interactive action type simultaneously. If it is
multi-person independent action, the action mode classifier (classifier 1) outputs the people count at the same
time. Then the raw data is segregated and treated as multiple single-person action recognition tasks.
5.3 Loss function and training strategy

performance. To train the multi-task learning model, the loss function needs to accommodate the diverse tasks.
5.3.1 Task mask. We propose task masks to indicate the tasks of the samples for training, thereby masking out
the samples not applicable to specific tasks, ensuring that only the losses relevant to the associated tasks are
calculated. We define the task masks as:
𝑚𝑎𝑠𝑘 = [mode, independent, interactive] (19)
A valid task is marked as 1 and an inapplicable task is marked as 0. Thus:
• A single-person action sample is marked as [1, 1, 0], indicating that the loss is computed only for the action
mode discrimination task and the independent action recognition task, while the loss for the interactive
action recognition task is masked.
• A multi-person interactive action sample is marked as [1, 0, 1], indicating that the loss is computed only
for the action mode discrimination task and the interactive action recognition task, while the independent
action recognition task is masked.
• A multi-person independent action sample is marked as [1, 0, 0] before being segmented, indicating that
the loss is computed only for the action mode discrimination task, while the loss for the independent
action recognition task and the interactive recognition action task are masked. After being segmented into
multiple single-person independent actions, each segment is treated as a single-person action sample.

of these methods on action recognition. Table 3 shows the results, demonstrating that CDM achieved the best

6.6 Effect of input data
Our method exploits both CDM and PTS for action recognition. The fusion of them achieves superior effect than
either alone, as CDM focuses more on spatial patterns and PTS focuses more on sequential features. We made
experiments to compare CDM+PTS with CDM and PTS alone. The results are shown in Table 3. Fusing CDM and
PTS outperformed using CDM or PTS alone in all the cases. CDM achieved higher accuracy than PTS, indicating
that Doppler data were more effective than trajectory data in action recognition.
Existing works on mmWave sensing proposed some input processing methods. CPDP proposed in mHome-
Ges [10] compresses the temporal dimension of 3D Doppler images to enriched 2D compressed Doppler images.
PRM proposed in M-Gesture [11] expresses the input by extracting 10 features. Gesture trajectory time-sequence
(GTT) proposed in [31] extracts complete gesture trajectories that identify trajectory outlines. We compared our
method with them by experiments. The results are shown in Table 3. For all the cases, our method CDM+PTS
achieved the best performance. CDM alone achieved better accuracy than the other input data, and PTS enhanced
CDM to acquire more comprehensive insights.
6.7 Effect of feature extraction models

For CDM, we compared ConvNeXt with Residual Network (ResNet) and Visual Geometry Group (VGG). For
PTS, we compared Transformer with Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) and LSTM. The comparisons are shown in
Table 4. These models all achieved high accuracy for single-person and two-person interactive action recognition.
But notable discrepancies were observed for two-person independent action recognition. Our feature extractor
ConvNeXt+Transformer achieved the best performance, with slightly higher computational cost.
For CDM feature extraction, although ResNet and VGG excel in image data, they showed slight inadequacy
in recognizing subtle motions in CDM. ConvNeXt exhibited superior capability of capturing subtle motions
in CDM, albeit with slightly higher complexity. For PTS feature extraction, GRU and LSTM did not perform
satisfactorily in capturing long-term dependencies in action trajectories. Transformer could identify complex
action trajectories accurately, at the cost of higher complexity.

6.9 Effect of segmentation methods
Multi-person independent action recognition requires to segregate the point cloud data first and then perform
independent action recognition for each person. The choice of clustering method is crucial to the segregation,
which in turn affects action recognition. Point cloud data from multiple persons often overlap, especially when
the persons are close to each other. Density based clustering methods may produce incorrect number of clusters,
resulting in wrong estimation of people count. Hence, we chose and compared the clustering methods k-Means,
Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), Spectral clustering, and DBSCAN. The number of clusters was determined
by the action mode discriminator and specified as a hyper-parameter to the clustering methods except DBSCAN.
The comparison results are listed in Table 6, showing their effect on people counting and action recognition. The
k-Means method performed the best. The performance of the GMM method was slightly lower than k-Means.
Both outperformed the Spectral clustering method by a large margin. Although DBSCAN could automatically
count the number of clusters, it was prone to being affected by noise and had a relatively lower accuracy in
identifying the correct number of clusters, which ultimately degraded its performance.

6.10 Cross-individual and cross-site
6.10.1 Cross-individual. The users in the training set often differ from the users being recognized. To study how
different users affect recognition accuracy, we selected one user as new user for single-person action recognition,
using the other users as the training set. For two-person action recognition, we considered two scenarios: one
user was selected as new user (i.e. only one person in the action being new) and two users were selected as new
users (i.e. both individuals in the action being new), using the other users as the training set. As shown in Table 7,
new users led to a drop in recognition accuracy. For single-person action recognition, new user caused a decrease
of about 4% in accuracy. For two-person interactive actions, one new user led to some decrease in accuracy, but
when two new users were introduced, the accuracy droped significantly. For two-person independent actions, the
impact of new users was also considerable. This was because new users often exhibited significant differences in
body size, height, and motion style, leading to inaccuracies in recognition results.
6.10.2 Cross-site. To evaluate the impact of different environments on recognition accuracy, we tested our model
in three indoor sites. We selected one or two sites as the training set and took the remaining one as the test set. In
Table 8, we summarized the impact of new sites on recognition performance. It can be observed that the new sites
had a relatively small impact on recognition accuracy. Our model demonstrated good robustness in new sites.
6.11 Time costs
We employed an NVIDIA 3090 GPU to train the method end-to-end. The key components include PTS generation,
CDM generation, ConvNeXt, Transformer, and MTL model. Generation of a PTS sample took 0.037s, generation of
a CDM sample took 0.025s. During the end-to-end training process, training of the ConvNeXt model took 486.241s,
training of the Transformer took 587.169s, and training of the MTL model took 235.261s. During the recognition
process, a feature extraction by the ConvNeXt model took 0.039s, a feature extraction by the Transformer took
0.087s, and a classification by the MTL model took 0.027s. The time cost of the rest components is trivial. Overall,
the whole training time of the method was 1708.371s. The recognition time of a single-person action was 0.154s,
an interactive action was 0.162s, and a recognition of two-person independent actions took 0.351s. Both the
training time and the testing time are acceptable for practical deployment.

7 Discussions
Number of individuals: Theoretically, the method can work for any number of people. But in practice, it is
limited by the capacity of the mmWave radar. In our experiments, we used the IWR1443BOOST mmWave radar,
which could provide up to 64 points per frame in the point cloud data. It worked accurately for one person. When
being applied to two persons, the points were distributed between the individuals, causing the points to be sparser
for each person, hence the accuracy decreased. We accumulated the data points in multiple frames and fused PTS
and CDM to alleviate the problem of data sparsity. The accuracy could reach above 90% for two persons. When
more people were present, the points assigned to each person would become too sparse to recognize the actions
accurately. To investigate the issue, we collected independent action samples for up to 4 concurrent persons and
conducted multi-person independent action recognition. The results with respect to person counts are illustrated
in Fig. 12(a). The evaluations demonstrated that more people decreased the recognition accuracy, as the data
points assigned to each person became sparser.
Distance: We exploit clustering methods to separate the points of multiple individuals in point clouds. The
accuracy of multi-person action recognition depends on the effectiveness of clustering. For precise segregation, a
minimum distance must be kept between the individuals. To verify it, we collected data from two persons at
different inter-individual distances from 30cm to 120cm, and conducted experiments to observe the impact. The
results are shown in Fig. 12(b). Our method achieved accurate recognition when the inter-individual distance
exceeded 60cm for independent action recognition. Below this threshold, the data points overlapped, complicating
the separation and reducing the number of valid points, which decreased the recognition accuracy. The distances
from the individuals to the mmWave radar also affect recognition performance. To verify it, we collected data
from two persons at different distances to the mmWave radar, and conducted experiments to observe the impact.
The results are illustrated in Fig. 12(b). When the distance to the mmWave radar increased from 30cm to 120cm,
the recognition accuracy decreased from 94% to 87%.
Undefined actions: For the experiments, we predefined some independent actions and interactive actions,
which could be extended by collecting the corresponding samples. During the recognition process, if an undefined
action occurs, the method can categorize it as “undefined” through feature extraction and clustering. The features
of undefined actions differ from those of predefined actions, hence they are clustered as outliers. To demonstrate,
we input the undefined actions “box” and “stand up” into the feature extractor, and fed the extracted vectors
into T-SNE for clustering. As shown in Fig. 13, the undefined actions (“box”, “stand up”) could be effectively
distinguished from previously trained ones (“L”, “O”, “S”, “V”, “W”, “Z”).

Complex action scenarios: Currently, the method discriminates the actions as independent actions or interac-
tive actions, and performs action recognition with corresponding strategies. For more complex action scenarios,
for example, some individuals perform independent actions and some perform interactive actions, simultaneously,
our method can not handle it yet. Such scenarios require high-resolution mmWave radar. By segregating the
points iteratively, the complex actions can be recognized. Due to the limitation of our mmWave radar, we did not
conduct such experiments.
Cross-domain generalization: Cross-individual and cross-environment are important issues for practical
deployment of such systems. Although our method demonstrated a certain level of robustness, we observed that
an increase in the number of individuals might lead to generalization errors. Our cross-individual experiments
showed that one new user caused an accuracy drop of about 4%, while two new users caused an accuracy drop
of about 10%, for independent and interactive action recognition. The reason lies in the sparsity of data points
and differences in body size and motion style between individuals. When deploying our method in completely
new environments, we observed that the new environments had relatively small influence on the recognition
performance. Our cross-site experiments showed that new environments introduced an accuracy drop of about
2% for single-person and interactive action recognition and an accuracy drop of about 3% for independent action
recognition. Our method was robust to new environments, but could be influenced by new individuals. To
solve the cross-domain generalization issue, cross-individual in particular, we intend to adopt a few potential
solutions in the future. One is to augment point cloud data, ensuring that a greater number of points are available.
Meta-learning and domain adaptation/generalization techniques will be leveraged to improve the generalization
performance in new domains. Moreover, better hardware will be considered for enhancing the performance.
Despite the limitations, our approach holds significant practical values and scalability. It is well-suited for
applications in various fields, such as smart home, where the ability to recognize and track multiple users
seamlessly can provide tremendous benefits. With improvements in hardware and integration of advanced
techniques, we are confident that our method can be further refined to meet the requirements of more complex
real-world scenarios.
8 Conclusions
Multi-person action recognition has been a challenge for RF sensing. Owing to the high resolution of mmWave
FMCW technology, multi-person mmWave sensing has become possible. In this paper, we make an attempt to
Proc. ACM Interact. Mob. Wearable Ubiquitous Technol., Vol. 9, No. 2, Article 64. Publication date: June 2025.

accomplish multi-person action recognition using a single millimeter-wave radar. Our method mmMulti is capable
of recognizing single-person actions, multi-person interactive actions, and multi-person independent actions,
without knowing the number of individuals and the action mode in advance, by exploiting a gate-controlled
multi-task model to integrate all these tasks in one model. To ensure proper recognition accuracy for multiple
simultaneous actions, we fuse the Doppler information and the trajectory information. We propose the input
feature CDM to represent the Doppler information of actions and adopt ConvNeXt to extract the pattern features.
We propose the input feature PTS to represent the trajectory information of actions and adopt Transformer to
extract the sequential features. Both features are fused with cross-attention, which proves effective to ensure the
recognition accuracy under the condition of sparse data points due to multiple persons. Through experimental
evaluations with real-world dataset, our work proves the feasibility of fusing different feature modalities to
achieve multiple sensing tasks simultaneously in one model, which lays a foundation for multi-modal multi-task
learning in wireless sensing in complex scenarios.
