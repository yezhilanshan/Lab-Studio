A B S T R A C T
Online Action Segmentation (OAS) aims to recognize and segment human actions from continuous video
streams in real time. The absence of future context makes OAS vulnerable to local ambiguity and over-
segmentation, as most existing methods treat it as a reactive pattern recognition problem over past features.
We argue that robust online understanding can benefit from a predictive view that leverages predicted future
dynamics as logical priors for current decisions. To this end, we propose Predictive Action Logic (PAL), which
reformulates OAS as a predict-verify process. Specifically, PAL predicts short-term motion trends to capture the
dynamic invariants underlying action logic, and jointly leverages these predictions with past observations to
infer the current action. To ensure the semantic faithfulness of the predicted future, we introduce Asymmetric
Self-Distillation that characterizes the dynamic invariants of actions while filtering task-irrelevant details. On
this basis, we design a temporal calibrator to align the current frame representation with the past and predicted
future representations, thereby mitigating local ambiguities and promoting temporally consistent recognition.
Experiments on multiple benchmarks show that PAL is particularly effective at improving segment-level
temporal consistency.
1. Introduction
Online Action Segmentation (OAS) aims to recognize and segment
human actions from continuous video streams in real time. In contrast
to offline protocols (Ahn and Lee, 2021; Behrmann et al., 2022; Kumar
et al., 2022), which leverage the complete video and global context,
OAS is restricted to processing incoming frames sequentially and mak-
ing predictions using only past observations. This causal constraint,
coupled with the need for immediate response, makes OAS particularly
suitable for real-world applications such as autonomous driving and
intelligent surveillance, where systems are required to react promptly
to ongoing behaviors rather than waiting for an event to conclude.
However, the absence of future context fundamentally constrains
the understanding of ongoing actions, making the model vulnerable to
local ambiguity and prone to boundary misprediction, leading to frag-
mented and unreliable outputs. To address this, existing OAS models
primarily rely on reusing or enhancing historical frames, commonly
through architectures such as memory mechanisms (Zhong et al., 2024)
or task-graph-based modeling (Shen and Elhamifar, 2024). More re-
cently, prototype-based approaches (Han et al., 2025) have emerged,
attempting to augment current feature representations by retrieving
static behavior prototypes that encode global class-level commonali-
ties. Despite their effectiveness, a significant performance gap remains

Drawing on this theoretical foundation, we propose the Predictive
Action Logic (PAL) framework to address the limitations of reactive
OAS models. PAL shifts the paradigm from retrospective recognition
to a predict–verify mechanism, where future prediction serves as a
critical regularization to disambiguate current observations. Specifi-
cally, before classifying the action at the current moment, PAL predicts
short-term future motion trends to capture the dynamic invariants
underpinning action logic, then integrates this predicted future with
the observed past to yield a temporally coherent interpretation of
the current frame. A critical challenge in this predictive paradigm
is ensuring that predictions rely on genuine action dynamics rather
than irrelevant nuisances. Video data contains two primary sources of
interference: static redundancy (backgrounds) and dynamic noise (non-
target motion). To rigorously address these, PAL introduces a two-stage
filtering mechanism within its Future Motion Prediction module. First,
to eliminate static redundancy, we employ explicit motion trend model-
ing via first-order differencing, forcing the model to focus on temporal
variations. Second, to strip away dynamic noise entangled with task-
relevant motion, we propose Asymmetric Self-Distillation (ASD). Unlike
standard mutual information methods that assume global alignment,
ASD enforces a granular consistency constraint. It leverages a subset-
based matching mechanism to align the predicted future representation
with its specific supporting past context. This effectively filters out
stochastic dynamic noise that lacks temporal grounding. Finally, a
Temporal Calibration Module aligns these purified future priors with
historical data to infer the current action.
In summary, the main contributions of this paper are as follows:
• We propose the Predictive Action Logic (PAL) framework for on-
line action segmentation. This framework implements a ‘‘predict-
verify’’ mechanism to transition the model from being reactive to
predictive, addressing the over-segmentation challenge.
• We introduce Asymmetric Self-Distillation (ASD) to decouple dy-
namic invariants from noise. By enforcing consistency between
predicted future trends and their supporting past contexts, ASD
encourages the model to learn robust action logic.
• We design a Temporal Calibration Module (TCM) that enforces
‘‘past-present-future’’ consistency, effectively resolving local am-
biguities by integrating purified future priors with historical ob-
servations.
• Extensive experiments on multiple benchmarks show that our
framework improves segment-level metrics on several bench-
marks, demonstrating the effectiveness of our proposed frame-
work.
2. Related works
2.1. Action segmentation
The task of temporal action segmentation can be broadly divided
into three categories based on the level of supervision employed:
unsupervised (Kumar et al., 2022; Sarfraz et al., 2021; Shen et al.,
2021; Tran et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022; Zhukov et al., 2019), weakly
supervised (Chang et al., 2019; Ding and Xu, 2018; Li and Todorovic,
2020, 2021; Li et al., 2019), and fully supervised approaches (Ahn and
Lee, 2021; Behrmann et al., 2022; Farha and Gall, 2019; Huang et al.,
2020; Ishikawa et al., 2021; Kuehne et al., 2016). However, the vast
majority of existing methods focus on addressing action segmentation
in offline settings, and significant progress has been achieved in this
domain. Nonetheless, offline methods (Ahn and Lee, 2021; Behrmann
et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2022) necessitate prior access to the en-
tire video sequence, thereby limiting their applicability in real-world
scenarios that demand real-time responsiveness.
In the task of online action segmentation, which is constrained
to leveraging only past and current information, existing fully super-
vised methods have achieved notable success. For instance, ProTAS
(Shen and Elhamifar, 2024) introduces causal constraints, action pro-
gression prediction, and task graphs to enhance stability and logical
consistency. Meanwhile, OnlineTAS (Zhong et al., 2024) strengthens
temporal modeling and corrects over-segmentation through context
enhancement, adaptive memory, and post-processing. More recently,
to mitigate the absence of future context, (Han et al., 2025) proposed
Behavior-Centered Feature Augmentation (BCFA), which utilizes be-
havior prototypes to capture global action semantics and enhances
the intrinsic representation of the currently observed behavior to re-
duce reliance on local context. However, the underlying paradigm of
these methods remains largely retrospective or static-focused. They
either rely heavily on historical information to infer the current state
or attempt to compensate for the lack of future context by anchor-
ing on pre-learned prototypes, often necessitating post-hoc correction
mechanisms.
Conversely, we argue that action coherence arises not only from
historical context but also from anticipating future events. We therefore
advocate for integrating both retrospective and prospective perspec-
tives to better capture the internal logic of human actions.
2.2. Online action detection/anticipation
Online action detection (Eun et al., 2020; Qu et al., 2020; Shou
et al., 2018; Gao et al., 2019) identifies actions in video streams in
real-time, restricted to historical and present information like online
action segmentation. Action anticipation forecasts future actions from
limited observations, including short-term (Kong et al., 2018; Girase
et al., 2023) and long-term predictions (Gong et al., 2022; Wang et al.,
2021). Early methods employed recurrent neural networks (Chen et al.,
2022; De Geest and Tuytelaars, 2018; Li et al., 2016; Furnari and
Farinella, 2019; Gammulle et al., 2019; Kong et al., 2018) for temporal
modeling. However, Transformers have gained dominance in vision
(Dosovitskiy et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021) and video tasks (Chen
et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022) via attention mechanisms. Consequently,
Transformer-based models (Cao et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2022; Rangrej
et al., 2023; Girase et al., 2023; Gong et al., 2022; Wang et al.,
2023) now prevail for online detection and anticipation. These tasks
share similar architectures and complementary temporal modeling ca-
pabilities, leading to recent integration efforts. For example, Wang
et al. (2021) proposed OadTR, where an encoder captures historical
dependencies and a decoder aggregates predicted future segments to
recognize current actions. Kim et al. (2021) introduced FATSNet, which
achieves temporally coherent, frame-level predictions by aggregating
features from both historical and anticipated future frames.

3.2. Overview
To address the local ambiguity resulting from the purely reactive
nature of existing OAS methods, the proposed Predictive Action Logic
(PAL) framework recognizes actions by constructing a temporally co-
herent context through a predict–verify mechanism, which integrates
both past observations and predicted future cues. As illustrated in Fig.
2, PAL consists of four sequential stages, where frame features are first
projected into the hidden dimension 𝐷𝐻 and subsequently processed
by our two proposed modules:
Future Motion Prediction (FMP). This module predicts short-
term future motion trends to guide recognition of the current frame.
To ensure faithfulness, it sequentially applies frame differencing and
asymmetric self-distillation to remove static redundancy and dynamic
noise, respectively, thereby obtaining future features that capture the
dynamic invariants underlying the action logic.
Temporal Calibration Module (TCM). This module enforces tem-
poral coherence by aligning past observations with predicted future
cues, allowing the model to focus on dynamic invariants that effectively
resolve local ambiguities.
3.3. Future motion prediction
This module is motivated by the predictive mechanism of the human
brain, which anticipates potential future actions and leverages these
predictions to support current recognition. This property motivates
leveraging past observations together with predicted future cues, rather
than interpreting each frame in isolation. To this end, the module filters
static and dynamic redundancy while refining the predictive signals,
yielding a minimal sufficient description of the future motion trend.

4. Experiments
4.1. Datasets
We evaluate PAL on six datasets in this study. 50Salads (Stein
and McKenna, 2013) contains 50 salad preparation videos with 17
action categories, averaging 6 min in duration and approximately 20
action instances per video. Breakfast (Kuehne et al., 2014) includes
1712 breakfast preparation videos across 18 kitchens, with 48 actions
and approximately 6 instances per video. EgoProceL (Bansal et al.,
2022) comprises 1055 videos featuring various tasks (e.g., car repair,
cooking), which contain 130 action categories and approximately 21
segments per video. EgoPER (Lee et al., 2024) consists of 386 videos
that feature users performing five procedural tasks. GTEA (Fathi et al.,
2011) consists of 28 videos, featuring 11 actions and an average of
33 segments per video. Cholec80 (Twinanda et al., 2016) contains
80 laparoscopic cholecystectomy videos annotated with seven surgical
phases. Following the commonly used 40/40 split of Cholec80, we used
the first 40 videos for training and the remaining 40 videos for testing.
4.2. Evaluation metrics
We evaluate all methods using mean frame accuracy (Acc.), edit
distance, and the segmental F1 score. The F1 score is computed at
temporal Intersection-over-Union (tIoU) thresholds of 10%, 25%, and
50%. Specifically, Acc. is defined as the proportion of frames cor-
rectly predicted by the model and is used to measure the frame-wise
classification performance. The edit distance quantifies the similarity
between two segment sequences by calculating the minimum number
of insertion, deletion, and substitution operations required to convert
one segment sequence into the other, thereby evaluating the struc-
tural correctness of the predicted action sequence. F1 score evaluates
the intersection over union between each predicted segment and the
corresponding ground truth annotation, based on a given threshold. A
predicted segment is considered a true positive only if its IoU with a
ground truth segment exceeds the threshold and it is the first correct
match within the same ground truth action region. We report both
frame-wise and segment-level metrics because they capture different
aspects of online segmentation: Acc. reflects instantaneous classifica-
tion correctness, whereas Edit and F1 are more sensitive to fragmented
boundaries and over-segmentation.
Since Cholec80 is primarily used in surgical phase recognition stud-
ies, we adopt the relaxed evaluation protocol commonly used in this
literature rather than unrelaxed evaluation metrics, to ensure com-
parability with the methods reported in Table 4. The relaxed evalu-
ation metrics are denoted as R-Accuracy, R-Precision, R-Recall, and
R-Jaccard. Under this protocol, a prediction is considered correct if it
falls within a 10-second temporal window before or after a surgical
phase transition and the predicted class corresponds to an adjacent
surgical phase.
4.3. Implementation details
For the standard OAS benchmarks, we follow prior studies and use
pre-extracted 2048-dimensional I3D (Carreira and Zisserman, 2017)
features as the main input representation for fair comparison with I3D-
based baselines. To examine whether PAL benefits from stronger visual
features, we also evaluate it with V-JEPA 2.1 (Mur-Labadia et al., 2026)
ViT-g/16 features, whose output dimension is 1408. For Cholec80,
we follow GDPA (Wang et al., 2025) and common surgical workflow
recognition practice by using CLIP ViT-B/16 to extract 768-dimensional
visual features.
Our model is composed of four stages, each containing 10 dilated
convolution blocks. To ensure causality and real-time performance, the
first stage performs initial spatiotemporal aggregation following the
approach of ProTAS (Shen and Elhamifar, 2024). The subsequent three
stages progressively introduce our proposed future motion prediction
and temporal calibration module. The hidden dimension 𝐷𝐻 is set to
64. In the future motion prediction, for each frame 𝑡, the differential
window 𝑀 is set to 32, and the number of query tokens 𝑁 is set to
4. The value of 𝐾 in Asymmetric Self-Distillation (ASD) is set to 8.
We train the model for 150 epochs using the AdamW optimizer with a
learning rate of 0.0005. The loss weights are configured as follows: the
weight 𝜆1 of the main classification loss is set to 1.0, the weight 𝜆2 of
the smoothness loss to 0.15, and the weight 𝜆3 of the ASD loss to 0.3.

Table 1 presents the model’s performance on large-scale egocentric
datasets. PAL shows its clearest advantage on EgoProceL. We attribute
this behavior to the procedural nature of EgoProceL: videos contain
diverse tasks, many action categories, and long action chains with
distinct procedural structures. In such scenarios, local visual features
tend to be ambiguous, while short-term future motion trends act as
effective priors to clarify the logic of current actions. However, on
the EgoPER dataset, BCFA achieves the best results. This suggests
that prototype-based behavior augmentation remains effective when
the dataset contains a relatively small number of recurring procedural
tasks and its class-level behavior prototypes are stable. Nevertheless,
the gap between PAL and BCFA is small, indicating that PAL remains
competitive on EgoPER.
The results on the 50Salads and Breakfast datasets in Table 2 reveal
a different behavior pattern. OnlineTAS achieves slightly higher frame-
wise accuracy, whereas PAL performs better in terms of Edit and F1
scores. This indicates a trade-off between instantaneous frame-wise
classification and segment-level temporal coherence. PAL can sup-
press short, isolated label changes caused by local ambiguity, thereby
improving Edit and F1 scores by reducing fragmented segments. There-
fore, PAL is more suitable for applications where stable action intervals
and reliable boundary structures are more important than maximizing
the number of correctly classified individual frames, such as procedural
monitoring and step-level assistance.
Table 3 summarizes the model performance on the GTEA dataset.
PAL achieves the best result only on F1@50. This is because GTEA is
relatively small and contains short actions with frequent transitions,
making the future trends predicted from a limited historical window
unstable, while temporal calibration may also over-smooth rapid action
changes. Therefore, although PAL can improve the strict F1@50 metric
by reducing fragmented segments, it does not perform best on metrics
that are more sensitive to coarse boundary localization or frame-wise
classification. This indicates that PAL is more suitable for mitigat-
ing over-segmentation through predictive temporal consistency, rather
than for scenarios with extremely short actions or limited training data.
We further report PAL with V-JEPA features as a feature robustness
setting. Across all five datasets, PAL+V-JEPA consistently improves
segment-level metrics. These gains are particularly evident in segment-
level metrics such as Edit and F1@50, indicating that stronger self-
supervised video representations can provide more reliable motion
cues for future trend prediction and temporal calibration. These re-
sults further reveal a division of labor between representation learning
and temporal reasoning: V-JEPA provides stronger motion-aware fea-
tures, making the predicted future trends less affected by appearance
noise and local ambiguity; PAL then converts these cleaner trends into
temporal priors for current-frame calibration.

4.6. Ablation study
In this section, we validate each specific design in our approach via
a thorough ablation study. All ablation experiments are carried out on
the 50Salads dataset.
4.6.1. Effects of predicted future
To investigate the impact of the future prediction mechanism on
model performance, we conduct the experiments summarized in
Table 5:
(1) M1 removes all components for future feature generation and
utilization from the PAL framework. This configuration yields the poor-
est performance, indicating that the absence of contextual information
makes model decision-making more challenging.
(2) M2 retains the future motion prediction module but removes
the temporal calibration module, meaning the predicted future is not
used to enhance current frame recognition. M2 shows significant im-
provement compared to M1. This indicates that even without using the
predictions for inference, the ASD in future motion prediction acts as
a regularizer, forcing the model to internalize underlying action logic,
which benefits inference.
(3) M3 replaces the predicted future trend features with real future
visual features, allowing the model to observe 𝑁 future frames directly.
M3 outperforms PAL by 8.9 percentage points in terms of F1@50.
However, PAL outperforms M1, which does not predict the future, by
14 percentage points in terms of F1@50. This suggests the predicted
future trend features can, to a certain extent, replace real visual features
in providing context for model reasoning.
4.6.2. Effects of motion trend
Table 6 validates the necessity of our two-stage motion modeling.
Predicting future trends directly from raw features (Row 1) yields the
poorest F1@50, as the model overfits to static redundancy. Notably,
applying only First-order Differencing causes a slight accuracy drop.
We attribute this to the fact that while differencing removes static
backgrounds, it inadvertently amplifies high-frequency stochastic noise
(e.g., camera jitter). Asymmetric Self-Distillation is thus critical; it acts
as a purifier that extracts consistent action logic from these variations.
The optimal performance in Row 4 confirms the synergy: RSR isolates
temporal variations, while RDN filters the resulting dynamic noise,
yielding the best trade-off between sensitivity and stability.

4.6.4. Ablation on future prediction methods
We investigated different methods for generating future motion
trend features, as shown in Table 8. The convolutional pooling method
was inferior to LSTM with pooling, indicating that effectively learning
temporal features is crucial. Our proposed approach, which uses an
attention mechanism and query tokens, improved performance further.
This is attributable to two factors: on the one hand, the attention
mechanism adaptively selects dynamic patterns most relevant to the
progression of the current action; on the other hand, the query token
actively retrieves the most probable future motion directions from
past trends via cross-attention, rather than depending on a predefined
temporal aggregation method.
4.6.5. Ablation on past and future context
This experiment investigates the required lengths for the historical
feature window 𝑀 and the future prediction window 𝑁 to classify the
present frame. As shown in Fig. 4, we evaluated different configurations
of 𝑀 and 𝑁. When 𝑀 = 8, all performance metrics are relatively low.
This suggests that insufficient context hinders both the generation of
reasonable future representations and the accuracy of present frame
classification. The model achieves the highest F1@50 score at 𝑀 =
32 and 𝑁 = 4. As 𝑁 decreases, performance drops, suggesting that
too little future information cannot provide sufficient contextual cues.
Conversely, as 𝑁 increases, performance also degrades. This implies
that predicting too far into the future yields unstable feature represen-
tations that cannot provide a coherent motion logic for classification.
Overall, performance at 𝑀 = 64 is comparable to that at 𝑀 = 32.
This indicates that an excessively long context window is unnecessary,
as it not only increases computational load but also may introduce
redundant information.

4.8. Streaming video test
To validate the practicality of PAL in real-world deployments, we
simulate a strictly online inference setting where video features are
streamed frame-by-frame on the EgoProceL dataset. Table 10 illustrates
the performance dynamics among the purely reactive baseline (M1),
the causal-graph-based ProTAS, and our proposed PAL. The integration
of the Future Motion Prediction and Temporal Calibration modules
inevitably introduces additional computational demands. Specifically,
PAL requires 2924.52 MFLOPs per frame and incurs a latency of
0.0446 s, resulting in an inference speed of 22.43 FPS. Compared to
ProTAS, this represents a marginal 4.8% increase in computational
footprint and a 4.3-ms increase in per-frame latency.
Crucially, this additional computation improves segmentation sta-
bility. PAL achieves an F1@50 score of 48.8%, outperforming the
baseline M1 by 30.2 percentage points and ProTAS2 by 12.0 percent-
age points. While M1 operates at the highest throughput, its severe
boundary mispredictions render it inadequate for precision-critical pro-
cedural tasks. Ultimately, while PAL trades a fraction of inference
speed for improved temporal coherence, its 22.43 FPS throughput
remains well within the acceptable real-time operational bounds for
most online video understanding systems. This demonstrates an optimal
trade-off that prioritizes semantic fidelity and temporal coherence with-
out breaking the latency constraints required for ongoing behavioral
analysis.

5. Conclusion
In this paper, we introduce Predictive Action Logic (PAL), a new
framework that reformulates online action segmentation as a predict-
verify process. PAL explicitly predicts short-term future motion trends
as logical priors for inferring the current action, thereby reducing local
ambiguity and the risk of over-segmentation. To ensure that the pre-
dicted future remains semantically accurate, we propose Asymmetric
Self-Distillation, which filters out task-irrelevant noise while preserving
9
T. Xu, R. Zhang, X. Tian et al. Computer Vision and Image Understanding 271 (2026) 104891
the dynamic invariants that are essential for action logic. Based on this,
the Temporal Calibration Module aligns representations across the past,
present, and predicted future, with a focus on the temporal coherence
of actions at the feature level. Experiments on six datasets show that
PAL is effective at improving segment-level metrics such as Edit and
F1, especially in procedural videos with strong temporal regularities.
