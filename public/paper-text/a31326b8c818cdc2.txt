Abstract
Spiking neural networks face hardware limitations as conventional architectures exhibit low array
utilization, underperforming GPU-driven artificial neural networks in vision tasks. We present a
programmable spiking neurocomputing architecture using CMOS-compatible photonic reconfigurable
devices that unify synaptic/neuronal functions in single components. The optical isolation of photonic
reconfigurable devices suppresses inter-cell crosstalk while enabling independent neuronal/synaptic
programmability without operational redundancy. Programmable spiking neurocomputing architecture
maximizes array utilization to enhance computational efficiency for spiking neural networks acceleration.
Compared with conventional architectures, our architecture on spiking Visual Geometry Group networks
demonstrates 1176× latency reduction and 239× energy savings in static recognition/classification and
dynamic detection/tracking tasks, while maintaining equivalent recognition accuracy. This photonic-
electronic integration establishes a scalable hardware framework that bridges the performance gap between
spiking neural networks and state-of-the-art artificial neural networks, particularly for complex visual
information processing applications requiring both efficiency and precision.

Introduction
Intelligent chatbots driven by artificial neural networks (ANNs) have gained significant global
popularity, accelerating and refining the world’s development from art to science1-2
. Within the
frenzied competition, the number of parameters involved in various large language models has
exploded by four orders of magnitudes3
since 2019 (Supplementary Fig. 1a), resulting in higher
computability demand and electric consumption4
. Spiking Neural Network (SNN) is a powerful
platform for enabling low-power edge computing5-7
, owing to its event-driven nature and ability
to process temporal information. Recently, endeavors8,9
based on emerging memory have been
made to experimentally demonstrate hardware-level applications based on fully connected SNNs
(FCSNNs), including static image recognition and classification10-14
. With the advancement of
dynamic cameras and autonomous driving technologies, there has been an urgent demand for high-
speed, cost-effective object detection and tracking in edge computing15-19
. However, researchers
generally suggest that FCSNNs are inadequate for detection and tracking tasks20,21
. On the other
hand, the performance of existing FCSNNs hardware still lags behind that of state-of-the-art ANNs
based on high-performance graphics processing units (GPUs), tensor processing units (TPUs), and
supercomputers, particularly in challenging vision tasks, which limits the further application of
SNNs.
Convolutional Spiking Neural Networks (CSNNs) have seen significant development by
combining the convolutional layer’s power of extracting spatial-temporal features with the energy
efficiency of spiking neuron layers, which is effective in addressing high-level sensory tasks
required by real-world scenarios, such as dynamic or mobile recognition and tracking22,23
.
However, existing SNN hardware architecture would limit the speed significantly regarding CSNN
implementation. The reason mainly pertains to the lack of an efficient array utilization. Firstly,
conventional SNN architecture consists of one neuron and one synapse module11-13
(Fig. 1a),
separated arrays controlled by channel switches generate redundant data and cause delays in signal
processing. Secondly, networks operate in a fully connected way due to the crossbar
interconnection. Then one line of synapses can only serve one neuron, otherwise, the input signal
crosstalk would lead to the failure of weight update on the same line. Thirdly, the convolutional
operation in CSNNs needs to slide over different input patches, which would output numerous vast
feature maps for challenging vision tasks. Then, a much larger synapse array is necessary to match

decreasing the array utilization and wasting the hardware computability.
Some emerging material platforms, such as memristors, ferroelectric, organic and two-

Unfortunately, the crossbar array controls the working states of individual cells through input
signals applied to bit lines and word lines, which would inevitably introduce signal interference
across multiple cells within the array, resulting in the working modes of existing reconfigurable
devices not being able to be freely edited during CSHA training. Consequently, this fundamental
limitation obstructs the resolution of low array utilization bottlenecks at their source. To fill this
gap, flexible programming of arbitrary nodes in the array is the root of increased utilization. In this
scenario, it is logical to seek reconfigurable devices that can resist such crosstalk, further fully
driving the hardware to implement SNNs.
Notably, while conventional CNN accelerators have addressed array utilization challenges through
optimizations of dataflow (e.g., weight reuse30
, tiling31
), calculation (e.g., systolic array32
, partial
convolution33
) and device (e.g., CMOS-based device34
), these strategies primarily target digital
array and cannot resolve the analog array underutilization inherent to SNN hardware. Unlike
CNNs, which rely on dense spatial features and static activation functions (e.g., ReLU), SNNs
demand dynamic resource allocation to exploit temporal sparsity and event-driven computation.
This divergence necessitates a fundamentally different optimization paradigm-array utilization
through hardware-level reconfigurability rather than algorithmic or architectural tuning.
In this work, we propose and demonstrate programmable spiking neurocomputing architecture
(PSNA) driven by photonic reconfigurable device (PRD) array, providing an efficient array
utilization to accelerate SNNs (including FCSNNs, sparse fully connected SNNs (SFCSNNs), and
CSNNs). The PRD proposed here can be dynamically assigned as either synapses or neurons
through programmed input signals, enabling the signal transfer between synapses and neurons
independently, therefore suppressing crosstalk between cells (Fig. 1b). To demonstrate the
versatility of the proposed PSNA to accelerate SNNs, we present incremental computing tasks,

Results
Compact PSNA for accelerating SNNs
The CSHA has been widely adopted for SNNs hardware, with numerous studies reporting its
superior performance in FCSNNs. As shown in Fig. 2a, CSHA connects two separate arrays-a
synaptic array and a neuron array-via a high-speed I/O interface. The synaptic array employs fully
connected array designs to compute weighted currents based on the weights between neurons and
input spikes. The weighted currents are then transmitted through the high-speed I/O interface to
the neuron array, where they activate output neurons. However, CSHA has notable limitations
when handling complex visual tasks, particularly when using advanced convolutional spiking
architectures to process large feature sizes. Since each output feature point corresponds to only a
small portion of the input region that needs computation, this fully connected design results in a
significant number of ineffective synapses, wasting computational resources. For instance, as
illustrated in Fig. 2c, when processing a 200×200 image during the first layer of convolutional
spiking computation, approximately 99.97% of the synapses connected to each neuron are
ineffective. Additionally, the separation of the two arrays introduces transmission delays. To
address these issues, we propose the PSNA, as shown in Fig. 2b. PSNA integrates the two arrays
into one, with each computing node capable of assuming two types. Critically, the PSNA
architecture enables dynamic reconfiguration of the computation graph at runtime, allowing each
node to be programmed as either a synapse or a neuron based on input signal types. This eliminates
the need for fixed hardware partitions and enables adaptive resource allocation according to the
spatiotemporal sparsity of SNN computations. A type control circuit dynamically adjusts the type
of each computing node, allowing us to maximize the utilization of on-chip computing nodes
according to different network computation requirements. This approach can even achieve
multiple-fold acceleration within the same number of computing nodes. Furthermore, the single-
array structure offers high integration and low-latency transmission capabilities.

To highlight the advance of the proposed PSNA, we compared its basic principles over CSHA in
FCSNN, SFCSNN, and CSNN, respectively. For FCSNN, all input signals are sequentially written
into the synapses, and following multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations, which are transmitted to
the neurons. Therefore, the node utilization rate within the array remains at 100% for both CSHA
and PSNA (Fig. 2d). However, pruning is utilized to simplify the inference and further reduce
redundant computations for SFCSNNs. The connection between synapses and neurons is sparsely
and irregularly distributed, leading to the invalidation of some synaptic nodes for CSHA, which
depends on the sparsity of the network (Fig. 2e). For PSNA, the positions of neuron nodes are re-
defined by collecting the number of synapses necessary for each neuron to achieve compact
integration and maximize the array utilization. For example, for neuron 1, only three synapses are
needed to realize MAC. Considering that the synaptic array is separated from the neuronal array
in CSHA, only three units of synapses are involved in the computation, and all the other synapses
are invalid, resulting in limited array utilization. PSNA defines the first three nodes as the synapse,
and the fourth node as the neuron to minimize the number of cells required for a single neuron,
thereby maximizing array utilization (50% to 75%). For CSNNs, feature maps generated by
convolution operations would require a much larger synapse array with very few nodes activated
(Fig. 2f). As a result, inference with CSHA at the CSNNs hardware level fails to fully utilize the
computability, and the inference speed is greatly lowered. Within this frame, PSNA can improve
array utilization (0.88% to 76.59%) of the CSNN to a greater extent than SFCSNN and effectively
solve the bottleneck that CSHA is currently facing.

Biological neurons are essential to the SNNs application, which are composed of three primary
components: inputs (dendrites), a processing unit (the soma, which includes the neuronal
membrane and spike-generation mechanism), and outputs (axons responsible for generating and
transmitting action potentials)38

properties of input signals. Upon exceeding a specific membrane potential threshold, they generate
a discrete electrical signal and Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) behavior. The intensity and duration
of the spike encode the temporal characteristics of the input, reflecting the dynamic processing
capabilities of biological neural systems. Here, LIF behavior was simulated by applying
consecutive electrical pulses to the PRD (pulse width of 100 ns, amplitudes of 4.5 V), where the
firing threshold was set as 5.6 pA, as shown in Fig. 3d. The mechanism underlying the LIF
behavior can be attributed to electric field-induced phase transitions in the HZO. Initially, several

Methods
1. Bottleneck of the implementation of CSNN with CSHA
In CSNNs applied to tasks such as Object detection, the convolution operation involves a sliding
window—referred to as the convolution kernel—that traverses the input data. At each location, it
performs local connectivity and weighted summation operations. Due to the intrinsic nature of the
convolution operation, only the synaptic weights within the current coverage area of the kernel are
relevant to the input and thus participate in computations. Specifically, within a two-dimensional
convolution layer, if the kernel size is K×K, then at any given time point, solely these K×K
synapses process the current input information. The remaining synapses outside this region do not
contribute to the computation at that moment because of the locality of connections inherent to
convolution: each neuron connects only to a local patch of the input space rather than the entire
domain. To illustrate in Supplementary Fig. 1b, an image is partitioned into four regions labeled
A, B, C, and D, where regions A, B, and C match the kernel's dimensions. When the kernel
overlays region A, only the synapses connected to A are active, while those linked to regions B,
C, and D remain inactive, merely conducting currents, with the output generated by neuron 1. A
similar situation occurs when the kernel overlays regions B and C. Consequently, this implies that
in the traditional arrays designed for fully connected SNNs (FCSNNs), the transition to CSNN
computations results in a significant number of unused synaptic devices, leading to suboptimal
utilization of the array resources.
2. Device fabrication
The PRD array was fabricated through a back-end process after utilizing a standard 0.18 μm Si
CMOS process in a commercial foundry. The stacks were integrated into the drain terminals of the
n-type transistors. The fabrication processes of the PRD (TiN/TiO2/ HZO/TiO2/ITO) are as
follows: First, a 50-nm TiN bottom electrode (BE) is sputtered on the drain of the transistors arrays,
followed by the 1-nm bottom TiO2 grown by plasma-enhanced atomic layer deposition (PEALD)
at 270 ℃. Then a 6-nm AFE Hf0.2Zr0.8O2 was grown by PEALD at 270 ℃. The TDMAHf,
TDMAZr, and oxygen plasma were used as hafnium precursor, Zr precursor, and oxygen source,

3. Motivation for the device structure design
The functional layers of the PRD cells consist of CMOS-compatible AFE HZO/TiO2 films. The
film is more practical to be integrated into traditional semiconductor manufacturing processes
compared to existing basic neuromorphic devices. Previous research has revealed that the gradual
polarization switching properties and intrinsic polarization/depolarization enable AFE HZO films
to serve as one of the promising elements for artificial spiking neurons without the requirement
for any capacitors or complicated circuits. Nonetheless, the near zero residual polarization (Pr)
naturally positions it far from the application for nonvolatile storage and synaptic weight updating
operations. Such an issue can be fixed by adding optical mode into AFE HZO-based devices to
obtain reconfigurable properties.
Owning to the wide bandgap of HZO (>5.0 eV), the optical response has long been considered
nearly impractical. Fortunately, TiO2 is a natural photosensitive material, which is compatible with
AFE HZO films. By meticulously crafting the thickness and stacking order of the two
(Supplementary Fig. 3a, b, and c), photogenerated excess carriers from TiO2 can tunnel through
the HZO layer to form optical nonvolatile behavior. This method could address the long-standing
unresolved issue, i.e., how to make a device to own both volatile and nonvolatile properties. On
the other hand, it was confirmed that the AFE property can be significantly enhanced by
introducing TiO2 to AFE HZO to form a heterojunction (Supplementary Fig. 3d-i). The bottom
TiO2 layer can enable the PRD to achieve a more symmetric coercive electric field and better
endurance. Furthermore, the optical/electrical dual mode modulation enables the construction of
reversible reconfigurable devices. Here, the volatile LIF neuronal function mainly originates from
the AFE HZO film. The non-volatile long-term potentiation (LTP) synaptic function is attributed
to the TiO2 film, which also serves as a photoreceptor.

7. Dynamic Array Mapping Algorithm for PSNA
The pseudocode (Supplementary Table 3) presents the core algorithm for optimizing the placement
of neurons within a single word line (WL) of the PRD array. The algorithm addresses the
fundamental limitation of CSHA, where one WL can only serve one neuron regardless of actual
connection count, resulting in significant "floating devices" (idle PRDs) during computation.
The dynamic array mapping algorithm implements a greedy optimization strategy that
significantly enhances array utilization by intelligently mapping SNN structures onto the PRD
array. (1) The algorithm maintains a set of occupied input that tracks which input features have
already been utilized by neurons placed on the current WL. When evaluating a candidate neuron,
it checks whether any of the neuron's required input features (including neuron) overlap with
already occupied features. Only neurons with completely non-overlapping input requirements are
considered for placement. (2) After placing a neuron, the algorithm reevaluates all remaining
neurons for potential placement on the same WL. This iterative approach continues until no more
neurons can fit on the current WL, ensuring maximum space utilization. (3) The algorithm selects
the neuron that provides the best balance between connection count and space utilization at each
step. This prevents the common pitfall of either leaving large gaps (by placing only small neurons)
or prematurely blocking space (by placing only large neurons).

9. Experimental DMD-PRD reconfiguration platform.
We constructed a benchtop optoelectronic testbed to validate PRD array reconfiguration
(Supplementary Fig. 12). A 405 nm laser (coherent illumination, ~2 mW/mm² intensity) was
expanded and directed onto the F9120 DMD system (DLPC910+DLP6500, 1920×1080 resolution,
up to 10,764 Hz binary frame rate), which projected binary or grayscale patterns onto the fabricated
PRD chip via a 4f imaging system (demagnification factor ~10×). Electrical probing provided
readout currents, while synchronized DMD frames controlled optical stimuli. In a 16×16 sub-array,
selective optical addressing achieved >98.7% mode-switching accuracy across cells, with no
observable crosstalk in neighboring devices, confirming the feasibility of large-scale PRD arrays
for PSNA.”
10. Simulation and Modeling
To validate the modeling, we simulated a small 3-layer SNN (input: 784, hidden: 100
synapses/neurons, output: 10) using the PRD compact model, achieving 91.8% MNIST accuracy.
Neuron initialization sets Vth via pulse width (50-200 ns), independent of firing execution, which
adheres to the physical LIF equation with <3% threshold variation under fixed pulses.

Editor’s Summary
Hybrid neural networks often underperform compared to conventional neural networks because of their low array
utilization. Lu et al. propose a programmable spiking architecture that leverages photonic reconfigurable devices to
integrate synaptic and neuronal functions without compromising performance.
