Energy-Efficient Wireless Technology Recognition
Method Using Time-Frequency Feature Fusion
Spiking Neural Networks
Lifan Hu, Graduate Student Member, IEEE, Yu Wang , Member, IEEE,
Xue Fu , Graduate Student Member, IEEE, Lantu Guo , Yun Lin , Senior Member, IEEE,
and Guan Gui , Fellow, IEEE
Abstract—Wireless Technology Recognition (WTR) distin-
guishes different wireless technologies by analyzing characteristic
features extracted from radio signals. While deep learning (DL)-
based methods are extensively used in WTR due to their ability
to extract hidden data features and make accurate classification
decisions, their application is often limited by excessive power
consumption. In this paper, we propose a novel WTR method that
addresses this challenge using a time-frequency feature fusion
spiking neural networks (TFSNN) framework. Our approach
combines information from both the time and frequency domains
to enhance feature extraction. Experimental results demonstrate
that our model performs exceptionally well at high signal-to-noise
ratios on open-source datasets. Specifically, at a sampling rate of
15 Msps, our method achieves a recognition accuracy of 99.85%.
Even when the sampling rate is reduced to 10 Msps, the average
accuracy remains 1.61% higher than the best existing method.
Additionally, our method reduces energy consumption by about
half compared to most current methods. These results emphasize
the effectiveness and necessity of time-frequency domain feature
fusion (TFSF) in WTR.
Index Terms—Spiking neural networks, attention mechanism,
wireless technology recognition, large-scale real-world dataset,
low power.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Input Formats of Wireless Technology Recognition
The inputs in existing WTR methods feature extractors
can be classified into three categories concerning the input
data format: 1) time domain features, 2) frequency domain
features, and 3) time-frequency domain features. The fol-
lowing describes the WTR method from the above three
aspects.
1) Time Domain Features: Time features, including ampli-
tude/phase (A/P), in-phase/quadrature (I/Q) and received
signal strength identifier (RSSI), offer a direct representa-
tion of a signal and can be easily acquired. Huang et al.
[34] used time-domain features such as I/Q vectors and A/P
vectors to train CNN classifiers by a semi-supervised
approach. Camelo et al. [33] used raw I/Q samples as input
and trained the model in a semi-supervised method through
a deep autoencoder (DAE) for WTR. Girmay et al. [14] used
two formats of time domain features as input, I/Q and RSSI,
training CNN to classify signals of six wireless technologies.
Yang et al. [16] discussed the LTE-U/WiFi coexistence system
model, where I/Q and A/P data are used as input for signal
recognition classification using CNN. In this paper, the authors
also introduced phase offset (PO) into the input data to
examine the effect of PO on the recognition performance of
LTE-U and WiFi signals.
2) Frequency Domain Features: Compared to time-domain
features, frequency-domain features are more robust in dealing
with multipath propagation and spectral interference. They
reveal stable spectral usage patterns and the energy distri-
bution of the signal. Fontaine et al. [22] used FFT data as
input, proposing a low-cost CNN solution for WTR tasks
in the sub-GHz band. Maglogiannis et al. [17] trained CNN
using FFT data for coordinated coexistence in the unlicensed
spectrum by recognizing LTE and WiFi. Rajendran et al.
[62] explored the combination of average-amplitude FFT
processing and LSTM modeling with a distributed sensor
architecture. The results show that the proposed method can
classify DVB, GSM, LTE, Radar, Tetra and WFM signals.
The authors of the above papers mapped the time-domain
data into frequency-domain representation via FFT. The results
show that models trained on FFT data perform better in terms
of accuracy in low SNR scenarios compared to time-domain
features.
3) Time-Frequency Domain Features: Time-frequency
domain features can provide comprehensive information about
the signal in time and frequency domain, which enhances the
ability to identify and analyze dynamic and non-stationary
signal characteristics. Fontaine et al. [15] used FFT and
I/Q samples to generate spectrograms as input to recog-
nize wireless technologies such as WiFi, LTE and DVB-T
by image-based CNN. Bhatti et al. [35] employed CNN
to recognize ten signal classes, which include LTE, Radar,
WiFi, filter bank multicarrier (FBMC) and their combinations
such as LTE+Radar, LTE+WiFi, FBMC+Radar, FBMC+WiFi,
WiFi+Radar, and Noise. The input to these CNN models
is a novel spectrogram representation termed the Quarter-
spectrogram (Q-spectrogram), which compresses time and
frequency information to fit the CNN model. Xie et al. [36]
proposed a framework (PRCL)based on contrastive learn-
ing, which not only solves the problems of prototype shift
and fragmentary negative distribution, but also improves
the robustness of the model through time-frequency feature
fusion.
The three classifications outlined above show the vari-
ous input formats employed by the DL-based WTR feature
extractor. Generally, incorporating frequency domain analysis,
such as FFT or STFT, can significantly enhance recognition
accuracy in low SNR scenarios. However, in high SNR condi-
tions, time-domain feature samples alone could achieve high
recognition performance, rendering additional processes like
FFT and spectrogram generation unnecessary.

B. Deep Learning for Wireless Technology Recognition
Inspired by the latest advancements and remarkable suc-
cesses of deep learning, particularly convolutional neural
networks (CNN), in a wide range of problems such as
object recognition, image segmentation and speech recog-
nition [50], many similar approaches have recently been
applied to enhance the performance of wireless technology
recognition task. Almazroueiet et al. [37] proposed a novel
deep learning-based method to classify various wireless tech-
nologies, including LTE and four of the most recent WiFi
standards. By employing a combination of denoising autoen-
coders (DAE) and CNN, this method significantly improves
computational efficiency and accuracy, even in the face of
adverse channel conditions. Experimental results show that the
classifier achieved up to 100% classification accuracy, with an
average accuracy of 91% across all propagation conditions.
Gao et al. [38] proposed a method that employs a convolutional
long short-term memory deep neural network (CLDNN) for
WTR. This method outperforms traditional energy detectors
as it can effectively detect signals without requiring prior
knowledge of the channel conditions. Zhang et al. [21] devel-
oped a deep neural networks (DNN) structure that intricately
fuses the robust feature extraction capabilities of CNN with
the temporal pattern recognition strengths of recurrent neural
networks (RNN).
To validate the efficacy of this hybrid model design, they
generated WiFi, LTE, and 5G NR signals utilizing the Matlab
communication toolbox. The receiver employed the DNN
to process the incoming I/Q samples for signal classifica-
tion, bypassing the traditional decoding step. Notably, the
incorporation of frequency domain analysis into the model
significantly bolstered its performance, with the classification
accuracy of the algorithm increasing an average of approxi-
mately 14%. Xu et al. [39] addressed the wireless interference
signal recognition (WISR) challenge in WLAN by deploy-
ing an enhanced quadruple generative adversarial network
(Improved-QGAN). Within the deep learning framework, the
authors proposed a collaborative learning mechanism that
replaces the original generative adversarial network (GAN)
with an auxiliary classifier GAN (ACGAN) to enhance the
information exchange between sub-networks and further opti-
mize the loss functions for the representation (R), classification
(C), and generative (G) sub-networks. Experimental result
shows that this method improves performance and effectively
reduces the model’s complexity.
Deep learning has a wide range of applications in wireless
signal recognition, and these advances fully demonstrate the
strong potential and significant effectiveness of DL-based
methods in dealing with WTR problems.
C. Applications of Spiking Neural Networks
As the next generation of neural networks, the spiking neu-
ral networks (SNN) are inspired by the neural dynamics of the
human brain. It follows an event-driven mechanism to realize
energy-efficient communication, and SNN has developed a
wide range of applications in various fields due to their energy-
efficient properties [28].
Yan et al. [43] presented an energy-efficient method
for electrocardiogram (ECG) classification that employs
SNN, targeting the effective processing of heart signals on
energy-constrained platforms, such as wearable devices. This
approach holds promise for facilitating real-time heart disease
detection. Feng et al. [44] constructed and trained a 14-layer
SNN model specifically for ECG classification, marking it the
most complex SNN model dedicated to ECG-related tasks. The
model is initially an artificial neural network (ANN) equipped
with ReLU activation functions; after conversion to an SNN, it
maintains a high level of performance. Remarkably, the trans-
formed SNN model demonstrated an impressive accuracy rate
of 84.41%. Saw and Wong [45] proposed a hybrid framework
that integrates CNN with stochastic reservoir (SR) to enhance
human activity recognition (HAR) using WiFi channel state
information (CSI). This innovative approach combines the
strengths of CNN and SR to create a HAR system that is robust
to noise, capable of handling individual and environmental
variations, and has low computational cost. Khan et al. [46]
proposed a novel federated learning framework, combining
SNN with long short-term memory networks (LSTM) to create
a privacy-preserving and energy-efficient solution for HAR.
The proposed spiking-LSTM (S-LSTM), uses the efficiency
of SNN and the robust sequence modeling capabilities of
LSTM. The experimental evaluation on two public datasets
shows that the proposed S-LSTM model surpasses LSTM,
CNN, and S-CNN models in terms of accuracy and energy
efficiency. SNN is also increasingly being explored in various
wireless communication domains. Jiang et al. [40] proposed an
ultra-low power radio frequency fingerprinting identification
(RFFI) system that leverages SNN. Using optimized spiking
neurons, this system attains high identification accuracy while
requiring only a minimal number of spikes. Due to its energy
efficiency advantages, the RFFI system is well-suited for
extending to other satellite communication systems with lim-
ited power availability. Henderson et al. [41] proposed spiking
convolutional neural networks (SCNN) for radar waveform
recognition, which is specifically designed to enhance the effi-
ciency and accuracy of low probability of intercept (LPI) radar
signal detection. Using the unique properties of SNN offers
a significant improvement over traditional techniques. The
experimental results show that the proposed SCNN dramat-
ically reduces model size and energy consumption, reducing
three orders of magnitude compared to traditional non-spiking
models. This pioneering study represents the first spike-based
implementation of an LPI radar waveform recognition system.
Safa et al. [42] investigated the application of SNN for
radar-based gesture recognition tasks, emphasizing the critical
role of preprocessing in enhancing SNN performance. This
research presents an effective solution for ultra-low-power,
radar-based gesture recognition, particularly for extreme-edge
devices.

IV. OUR PROPOSED TFSNN-BASED WTR METHOD
In this section, we present the proposed TFSNN-based
WTR method across five sub-sections. The first sub-section
provides an overview of the framework, detailing the functions
and benefits of each component within the TFSNN-based
WTR method. The second sub-section discusses the time-
frequency sample-level feature fusion (TFSF) implemented
using an attention mechanism. The third sub-section explores
the principles of SNN, including the spiking neurons, the
leaky-integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron model, and the surrogate
gradient method. The fourth sub-section describes the method
used for calculating energy. The final sub-section outlines the
training and testing procedures for the proposed TFSNN-based
method.
A. The Overview of TFSNN-Based WTR Method
As shown in Fig. 3, our proposed TFSNN-based WTR
method consists of three parts: sample-level feature fusion,
feature extraction, and feature classifier. First, the preprocessed
signals go through the FFT transformation. The I/Q and
FFT data reflect the features of the signal in the time and
frequency domain, respectively. Then, the raw I/Q data and
the transformed FFT data are all fused into a feature fusion
module. The attention mechanism for time-frequency sample
fusion inspired by AFF [47] focuses on more discriminative
data and compresses the less informative ones. Next, the
fused samples are fed into a feature extractor containing
convolutional layers, batch normalization layers, LIF spike
neurons, and max pooling layers. Finally, the features are
processed by a classifier composed of a fully connected (FC)
layer and a Softmax function.
B. Time-Frequency Sample-Level Feature Fusion (TFSF)
Feature fusion is a fundamental concept that combines
multiple types of features or information from different sources
to enhance the representational ability. By integrating comple-
mentary features, feature fusion can capture richer semantic
information, mitigate the limitations of single-source features,
and improve the robustness and generalization ability of a
model. For example, Zhao et al. [48] employs the multi-scale
fusion unit (MSFU) to integrate local details and global con-
textual information, providing more reliable feature support
for subsequent instance-aware tasks such as position prediction
and segmentation. Similarly, Jianget al. [49] designed the dual-
flow semantic consistency (DFSC) method, which leverages
class-level semantic modulation (CSM) and instance-level
semantic modulation (ISM) to achieve cross-sequence fea-
ture fusion. This method significantly enhances the trackers
generalization ability to the UAV category and improves its
precision in distinguishing the current target.
The attention mechanism is a natural extension of fea-
ture fusion, as it allows the model to dynamically assign
weights to features based on their relevance to the task.
Instead of treating all features equally, the attention mechanism
selectively emphasizes the most informative features while
suppressing irrelevant or noisy ones. This ensures that the
fused representation is not only richer but also more focused,
reducing redundancy and improving efficiency.
Attention mechanisms can be categorized into channel
attention, spatial attention, temporal attention, and branching
attention. In visual tasks, the attention mechanism functions
as a dynamic selection process, adaptively weighting features
according to their relevance to the input [50], [51], [52].
In deep neural networks, different channels usually represent
different objects in the feature map. The channel attention
mechanism plays a significant role by recalibrating the weights
of each channel adaptively. This process effectively operates
as an objective selection method, deciding which channels
of information merit emphasis based on their relevance to
the task at hand. Since not every kind of input data has the
same influence on the recognition result. Therefore, adding
an attention mechanism to focus on a more informative input

C. Spiking Neural Networks (SNN)
As illustrated in Fig. 3, the fused sample datasets are fed
into our designed SNN module after the TFSF module. Such
SNN structure has found use in EdgeMap and human activity
recognition (HAR) [25], [26]. Similar to those tasks, the
signals used for WTR are also time series data. Thus, SNN
could naturally capture the temporal correlation and dynamic
modes of these signals. Here we employ the framework of [53]
as shown in Fig. 3, consisting of three convolutional layers and
one fully connected layer.
In the SNN architecture, three convolution layers extract
features from the input samples. Following each convolution
layer, a batch normalization layer is employed. Unlike in a
standard CNN, the batch normalization layer in an SNN is not
followed by an activation function but by a LIF spike neuron.
This design is intended to simulate the dynamic behaviors
and spiking mechanisms found in biological neurons. During
forward propagation, these neurons update their membrane
potentials. A spike is emitted when the potential reaches a
specific threshold, resulting in an adjustment of the membrane
potential. During backward propagation, the model parameters
are optimized based on the performance metrics. Additionally,
each convolution layer is followed by a pooling layer that
performs max pooling to reduce the spatial dimensions of the
feature maps.
The classification phase of the model comprises a fully
connected layer followed by a softmax classifier. Initially,
the inputs to the classification component are flattened to
transform them into a one-dimensional vector. This vector is
then fed into the fully connected layer, which integrates the
extracted features and prepares them for classification. The
output of the fully connected layer is subsequently passed to
the softmax classifier. The softmax classifier is responsible

reliability of the reported performance, we conducted a 5-fold

recognition accuracy when the sampling rate is increased to
20 Msps.
When the SNR is low, TFSNN does not perform favorably.
For example, when the sampling rate is 20 Msps and the SNR

in practical wireless communication scenarios. In real-world
environments, techniques such as transmission power control,
signal amplification, and other reliability-enhancing mecha-
nisms typically maintain SNR levels above 0 dB [57], [58],
[59], [60]. Under these more realistic wireless communication
conditions, TFSNN demonstrates strong performance. At 0
dB, it achieves recognition accuracies of 74.46%, 89.16%,
and 93.27% for sampling rates of 10 Msps, 15 Msps, and 20
Msps, respectively. These results not only outperform baseline
methods but are also sufficient to meet the demands of many
real-world application scenarios.

H. Impact of Input Feature Resolutions
In this paper, the feature dimensionality of the input fea-
tures are determined by two key parameters in the time and
frequency domains: the time domain sampling rate and the
number of FFT points in the frequency domain. The time-
domain sampling rate represents the number of sampling
points captured per second, which determines the feature
resolution of the input signal on the time axis; while the
number of FFT points represents the number of discrete points
when converting the time-domain signal to the frequency-
domain signal, which determines the feature resolution of the
spectral representation.
The experimental results show that increasing the sampling
rate significantly enhances the model’s recognition perfor-
mance. As shown in Fig. 9, when the sampling rate is
increased from 10 Msps to 20 Msps, the average recognition
accuracy increases from 70.48% to 81.83%. This indicates
that higher sampling rates capture richer temporal information,
enabling the model to better extract and distinguish signal
features. However, the results also reveal diminishing returns

in performance improvement as the sampling rate increases.
For instance, increasing the sampling rate from 10 Msps to 15
Msps yields an 8.51% improvement in accuracy, while further
increasing it from 15 Msps to 20 Msps results in only a 3.6%
gain. This suggests that the contribution of additional temporal
detail to performance gradually saturates once the sampling
rate exceeds a certain threshold.
At the same sampling rate (10 Msps), increasing the number
of FFT points further improves model performance. Experi-
mental results indicate that using the sequence length as the
number of FFT points achieves the best recognition accuracy.
However, it is worth noting that the impact of the time-domain
sampling rate on model performance is more significant than
that of the frequency-domain feature feature resolutions. This
highlights the critical role of temporal information in extract-
ing and distinguishing signal features effectively.

more universal features, enabling it to maintain high recog-
nition performance across diverse environments. Additionally,
the techniques developed in this work have the potential to
be extended to other domains, such as radar signal recogni-
tion. Exploring cross-domain applications could open up new
opportunities and further expand the impact of the proposed
method.
