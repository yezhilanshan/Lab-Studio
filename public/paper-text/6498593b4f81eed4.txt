Abstract
Transformers significantly raise the performance limits
across various tasks, spurring research into integrating
them into spiking neural networks. However, a notable
performance gap remains between existing spiking Trans-
formers and their artificial neural network counterparts.
Here, we first analyze the cause of this gap and attribute
it to the dot product’s ineffectiveness in measuring similar-
ity between spiking queries and keys, due to numerous non-
spiking events. To address this, we propose a novel α-XNOR
similarity measure tailored for spike trains. It redefines the
correlation between non-spike pairs as a specific value α,
effectively overcoming the limitations of dot-product simi-
larity. Furthermore, considering the sparse nature of spike
trains where spikes carry more information than non-spikes,
the α-XNOR similarity correspondingly highlights the dis-
tinct importance of spikes over non-spikes. Extensive ex-
periments demonstrate that α-XNOR similarity significantly
improves performance across different spiking Transformer
architectures on various static and neuromorphic datasets,
further revealing the potential of spiking Transformers.
1. Introduction
Transformer in Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) [10, 11,
31, 38] offers an effective method for processing long se-
quences in parallel, significantly improving performance in
various tasks [6, 10, 16, 23, 37]. However, its reliance on
large-scale parameters [31] and the complexity of its self-
attention mechanism [52] result in high energy consump-
tion and hardware costs [18, 41]. Consequently, developing
energy-efficient and high-performance Transformer [3, 24]
remains a prominent research focus.
Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) [17, 26, 32, 33], as
the third generation of neural networks, gain significant
attention due to their superior energy efficiency [33, 42].

Spiking neurons fire only when activated and remain silent
in others. This event-driven mechanism [5] greatly en-
hances energy efficiency by promoting sparse synaptic op-
erations and avoiding multiply-accumulate (MAC) compu-
tations [7, 34, 49]. Recently, numerous researchers focus
on developing spiking Transformer models, such as Spik-

performance gap remains compared to their ANNs’ coun-

gap to the unsuitability of dot-product similarity for the bi-
nary nature of spike trains in existing spiking Transformers.
In ANNs, self-attention calculates token similarities by
taking the dot product of Queries (Q) and Keys (K), assign-

attention [12]. However, the sparse, event-driven nature of
SNNs results in numerous non-spikes (represented as 0) and
a limited number of spikes (represented as 1) in spiking Q

In this paper, we propose the α-XNOR similarity calcu-

trains, spikes convey more critical information than non-

spikes. Based on α-XNOR similarity, we integrate it into
the spiking self-attention mechanism and and develop an α-
XNOR Spiking Self-Attention (α-SSA) module. Extensive
experiments demonstrate that α-SSA significantly improves
the performance of spiking Transformers across various ar-
chitectures. The main contributions of this paper are sum-
marized as follows:
• We thoroughly analyze the reasons why the dot product
is unsuitable for calculate spiking similarity. Our findings
reveal that the unique multiplicative properties of non-
spike hinder the dot-product’s ability to accurately cap-

• To address this issue, we propose the α-XNOR similar-
ity, which redefines the correlation of non-spike pairs as a
specific value α. This adjustment not only overcomes the
ineffectiveness of dot-product similarity but also empha-
sizes the distinct importance of spikes over non-spikes,
enabling richer information representation.
• Based on the α-XNOR similarity, we propose the α-
XNOR Spiking Self-Attention (α-SSA) module. Exten-
sive experiments show that α-SSA achieves significant
performance improvements across various Transformer
architectures on both static and neuromorphic datasets.
2. Related Work
Spiking Neural Networks: The event-driven mechanism
of SNNs greatly enhances energy efficiency, providing a
critical advantage for compute-constrained edge devices.
The advent of ANN-to-SNN conversion [4, 22] and di-
rect training methods [20, 42] significantly reduce the chal-
lenges of training high-performance SNNs. Leveraging
these advanced learning algorithms, some studies [25, 51]
propose deep residual SNNs, while others [43, 48] develope
multi-dimensional spiking attention mechanisms, achieving
competitive results on ImageNet [8, 35] and various neuro-
morphic datasets [1, 28, 30]. Further research explores par-
allel training strategies [15] and shortcuts residual connec-
tions [13, 25] for SNNs in the ResNet framework, extend-
ing SNNs’ applicability to diverse tasks. However, despite
these advances, a notable performance gap remains between
the deep SNN architectures and the latest Transformers.
Spiking Transformers: Recently, growing attention has
been paid to high-performance, energy-efficient spiking
Transformers. Some models [39, 53, 54] adopt a single-
stage Vision Transformer (ViT) architecture [11], while
recent approaches [36, 44, 46] favor a multi-stage archi-
tecture [31] to enable hierarchical feature extraction. For
instance, Spikformer [54] first proposes spike-based self-
attention computation, establishing the first spiking Trans-
former. Building on this, Spikingformer [53] introduces a
hardware-friendly residual learning architecture that avoids
non-spike computations in SNNs. Additionally, the Spike-
driven Transformer [44] incorporates the Hadamard prod-
uct into the self-attention module, achieving a fully spike-
driven mechanism. Furthermore, SpikingResformer [36]
integrates a Dual Spike self-attention module, enhancing
both performance and energy efficiency. Nonetheless, these
models primarily utilize self-attention as a token mixer [47],
without paying attention to exploring effective similarity
calculations suited to spike trains [2, 50]. Therefore, design-
ing spiking self-attention mechanisms tailored to the binary
nature of SNNs is essential for further advancement.
3. Preliminaries
3.1. Leaky Integrate-and-Fire model
The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) model [32] is one of
the most widely used spiking neuron models in SNNs due
to its efficiency in mimicking biological neurons. In a LIF
neuron, the membrane potential H[t] accumulates synaptic
input and decays over time. When H[t] exceeds the thresh-
old Vth, the neuron emits a spike. After firing, H[t] is reset
to Vreset, and the postsynaptic potential U[t] continues to
evolve, decaying back to the resting potential if no further
input arrives. These dynamics are governed by:
H[t] = τU[t − 1] + WS[t], (1)
S[t] = Θ (H[t] − Vth) , (2)
U[t] = H[t] (1 − S[t]) + VresetS[t], (3)
where τ is the decay constant, W the synaptic weight ma-
trix, S[t] the input spike at time t, and Θ(·) the Heaviside
function. Θ(·) outputs 1 when a spike is fired, and 0 for the
absence of a spike (i.e., a non-spike). For brevity, we denote
the spiking neuron layer as SN(·) in subsequent sections.

4. Methods
4.1. Spiking Dot-Product Similarity Ineffectiveness
In ANNs, the attention mechanism effectively captures rel-
evant information by using dot products to calculate query-
key similarity, where higher similarity increases the weight
of the corresponding value. However, in SNNs, the spike-
driven nature poses two major challenges, making the spik-
ing dot products ineffective for calculating similarity be-
tween spike trains.
Firstly, the unique mathematical properties of 0 prevent
dot product from effectively capturing correlations involv-
ing non-spike events (represented as 0) in SNNs. As shown
in Fig.1, in the first spike train pair q1 and k1, the 3 match-
ing values yield a dot-product similarity score of 3, which is
consistent. However, this consistency relies on the absence
of 0-0 pairs. In the second pair q2 and k2, despite 6 match-
ing values, the dot-product score is only 2, as 0-0 pairs do
not contribute in multiplication. A more extreme case is the
third pair q3 and k3, where both spike trains are entirely
identical, yet the dot-product similarity score is 0, which
5446
is significantly unreasonable. Notably, due to the sparsity
of spike trains, pairs like the second are the most common.
Thus, the dot-product similarity is ineffective in SSA due to
the multiplication properties of 0.
Secondly, dot-product operations in ANNs effectively
capture a wide range of attentional preferences, with sign
bits indicating the direction of correlation between corre-
sponding elements. In contrast, due to the binary nature of
spike trains in SNNs, dot product results are limited to two
states. Additionally, they lack sign bits to denote the direc-
tion of correlation. Consequently, these limitations lead to
a significant deficiency in information capacity during dot-
product similarity calculations. As depicted in Fig.2, com-
pared to the rich dot product outcomes in ANNs, the results
become increasingly sparse following dot-product calcula-
tions in SNNs and fail to represent the correlation direction.
4.2. α-XNOR Similarity
To address the issue wherein matching non-spike pairs do
not contribute to the dot-product similarity score, we intu-
itively expect that identical pairs, whether they are spikes or
non-spikes, contribute equally to the similarity score. Thus,
we replace the multiplication operation in dot product with
an operation where matching values contribute a score of 1
while differing values contribute a score of 0. This adjust-
ment aligns with the XNOR operation in binary logic:

the spiking data. Building on this insight, we further

a lower weight to matching non-spikes, making them less
significant than matching spikes yet more significant than

4.3. α-XNOR Spiking Self-Attention
In Sec. 4.2, we introduce α-XNOR similarity to address
dot-product limitations, and differentiate spikes and non-
spikes in similarity calculations. Leveraging this spik-
ing similarity calculation, we develop an improved spik-
ing self-attention module, termed as α-XNOR Spiking Self-
Attention (α-SSA).
The overall architecture of α-SSA is shown in Fig. 4 (a).
Initially, the spiking Q, K, and V matrices are generated
similarly to SSA using Eq. 4. Then, we calculate the simi-
larity scores with α-XNOR similarity instead of the original
dot-product similarity:
Score = Simα-XNOR(Q, K). (24)
Following this, the obtained attention scores undergo
transformation. In the original SSA, only a scaling factor
adjusts the score magnitudes, which is inadequate for scores
based on α-XNOR similarity, as they exhibit different dis-
tribution patterns (see Fig. 4 (b)). In α-SSA, we use a linear
transformation for the score with an added bias term:
Attn′

5.2. Study of Different α
In the α-SSA module, α serves as a critical parameter. We
conducted experiments on the Spikformer architecture on
CIFAR100 dataset to investigate the effect of varying α val-
ues on metrics. These metrics include the linear scaling
factors k and b; the spiking firing rates fQ, fK, fV, and
fAttn responsible for generating Q, K, V, and the spiking
self-attention output Attn, respectively; and the network’s
performance accuracy (Acc.). We set α to [0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6,
0.8, 1], where α = 0 and α = 1 correspond to dot-product
similarity and XNOR similarity, respectively, while inter-
mediate values represent α-XNOR similarities. Fig. 6 illus-
trates the average values of these metrics, and detailed data
across different α-SSA layers are provided in Suppl.D. The
results show a clear negative correlation between α and the
parameters k and b. The parameters fQ, fK, fV, and fAttn
exhibit no clear correlation and present a complex interac-
tion, with fV showing relatively minimal variation. More-
over, model accuracy peaks when α ranges from 0.2 to 0.4,
indicating that this range optimally reflects the differential
importance of spikes and non-spikes in attention scoring.
5.3. Ablation Study
To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed model’s com-
ponents, we conducted an ablation study on the CIFAR100
dataset using Spikformer as the baseline. We replaced Spik-

ues of α: 0, 1, and 0.3. For α = 0, the original SSA replaces
only the linear transformations of attention scores, main-
taining the traditional dot-product similarity. For α = 1
and α = 0.3, the model employs XNOR similarity and
α-XNOR similarity, respectively. As shown in Table 3,
adding the linear transformations for attention scores in-

6. Conclusion
This work identifies a fundamental challenge in spiking
Transformers: the dot product is ineffective for calculat-
ing spiking similarity due to the binary and sparse na-
ture of spike trains. To address this issue, we propose
a novel α-XNOR similarity method specifically tailored
to spike trains, which effectively resolves the limitations
of dot-product similarity caused by numerous non-spiking
events. Moreover, given the sparse nature of spike trains,
where spikes carry more information than non-spikes, the
α-XNOR similarity emphasizes the distinct importance of
spikes, with enhanced representation capability in spik-
ing self-attention. Extensive experiments demonstrate sig-
nificant performance improvements across various Trans-
former architectures. These advancements bring us closer
to realizing the full potential of spiking Transformers in ef-
ficient, neuromorphic computing applications.
