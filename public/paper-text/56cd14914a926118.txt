Abstract
Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) have gained significant at-
tention due to their event-driven computational paradigm,
making them promising for neuromorphic computing. In
recent years, the integration of SNNs and Transformer ar-
chitectures has made remarkable progress in various tasks.
However, existing spiking self-attention mechanisms pre-
dominantly focus on spatial information while neglecting
explicit temporal modeling, leading to suboptimal perfor-
mance. In this paper, we introduce the Temporal Interaction
Coefficient (TIC) to analyze temporal dependency patterns
in these spatial-only attention mechanisms, revealing their
limited temporal interactions and restricted pattern diver-
sity. To overcome this issue, we propose the Multi-Delay
Mixer (MD-Mixer), drawing inspiration from time delay
mechanisms in the nervous system. Specifically, MD-Mixer
introduces multiple temporal delays to perform effective
time mixing and facilitate temporally enriched spatial at-
tention. In addition, it can be integrated seamlessly into ex-
isting Spiking Transformers as a drop-in replacement while
maintaining computational efficiency. Extensive evalua-
tions on static and neuromorphic benchmarks demonstrate
that MD-Mixer substantially improves the performance of
Spiking Transformers, outperforming existing state-of-the-
art (SOTA) methods. This work establishes MD-Mixer as
an effective and general solution for temporal modeling in
event-driven architectures.
1. Introduction
Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) have emerged as a
promising paradigm in neuromorphic computing, offering
a fundamentally different approach to information process-
ing compared to conventional Artificial Neural Networks
(ANNs) [9, 14, 27]. Drawing inspiration from biological
neurons, SNNs encode and transmit information through
discrete, event-driven spikes, which are triggered when
membrane potentials surpass firing thresholds. The sparse
and event-driven nature [4] of spike-based computation en-
ables remarkable energy efficiency when deployed on neu-
romorphic hardware platforms [1, 5, 28, 30]. Moreover, the
inherent temporal dynamics [52] embedded in spike timing
provide a natural framework for processing spatiotempo-
ral information. These distinctive characteristics establish
SNNs as ideal candidates in energy-constrained spatiotem-
poral computing scenarios [21, 22, 24, 25].
To further unlock their potential, Transformer architec-
tures have recently been integrated into SNNs, spawning
numerous Spiking Transformer models [35, 45, 53, 56,
62, 63], demonstrating substantial performance improve-
ments across diverse applications [19, 36, 49, 60]. Despite
these advances, existing self-attention mechanisms [11, 38,
41, 51] suffer from a fundamental limitation that they
effectively capture spatial relationships within individual
timesteps but critically fail to model temporal dependen-
cies. This temporal modeling deficiency stems from the
exclusive reliance on intrinsic spiking neuron dynamics [8,
10], which proves insufficient for capturing temporal pat-
terns inherent in neuromorphic data [33, 58]. Existing so-
lutions attempting to address temporal dependencies either
incur prohibitive computational costs or sacrifice modeling
capacity [18, 34, 48], leaving temporal modeling as a crit-
ical unresolved challenge in Spiking Transformers. Com-
pounding this issue, the absence of proper evaluation met-
rics limits researchers to task-specific performance metrics,
impeding both theoretical understanding and practical de-
velopment of Spiking Transformers.
In this paper, we introduce the Temporal Interaction Co-
efficient (TIC), a quantitative metric that measures tempo-
ral modeling capability in attention mechanisms. Through
TIC-based analysis, we reveal that existing spiking attention
mechanisms exhibit sharply peaked reliance distributions
at current timesteps with negligible historical contributions,
indicating severely limited temporal interactions. This find-

tational efficiency.
2. Related Work
2.1. Spiking Neural Networks
SNNs fundamentally differ from conventional ANNs by
encoding and transmitting information through discrete
spikes rather than continuous activations, which endows
them with promising advantages in terms of energy effi-
ciency [26, 37] and biological plausibility. The introduction
of direct training methods [8, 50] and ANN-to-SNN conver-
sion strategies [2, 12] has enabled the development of high-
performance and energy-efficient SNNs [15, 39, 40, 47].
Building on these advances, researchers have incorporated
spiking attention mechanisms [55, 59, 64] into SNNs to
further enhance recognition performance on ImageNet [6]
and neuromorphic datasets [20]. Moreover, the adoption
of residual connections [7, 13] has effectively mitigated
the gradient vanishing problem in deep SNNs, enabling
large-scale network training. Additionally, recent studies
have explored integrating Transformer architectures into
SNNs, resulting in representative models such as Spik-
former [63], SpikingResformer [38], and the Spike-Driven
Transformer [53, 57]. These models have further improved
the performance of SNNs on image recognition and neuro-
morphic datasets.
2.2. Temporal Interactions in Spiking Transformers
Spiking Transformers have shown strong representation ca-
pability while consuming substantially less energy than
their ANN counterparts. However, temporal modeling in
existing methods remains limited, as it is mainly handled
implicitly through spiking neuron dynamics. Existing stud-
ies on temporal interactions in spiking transformers mainly
follow two directions: explicit spatiotemporal modeling and
lightweight temporal enhancement. In the first direction,
STSA [48] jointly models temporal and spatial interactions,
improving performance on neuromorphic datasets at the
cost of increased computational overhead. STAtten [18] fur-
ther improves efficiency by adopting a block-wise strategy
for temporal and spatial feature modeling while preserv-
ing the same computational complexity as spatial-only ap-
proaches. In the second direction, TIM [34], built on Spik-
former [63], incorporates historical temporal information
into the query branch with only a few additional parameters,
thereby improving temporal modeling. SNN-ViT [46] in-
troduces a dedicated Saccadic Spike Self-Attention (SSSA)
mechanism to enhance whole-scene understanding through
temporal interactions.
3. Preliminary
3.1. Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron
Spiking neurons serve as the fundamental computational
units of SNNs, with the leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) model
being the most widely adopted due to its balance between
biological plausibility and computational efficiency. The
LIF neuron integrates incoming input currents over time
while allowing a gradual decay of the membrane potential
through a leakage mechanism. When the accumulated po-

ates a spike and resets its state. This process can be formally
described by the following equations:
Ut = λHt−1 + It (1)
St = Θ (Ut − Vth) (2)
Ht = Ut(1 − St) + VresetSt (3)
Here, t denotes the discrete time step. I represents the input
current, and U denotes the membrane potential after inte-
gration but prior to spike generation. H indicates the mem-
brane potential after firing, which decays exponentially un-
der the influence of the leaky factor λ. Θ(·) is the Heavi-
side step function responsible for spike activation. When U
exceeds the firing threshold Vth, the neuron emits a spike,
and its membrane potential is reset to Vreset; otherwise, no
spike is generated. For brevity, we denote the spiking neu-
ron layer as SN(·) throughout the following sections.

tailored for SNNs. Given binary queries (Q), keys (K), and

4.3. Multi-Delay Self-Attention Framework
By integrating the proposed MD-Mixer into existing spik-
ing transformer architectures, we develop the unified Multi-
Delay Self-Attention Framework, as illustrated in Fig. 3(a).
This framework enables temporal mixing through delay-
based temporal interactions, thereby achieving space–time
mixing by applying spatial attention to temporally inte-
grated features.
Temporal Mixing. The MD-Mixer performs multi-scale
temporal integration within each channel, thereby generat-
ing delay-enhanced K and V that encode rich temporal dy-
namics, while Q maintain the original temporal alignment.
Formally, given an input tensor X ∈ RT ×N×C
, the query,
key, and value projections are obtained as:
Q = SN(BN(Linear(X))), (8)
K = SN(BN(MD-Mixer(X))), (9)
V = SN(BN(MD-Mixer(X))). (10)
Here, BN(·) denotes batch normalization, and Linear(·)
represents a fully connected layer. After temporal mixing,
the representations carry temporally enriched context, facil-
itating space–time modeling.
Space-Time Mixing. The obtained Q, K, and V are sub-
sequently fed into the spiking self-attention module, where
the attention computation extends from purely spatial cor-
relations to spatiotemporal interactions. To illustrate this,
Fig. 3(b) visualizes the vanilla K/V features used in the
baseline attention, which are computed independently at
each timestep and therefore capture only spatial correla-
tions. In contrast, the delayed K/V features in Fig. 3(c)
aggregate contextual information from current and preced-
ing timesteps, achieving space–time mixing in the attention
computation. Formally, the attention output is given by
Z = Atten(Q, K, V), (11)
where Z denotes the attention output, and Atten(·) denotes
a generic attention function. With the delay-based temporal
interactions provided by the MD-Mixer, the spiking self-
attention evolves from spatial-only modeling to space–time
mixing, capturing both spatial and temporal dependencies
in spike-driven computation.

4.5. Computational Efficiency
Analysis of the MD-Mixer’s computational complexity pro-
vides insights into its efficiency characteristics. As shown
in Eq. 7, the module reduces both computational complexity
from O(TD2
) to O(KTD) and parameter count from D2
to KD, yielding a reduction factor of approximately D/K
in both cases. Given the architectural design where K ≪ D
(e.g., K = 5 while D = 256), the MD-Mixer theoretically
achieves efficiency gains over standard linear layers used
for Key and Value computation in attention mechanisms,
demonstrating that temporal modeling can be incorporated
without substantial computational overhead.

For performance comparison, we integrate MD-Mixer

5.2. Performance Comparison
5.2.1. Static Dataset
We evaluate MD-Mixer on ImageNet [6], comprising ap-
proximately 1.3 million training images and 50,000 val-
idation images across 1,000 classes, using synaptic-wise
delays. Table 1 presents performance comparisons across
various Spiking Transformer architectures. When inte-
grated into Spike-driven Transformer V1, MD-Mixer con-
sistently delivers substantial performance gains across dif-
ferent model scales, significantly outperforming the base-
line while maintaining competitive performance with STAt-
ten [18]. Specifically, under the 8–768 configuration at
224 × 224 resolution, MD-Mixer achieves 78.23% top-1
accuracy, corresponding to a notable 1.91% improvement
over the baseline and remaining on par with STAtten (a
0.12% gain). When deployed with Spike-driven Trans-
former V2 in the 8-512 configuration, MD-Mixer attains
80.02% top-1 accuracy at 224 × 224 resolution, achieving
a 0.53% improvement over the baseline. These results con-
vincingly demonstrate the efficacy of our temporal model-
ing approach on static datasets.

5.4. Ablation Study
5.4.1. Effect of Learnable Delays
To assess the impact of learnable delays, we compare two
configurations: random delays with fixed, randomly initial-
ized values, and MD-Mixer with delays optimized during
training. Aggregation coefficients remain learnable in both
settings. Table 5 summarizes the results on s-CIFAR10
and CIFAR10-DVS datasets. Random delays bring mod-
est performance gains of 0.74% and 0.52% over the base-
line on s-CIFAR10 and CIFAR10-DVS, respectively. In
contrast, MD-Mixer achieves substantially larger improve-
ments of 3.00% and 2.44%, respectively. These results
demonstrate that while random delays provide slight im-
provements through temporal diversity, learnable delays in
MD-Mixer fundamentally enable adaptive capture and op-
timization of multi-scale temporal patterns, thereby achiev-
ing significantly superior performance. Visualization of the
learned delays is provided in the Supplementary Material.
5.4.2. Effect of the Number of Delay Branches
We evaluate the impact of the number of delay branches
on model performance across s-CIFAR10 and CIFAR10-
DVS datasets. As illustrated in Fig. 6(a), the number
of delay branches has a clear impact on model perfor-
mance across both datasets. On s-CIFAR10, the accu-
racy increases from 84.3% with a single branch to a peak
of 86.7% with three branches, then gradually declines to
84.2% with six branches. Similarly, on CIFAR10-DVS, per-
formance improves from 81.1% with one branch to 82.4%
with four branches, before dropping to 80.9% with six
branches. These results indicate that an optimal number of
delay branches most effectively captures multi-scale tempo-
ral patterns, while excessive branches impair performance
due to overly complex temporal modeling.

6. Conclusion
In this paper, we introduce a novel metric TIC to evalu-
ate the temporal interaction capability of existing spiking
self-attention mechanisms and visualize their temporal de-
pendency patterns. Our evaluation reveals limited temporal
interactions that hinder their performance. Inspired by bi-
ological time delay, we propose MD-Mixer with learnable
multi-scale temporal delays that seamlessly integrate into
existing spiking transformers. By coupling multi-scale tem-
poral patterns with self-attention, MD-Mixer achieves com-
prehensive space-time mixing and significantly enhances
spatiotemporal feature learning. Extensive experiments
demonstrate substantial improvements across various archi-
tectures on both static and neuromorphic datasets. We hope
this work inspires future research on biologically-plausible
temporal modeling in neuromorphic computing.
