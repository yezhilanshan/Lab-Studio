ABSTRACT Three-dimensional human pose estimation (3D-HPE) is important for human-centered sensing, but vision-based
solutions are limited by privacy concerns, illumination changes, and occlusion. Although millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar
provides a privacy-preserving alternative, existing radar point clouds are often sparse and incomplete, and existing radar 3D-
HPE datasets remain limited in subject scale, frame volume, and motion diversity. This paper presents a mmWave radar
prototype for dense point-cloud sensing, a large-scale mmWave radar dense human pose estimation (mmWave-DensePose)
dataset, and the part-aware spatio-temporal network (PAST-Net) for radar-only 3D-HPE. Specifically, we design a four-chip
cascaded mmWave radar system with an azimuth and elevation resolution of 1.30∘
and 2.24∘
, respectively, to generate
dense human-related point clouds. The constructed dataset contains 101 subjects, 19 designed motions, numerous free
motions, and 570 124 valid synchronized frames. Each valid frame contains 164 valid subject-related radar points on average,
exceeding the representative released datasets and providing clearer body-region cues for radar-based 3D-HPE. The proposed
method uses only mmWave radar point clouds as input and Kinect V2 skeletons as ground-truth annotations. PAST-Net
reorganizes frame-level point-cloud features into skeleton-aware part-token representations and models their temporal
evolution through part-wise self-attention, compensating for weak and incomplete observations in local body regions.
Experiments on the proposed benchmark show that PAST-Net achieves an average mean localization error of 4.08 cm,
reducing the error by 22.14% compared with a state-of-the-art baseline. Ablation results further confirm the contributions of
the proposed modules and input features, which include skeleton-aware spatial modeling, part-wise temporal modeling, and
radar-specific point attributes. These results demonstrate that the designed mmWave radar prototype and the proposed method
provide an effective sensing basis for privacy-preserving and fine-grained 3D-HPE.

1. Introduction
Human pose estimation (HPE) involves locating human skeletal key points and characterizing human postures using
standardized structured expressions. This technology can effectively establish spatial representations of human body
topological structures and dynamic motion characteristics. As a core perception technology, HPE holds pivotal practical value
in diverse research and application fields, including human–computer interaction, intelligent health management, clinical
rehabilitation evaluation, indoor intelligent perception, and public safety monitoring [1–3]. Engineering studies further
demonstrate the value of pose-related sensing for industrial activity analysis, three-dimensional joint-kinematics
measurement, and fine-grained hand–object interaction [4–6]. Accordingly, achieving accurate and robust HPE capabilities
is crucial for reliable human-oriented sensing systems.
2
Vision and video-based HPE mainly involve two-dimensional (2D) joint positioning and three-dimensional (3D) human
skeleton reconstruction. The establishment of sufficient large-scale visual benchmark datasets and deep learning architectures
is substantially driving the advancement of this research direction [7–10]. Nevertheless, visual perception solutions are highly
dependent on favorable imaging conditions and target visibility; their estimation accuracy tends to drop significantly under
low-light conditions, target occlusion, smoke disturbance, and intricate background environments. Moreover, camera-based
visual perception poses a serious risk of privacy breaches, greatly limiting its practical application.
Against the aforementioned limitations, millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar serves as an ideal privacy-secured and non-
contact sensing solution for three-dimensional human pose estimation (3D-HPE). Unlike optical devices, mmWave radar
cannot record human facial details, body textures, or visual appearances and thus fundamentally avoids potential privacy
risks. In addition, it offers all-time and all-weather sensing capability and is robust to darkness and extreme lighting
environments. Beyond that, it can accurately extract abundant physical perception information of human subjects, including
spatial position, radial velocity and azimuth information. Thanks to these advantages, mmWave radar is well qualified for
human-centric intelligent sensing tasks in numerous scenarios [11–14].
Current radar-based 3D-HPE studies primarily adopt two categories of data representations, each with distinct
characteristics and application scenarios. The first category leverages raw or intermediate radar signals, including analog-to-
digital converter (ADC) signals, range-Doppler maps (RDMs), range-angle maps (RAMs), and radar tensors [12,15,16].
These signal-based representations retain comprehensive intrinsic radar information, which is conducive to capturing the fine-
grained motion and spatial features of human targets. However, they typically demand high computational resources, and
their performance is highly dependent on hardware configurations and preprocessing pipelines, limiting their generalization
to diverse deployment scenarios. The second category employs radar point clouds, which offer a compact geometric
representation with lower dimensionality. Compared with signal-based representations, radar point clouds not only reduce the
computational burden but also exhibit better compatibility with mainstream 3D geometric learning frameworks. More
significantly, they create a direct spatial alignment with human skeletal keypoints, thereby facilitating the extraction of pose-
related spatial features and substantially improving the efficiency and precision of real-time 3D-HPE. [13,17–21].
Despite the numerous advantages of radar-based 3D-HPE, this research direction still presents several critical challenges.
First, the radar point clouds used in existing studies are generally sparse and spatially incomplete. Such sparsity fails to
provide stable and continuous contour information of the human body, especially for slender limbs and small joints (e.g.,
wrist, ankle, and finger joints), greatly increasing the ambiguity of skeletal keypoint localization and degrading the accuracy
of pose estimation [20,22–24]. Second, current radar-based 3D-HPE datasets often suffer from inherent limitations, such as a
small number of subjects, insufficient frame counts, restricted motion categories, and sparse point-cloud quality. These
deficiencies affect the model’s generalization ability across different subjects and its robustness in complex environments
[15,25–29]. Third, most radar point-cloud 3D-HPE models extract global features from sparse data and apply temporal
learning or point completion. They generally adopt unified modeling for the entire body, ignoring the varied reflection traits
and movement rules of different body segments. Limbs and small joints yield weak and unstable echoes, while the torso
shows steady radar responses. The use of uniform modeling results in uneven prediction accuracy. Hence, designing dedicated
modules for separate body parts is vital to improve overall estimation reliability [23,30–32].
To address the aforementioned challenges, this work builds a mmWave radar dense point-cloud HPE dataset denoted as
mmWave-DensePose and develops the part-aware spatio-temporal network (PAST-Net) for radar-based 3D-HPE. Fig. 1
illustrates the radar-based 3D-HPE scenario and the overall workflow of PAST-Net, from dense point-cloud sensing to
skeleton-aware spatio-temporal modeling and 3D keypoint regression. At the hardware level, recent mmWave multiple-input
multiple-output (MIMO) implementations demonstrate that scalable multi-channel transceivers require high channel isolation,
low-loss packaging, and compact system integration [33]. Specifically, a customized mmWave radar system is developed to
generate dense human point clouds capable of characterizing the continuous body contours of human subjects during various
motion processes. The constructed dataset comprises over 570 000 valid synchronized frames collected from 101 subjects,
covering 19 predefined motions and free-motion sequences, with each frame integrating multiple types of data, including
mmWave radar point clouds, Kinect V2 skeleton annotations, and red–green–blue (RGB) images. For the proposed 3D-HPE
method, only radar point clouds are utilized as input, while Kinect V2 skeleton annotations serve as ground truth. The
accompanying image data are included as extensible resources, which can support future research on multimodal sensing,
cross-modal learning, and visualization tasks.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
(1) We construct a customized radar acquisition prototype. Benefiting from the optimized hardware of a four-chip
cascaded design with 12 transmitters and 16 receivers, the radar system outputs dense point clouds and delivers more
complete contour details than conventional sparse radar data.
(2) We establish the mmWave-DensePose dataset, a large-scale dense radar pose dataset. With abundant
participant samples and frame volumes, this dataset offers qualified data sources for model training and performance
assessment. Videos S1–S4 in Appendix A also create favorable conditions for subsequent cross-modal exploration.

(3) We present PAST-Net for radar-based 3D-HPE. Unlike mainstream global modeling schemes, PAST-Net
splits the human body into independent segments and adopts combined spatial and temporal attention mechanisms. It
effectively captures segment-specific scattering characteristics and inter-frame motion relationships, thereby reducing
estimation errors in vulnerable small joints and limbs. The network thus provides better accuracy balance in pose
prediction.

2. Related work
2.1. mmWave radar for 3D-HPE
mmWave radar systems detect human targets by capturing and analyzing reflected electromagnetic waves. Specifically,
frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) radar provides core target parameters such as range, velocity, and angle
through signal processing. After target detection and spatial localization, the measured parameters are reconstructed into radar
point clouds. The fidelity and quality of these point clouds depend on several critical factors, including radar waveform
configuration, antenna array design, system signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR), and target detection strategies [32,34,35].
For radar-based 3D-HPE, point-cloud density and spatial resolution are key factors affecting detection accuracy. Radar
system bandwidth primarily determines range resolution, while chirp configuration parameters significantly influence
velocity estimation and temporal sampling quality. Array aperture size and the number of virtual channels, in turn, determine
angular resolution [36]. Higher spatial resolution enables the radar sensor to distinguish adjacent body parts and accurately
capture body contours, making it essential for fine‑grained keypoint localization. Moreover, existing studies indicate that
sparse radar measurements severely limit the detailed reconstruction and accurate perception of human body information [12–
14,20].
Most existing radar human-sensing systems or prototypes focus on motion recognition, gesture recognition, vital-sign
monitoring, and coarse posture classification [37–41]. Unlike 3D-HPE, these tasks do not require the precise localization of
skeletal keypoints. In contrast, high-quality 3D-HPE relies on detailed and fine-scale spatial representation of the human
4
body. Consequently, dense mmWave radar point clouds provide a more reliable sensing foundation for skeleton-level 3D-
HPE.
2.2. mmWave radar human pose datasets
Research on radar-based 3D-HPE has yielded several publicly available datasets to support model development and
evaluation. Early efforts concentrated on pure radar point clouds or coarse multimodal labels. MiliPoint [42] is a large-scale
open dataset containing 545 000 frames across 49 human motions. This dataset validates the effectiveness of point‑based deep
learning for mmWave radar human perception tasks and establishes solid baselines for future research. HuPR [25]
synchronizes radar data with monocular RGB images and adopts a cross-modal training scheme to ease radar annotation
challenges. It shows that radar‑only data can achieve pose estimation accuracy superior to traditional radio‑frequency
methods. The mmBody Benchmark [26] focuses on mmWave radar based on 3D human body reconstruction. It provides
paired radar point clouds and RGB–depth (RGB-D) images with complete skeleton and mesh labels. Experimental results
show that mmWave radar achieves high reconstruction accuracy between RGB and depth cameras, along with greater
robustness under adverse weather conditions.
As tasks have become more complex, modern mmWave radar datasets have increasingly focused on multimodal fusion
and 3D perception. The mRI dataset [43] integrates three data modalities and includes 160 000 frames of therapeutic
movements from 20 participants. Differing from point-cloud-based schemes, RT-Pose [15] builds a benchmark centered on
four-dimensional (4D) radar tensors for 3D-HPE and target localization. This dataset provides 72 000 frames across 240
sequences of six motion complexities, together with light detection and ranging (LiDAR) and RGB annotations.
More recent datasets further emphasize large-scale multimodal collection and privacy protection. MM‑Fi [27] is the first
non‑intrusive 4D human dataset to combine six modalities, covering 27 daily and rehabilitation motions. It provides 2D and
3D pose labels, motion classes, and 3D coordinates to advance wireless sensing research. The mmFree‑Pose dataset [28]
focuses on privacy-friendly 3D-HPE; it adopts a vision-free framework to synchronize mmWave radar data with signals from
a VDSuit-Full motion capture system and records over ten types of motions ranging from basic gestures to falls. As the latest
release, M4Human [29] stands as the largest multimodal benchmark for mmWave radar-based human mesh reconstruction.
It includes 661 000 synchronized frames from 20 participants across 50 motions, along with high-precision marker-based 3D
mesh and global trajectory annotations. With a volume nine times larger than those of prior datasets, it effectively supports
fundamental perception research and multimodal fusion development.
2.3. Deep learning methods for radar-based 3D-HPE
Deep learning methods for radar-based 3D-HPE can be categorized by their input data representations. Methods relying
on raw radar data extract pose-relevant feature representations directly from original radar tensors. Early works mainly use
convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to estimate human skeletal poses using radar heatmaps and projected radar images. A
method has been proposed to capture human poses using a 77 GHz commercial mmWave radar [12]. In this approach, dual
2D range-angle heatmaps are obtained from two orthogonal radars with 2D-fast Fourier transform (FFT); then, a CNN is
employed for joint regression. For real‑time 3D pose estimation, the mm‑Pose system converts radar tensors into
three‑channel pseudo‑RGB images and applies a two‑branch CNN to detect over 15 joints [13]. The mPose system [14] adopts
a three-layer CNN for spatial feature extraction and achieves 3D skeleton reconstruction with only one radar. To further
capture global feature correlations, a transformer module combined with a confidence refinement network is introduced [44].
This design enhances long-range context modeling and keypoint localization accuracy, yielding better pose estimation than
conventional CNN-based methods. For multi‑format feature fusion, ProbRadarM3F combines traditional FFT heatmaps with
probability‑based position encoding to estimate 14 human joints, reaching an average precision (AP) of 69.9% on the HuPR
dataset [45]. To exploit dual‑radar observations, the TransHuPR transformer fuses features from two orthogonal 2D
projections and achieves an improvement of 9.4% in the AP score compared with single‑radar systems [46]. Although these
methods fully utilize the rich information in raw radar signals, they usually incur high computation costs and require
radar‑specific preprocessing, which restricts real‑world deployment.
In contrast, point-cloud-based 3D-HPE methods adopt detected radar point sets as network inputs. Each radar point
provides spatial coordinates and can be augmented with auxiliary attributes such as velocity, SNR, and temporal features.
Unlike cameras or LiDAR, mmWave radar generates sparse and noisy point clouds, which create considerable challenges for
reliable pose estimation. Inspired by general point-cloud networks [47–49], several studies have applied point-based deep
learning to mmWave radar 3D-HPE. The mmHPE framework [50] adopts a coarse-to-fine structure to gradually refine joint
predictions from global localization to local fine-tuning. Another mmHPE approach [18] combines CNNs with bidirectional
long short-term memory (BiLSTM) to capture spatio-temporal dependencies in sequential point clouds, improving
performance in dynamic scenes. PoseGraphNet [51] integrates an attention-enhanced dynamic graph convolutional network
with human pose priors, modeling the skeleton as a graph to capture local joint relations and global posture patterns. To tackle
pose vibration induced by radar noise and low resolution, mmPose-FK [52] embeds forward kinematics into an end-to-end
network to realize stable pose estimation. Owing to the compact nature of point-cloud representations, these methods are well
suited for real-time 3D-HPE applications. Nevertheless, radar point clouds still suffer from inherent sparsity, incompleteness,
5
and spatial non-uniformity. Direct keypoint regression from such low-quality data often results in unstable and inaccurate
limb localization.
To mitigate the sparsity and discontinuity of mmWave radar point clouds, researchers are exploring two primary
enhancement strategies. First, temporal modeling improves radar-based 3D-HPE by exploiting the inherent motion continuity
of human poses across consecutive frames. Current multi-frame learning methods use recurrent neural networks, bidirectional
recurrent units, temporal attention mechanisms, graph-based temporal structures, and Mamba-driven feature fusion to reduce
the pose ambiguity caused by sparse radar observations [21,53–56]. Beyond radar-based HPE, limb-based spatial hypergraphs
and temporal hypergraphs have also been used to model high-order relationships among human joints across body regions
and consecutive frames for visual body-language recognition [57]. However, such visual action-recognition formulations do
not address radar-specific missing observations or direct 3D keypoint regression. Although temporal modeling stabilizes pose
predictions across frames, it introduces extra inference latency and computational overhead. Therefore, real-time radar 3D-
HPE systems require a trade-off between temporal context and inference efficiency. Second, point completion and generative
data augmentation effectively alleviate the sparsity and incompleteness of radar point clouds. The VirTeach framework uses
synthetic radar point clouds to fill radar sensing blind spots and balance the signal response of different human body parts
[23]. To mitigate the sparsity of radar point clouds, a semi-supervised point-cloud completion strategy for data enhancement
has been proposed [30]. Based on the enhanced data, a dual-branch global-local network is further constructed to extract
features from enhanced and raw point clouds for 3D human pose regression. Despite these advances, existing studies indicate
that sparse and incomplete radar observations still limit the performance of radar-based 3D-HPE.
Overall, existing radar-based 3D-HPE methods achieve progress through diverse techniques, including feature learning
from raw radar tensors, sparse point-cloud modeling, temporal context learning, and generative data enhancement. However,
few studies unify dense mmWave point-cloud sensing, large-scale high-quality benchmarks, and part-aware spatio-temporal
modeling within a single framework. To fill this gap, the present work constructs a large-scale dense mmWave radar point-
cloud dataset and proposes PAST-Net for radar-only 3D-HPE. The network reorganizes dense radar point-cloud features into
skeleton-aware part-token representations and models their temporal evolution to improve keypoint localization under non-
uniform radar observations.
3. Acquisition prototype of dense mmWave radar data
This section introduces the acquisition prototype used to construct the mmWave-DensePose dataset. The prototype
contains a customized mmWave radar system, a multimodal data-acquisition system, and a calibration-synchronization
pipeline. These components provide the sensing, annotation, and alignment basis for the final dataset described in Section 4.
3.1. The customized mmWave radar system
Accurate skeletal keypoint localization for 3D-HPE requires fine-grained spatial observations of the human body. For
radar-based 3D-HPE, the density and spatial coverage of radar point clouds are important for reliable keypoint regression.
However, many representative radar point-cloud datasets are collected using commercial mmWave radar development boards,
such as the TI IWR1843 and IWR6843, whose antenna configurations and angular resolutions are designed for general radar
sensing rather than fine-grained human keypoint localization. Consequently, the resulting human point clouds can be
relatively sparse or incomplete, especially for weakly observed body regions such as the arms and legs. A radar system with
improved spatial sensing capability is therefore desirable for providing more complete human-related observations for 3D-
HPE [20,42,58].
To address this issue, we designed a customized 4D mmWave radar system for dense human point-cloud acquisition.
The system aims to improve the spatial separability and coverage of human-related reflections in both the azimuth and
elevation directions. It adopts a four-chip cascaded MIMO architecture, in which each radar chip provides three transmit and
four receive channels. The complete system therefore contains 12 transmitters and 16 receivers, corresponding to 192
transmitter–receiver channel combinations.
Rather than directly using the standard cascaded AWR2243 uniform-array configuration, we redesigned the sparse-array
layout to improve the elevation resolution and spatial observability of the human body. The virtual array uses an enlarged 2D
aperture with horizontal and vertical baselines designed for azimuth and elevation estimation. It combines uniform subarrays
with non-uniform spatial sampling. The uniform components provide stable spatial sampling for angle estimation, while the
non-uniform components provide additional spatial information for resolving angle ambiguities. Together with the subsequent
angle-estimation and ambiguity-resolution algorithms, this hardware–algorithm co-design supports finer spatial separability
of human-related reflections in both the azimuth and elevation while maintaining a practical field of view for human motion
sensing. The resulting range, azimuth, elevation, and velocity resolutions are 0.06 m, 1.30∘
, 2.24∘
, and 0.0225 m·s−1,
respectively.
6
The MIMO transmission strategy is also customized for the proposed sensing system. The transmit channels are
organized into two complementary waveform groups, denoted as A-wave and B-wave. A phase-coded Doppler division
multiple access (DDMA) scheme is applied to the transmit antennas so that signals from different transmitters can be separated
in the Doppler/code domain after range–Doppler processing. Compared with sequential time division multiple access (TDMA)
transmission, DDMA reduces the temporal separation among multi-transmitter observations and avoids sequential transmitter
switching during one MIMO acquisition cycle. The A/B waveform organization, together with DDMA coding, enables
measurements from different transmitter–receiver combinations to be separated and reconstructed for subsequent virtual-array
processing and 2D angle estimation.
The customized radar outputs processed point clouds rather than continuously transmitting raw ADC data to the host
computer. Raw radar measurements have a substantially larger data volume and are less suitable for real-time transmission
through an external interface during continuous acquisition. Therefore, the point-cloud generation pipeline is embedded in
the radar system. Radar echoes are processed locally, and only the resulting point clouds are transmitted to the host computer
through Ethernet using the user datagram protocol (UDP). This architecture reduces the transmission burden and provides a
compact geometric representation for online radar-based 3D-HPE. The host-side acquisition interface further manages
synchronized frame reception, frame indexing, calibration-file association, acquisition monitoring, and offline batch
reconstruction, supporting stable and repeatable data collection across different subjects and motion categories.
Fig. 2 illustrates the main radar parameters and the complete point-cloud generation pipeline. At the signal-processing
level, the raw ADC measurements are first separated into A-wave and B-wave streams. Range FFT, data buffering, and
Doppler FFT are then performed to obtain the corresponding range–Doppler data. A pre-digital-beamforming (pre-DBF)
operation is applied to the receive channels to form several coarse beams covering the sensing field of view. This operation
aggregates target responses along multiple coarse directions and improves the detectability of weak human reflections before
target detection. The beam-domain responses are subsequently combined using non-coherent compression (NCC) and further
compressed along the Doppler dimension to construct the detection map. Constant false alarm rate (CFAR) detection is then
applied to identify candidate human-related scattering points.

For each detected candidate, the corresponding multi-channel complex samples are recovered from the A-wave and B-
wave measurements according to the DDMA phase-coding scheme. These samples are reconstructed into a virtual-array
snapshot and calibrated using complex-valued channel compensation coefficients. The azimuth and elevation are then
estimated from the calibrated virtual-array observations, followed by angle ambiguity resolution and coordinate
transformation. Finally, the estimated range, azimuth, elevation, and Doppler information is converted into a 3D radar point
cloud.
The dense human point clouds used in mmWave-DensePose therefore result from the combined operation of the
customized cascaded MIMO configuration, 2D virtual-array design, A/B DDMA transmission, channel calibration, pre-
detection beam aggregation, target detection, and high-resolution azimuth–elevation estimation. These components jointly
improve the spatial separability and coverage of human-related radar observations. The embedded point-cloud processing and
host acquisition interface further support efficient, stable, and repeatable continuous data collection.
3.2. Multimodal data-acquisition system
We then built a synchronized acquisition system that records radar observations and human pose annotations. This
system is deployed in an indoor environment and is designed to collect dense radar point clouds, Kinect V2 skeleton
annotations, and auxiliary image-related data.
As shown in Fig. 3, the acquisition system consists of a 4D mmWave radar, a Kinect V2 sensor, and a Python-based host
computer. The radar provides the sensing input for radar-based 3D-HPE, while the Kinect V2 sensor provides reference
skeleton annotations and auxiliary image data. The host computer controls the data streams, records timestamps, stores raw
data, and manages the subsequent synchronization procedure.
Fig. 3. Overview of the multimodal data-acquisition system. The system consists of a dense mmWave radar, a Kinect V2 sensor, and a Python-
based host computer. The subject moves within a predefined sensing area jointly covered by the two sensors.
During acquisition, each subject is jointly recorded by the radar and the Kinect V2 sensor. The two sensors are arranged
vertically to observe the same motion region, with the mmWave radar mounted above the Kinect V2 sensor. In the world
coordinate system, the radar position is set to (0, 0.17, 2.65) m, and the Kinect V2 position is set to (0, 0.28, 1.47) m. This
sensor layout allows the radar point clouds and Kinect skeletons to describe the same human motion from a similar frontal
sensing direction. It also provides the basis for the spatial calibration and temporal synchronization described in Section 3.3.
The mmWave radar provides a dense point cloud for each radar frame. To describe both the spatial distribution and the
radar-specific properties of human reflections, each radar point is represented by five attributes, including the Doppler
information, SNR, and 3D spatial coordinates (𝑋,𝑌,𝑍), where 𝑋, 𝑌, and 𝑍 denote the coordinates along the three axes of
the sensor coordinate system. The spatial coordinates describe the geometric distribution of human-related reflections, the
Doppler information provides motion-related cues, and the SNR reflects the reliability of each detected point. Thus, these
attributes form a compact radar representation that combines geometric structure, motion information, and signal quality for
the subsequent 3D-HPE task.
8
In addition to radar point clouds, the acquisition system records synchronized human annotations and image-related data
using the Kinect V2 sensor. The skeleton stream provides 3D joint annotations, whereas the image streams provide RGB
images, depth images, and silhouette images as extensible dataset resources. Their benchmark roles are defined in Section
4.2.
To manage these heterogeneous data streams, a Python-based host computer is used as the central acquisition controller.
It controls the radar and Kinect V2 streams, assigns timestamps to incoming frames, and stores the collected data in a unified
manner. The host computer also monitors the tracking quality of the Kinect V2 skeleton during acquisition. Kinect V2
provides 25 skeletal joints together with their tracking states. A frame is regarded as valid only when at least 22 of the 25
joints are reliably tracked, while frames containing a larger number of inferred or untracked joints are excluded from the valid
data. This quality-control procedure reduces unreliable skeletal annotations caused by unstable tracking or severe self-

acquisition, the radar data is first stored in an SQLite database to support efficient query and data management. After temporal
synchronization and spatial calibration, the aligned radar-skeleton pairs and auxiliary image data are further saved as

3.3. Data calibration and synchronization
The acquisition system records radar point clouds, Kinect V2 skeletons, and image data from different sensors; an aligned
frame-level example is shown in Fig. 4. These data streams cannot be directly paired because they differ in both spatial
coordinates and temporal indices. In the spatial domain, the radar and Kinect V2 sensors have different coordinate definitions,
coordinate origins, and installation poses. Therefore, their measurements must be transformed into a unified world coordinate
system. In the temporal domain, the radar and Kinect V2 streams have different frame rates and data transmission delays.
Therefore, each radar frame must be matched with the nearest Kinect V2 frame after timestamp compensation. Based on these
requirements, the data-preparation process contains two main steps: spatial calibration and temporal synchronization.

4.2. Benchmark protocols
Based on the synchronized sequence-level data in the mmWave-DensePose dataset, this work defines a radar-only
benchmark protocol for 3D-HPE. In this benchmark, the network uses only mmWave radar point clouds as input, and Kinect
V2 skeletons are used as ground-truth annotations. RGB images, depth images, and silhouette images are retained as auxiliary
dataset resources, but they are excluded from training, inference, and evaluation.
The benchmark supports both single-frame and multi-frame 3D-HPE settings. For the multi-frame setting, consecutive
radar frames are sampled from each continuous sequence to form a temporal input window. The frame length can be specified
according to the task requirement. To ensure temporal continuity, a valid temporal sample requires the time interval between
two adjacent frames to be smaller than 300 ms. In this way, the generated samples preserve the motion continuity of the target
subject and provide reliable temporal context for sequence-based 3D-HPE.
To construct the main benchmark, each continuous sequence is first divided into non-overlapping chunks. The chunks
within each sequence are then randomly assigned to the training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 8:1:1. This sequence-
chunk strategy is adopted to preserve local temporal continuity while maintaining comparable data distributions across the
three subsets. Since highly adjacent frames within the same local temporal segment remain in the same chunk, they are not
directly separated into different subsets. Partitioning the chunks within each sequence allows the training, validation, and test
sets to maintain similar subject composition, motion coverage, and sensing conditions. This protocol therefore provides a
consistent setting for controlled comparison among different 3D-HPE methods.
To provide a more comprehensive evaluation of model generalization, we further introduce a subject-independent
protocol. Under this protocol, the dataset is partitioned according to subject identity, and all sequences belonging to the same
subject are assigned exclusively to the training, validation, or test set. The three subsets therefore contain mutually exclusive
subjects, and the complete motion sequences of the test subjects remain unseen during training. While the sequence-chunk
protocol provides a controlled comparison under closely matched data distributions, the subject-independent protocol further
evaluates the ability of the models to generalize to unseen subjects. The same subject-level partition is used for all compared
methods to ensure a consistent comparison.
For network input, each radar frame is normalized to a fixed number of points. If the number of points exceeds the
required size, farthest point sampling (FPS) is used to downsample the point cloud and preserve its spatial coverage. If the
number of points is insufficient, zero padding is applied to complete the input. As a result, all radar frames are represented
with a consistent input size, which enables batch-based network training while retaining the main spatial structure of the
original point cloud. The point features include 3D position, Doppler information, and SNR, as described in Section 3.2.
The benchmark uses a 19-keypoint skeletal representation as the pose output. Fig. 7 illustrates the anatomical definition
and index order of the 19 keypoints. This definition is used consistently for Kinect V2 skeleton annotation, network prediction,
and per-joint evaluation. Therefore, the keypoint indices in the experimental results correspond to the same skeletal layout.

5. The part-aware spatio-temporal network (PAST-Net) for dense radar pose estimation
Building on the dense radar observations described in Section 4.1, this section introduces PAST-Net for mmWave radar
3D-HPE. Although dense radar point clouds provide richer spatial information, their distributions over the human body remain
non-uniform due to different electromagnetic scattering properties and motion-induced responses. As a result, weakly
reflective or slightly moving regions, such as limbs and hands, may still suffer from local point loss and incomplete
observations. To address this issue, PAST-Net converts frame-level radar features into structured latent part tokens and further
models their temporal evolution across consecutive frames. This design uses dense radar observations while reducing the
16
influence of incomplete local point distributions. The following subsections define the problem formulation and describe the
network design in detail.

Fig. 8 presents the data flow of PAST-Net. The network contains four core modules: frame-level point-cloud feature
extraction, skeleton-aware part-token spatial feature extraction, part-wise temporal modeling, and global-part keypoint
regression. First, the frame-level feature extraction module encodes each radar point-cloud frame into a compact global
representation. Then, the skeleton-aware spatial modeling module reorganizes the global feature into several body-region-
guided part tokens, providing a more structured spatial representation than a single global feature. After spatial feature
extraction, the part-wise temporal modeling module collects the same part token across the input window and applies temporal
self-attention along the frame dimension. This design allows each part token to model its own temporal evolution and helps
introduce motion continuity into weakly observed regions. Finally, the global feature of the target frame and the corresponding
part-token representations are fused for keypoint regression. As a result, the whole network links frame-wise point-cloud
encoding, part-token spatial organization, part-wise temporal modeling, and target-frame pose prediction in a unified pipeline.
It should be noted that the part tokens are not explicitly supervised as deterministic anatomical parts. Instead, they are guided
by coarse body-structure priors and are learned under keypoint-level supervision.

The methodological design of PAST-Net focuses on the part-wise organization of both spatial and temporal radar
features. Instead of directly applying temporal modeling to a single global frame representation, PAST-Net first re-projects
the frame-level human feature into a compact set of latent skeleton-aware part tokens. The spatial module models the
interactions among these tokens to obtain complementary latent part representations. Subsequently, tokens of the same type
are collected across consecutive frames, and their temporal evolution is modeled independently. Therefore, PAST-Net uses a
strategy of spatial reorganization followed by part-wise temporal modeling, rather than extracting a unified global temporal
feature from the complete human representation.
5.3. Frame-level feature extraction module
Each radar frame is an unordered point set. Therefore, the network first requires a point-cloud encoder to extract a
compact frame-level representation. In this work, PointNet++ [48] is used as the frame encoder. This choice is based on our
preliminary evaluation of several single-frame point-cloud networks, including Point Transformer [60], PointMLP [61],
DGCNN [62], and PointNet++. Under the 19-keypoint 3D-HPE setting, PointNet++ provides the lowest MLE among these
candidates.
This result indicates that hierarchical local feature aggregation is important for frame-level radar point-cloud encoding.
PointNet++ meets this requirement by learning local neighborhood features at multiple spatial scales and progressively
aggregating them into a global frame representation. In this way, it preserves useful local geometric cues while maintaining
permutation invariance to unordered radar points.
For the 𝑡th radar point-cloud frame 𝑷𝑡, the frame-level encoder produces a global frame-level feature 𝒈𝑡 as follows:

where 𝐸f( ⋅ ) denotes the frame-level point-cloud encoder and 𝐷g denotes the dimension of the global frame-level feature.
The same encoder is applied to each frame in the input window. Therefore, the temporal window is represented by a set of
global frame-level features 𝑮:
𝑮 = {𝒈1,𝒈2,…,𝒈𝑇} (13)
This representation preserves the unordered nature of the radar points and provides a compact global description for each

6. Experiments
This section evaluates PAST-Net on the mmWave-DensePose dataset. The experiments first introduce the
implementation setting and evaluation protocol. Then, PAST-Net is compared with representative radar-based 3D-HPE
methods under the same training setting. To examine the reproducibility and statistical robustness of the reported performance,
we further repeat the complete training and evaluation procedure with different random seeds for PAST-Net and the strongest
competing multi-frame method. After that, ablation experiments analyze the contribution of the main network components.
A controlled input point-count analysis is further conducted to quantify the influence of radar point-cloud observations on
pose estimation performance. Finally, qualitative visualization and error analysis are provided to further explain the
performance differences.
6.1. Experimental setup
Unless otherwise specified, all experiments follow the sequence-chunk benchmark protocol defined in Section 4.2. Under
this protocol, each continuous sequence is divided into non-overlapping chunks of 200 frames, and the chunks within each
sequence are randomly assigned to the training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 8:1:1. Each chunk is assigned exclusively
to one subset, which preserves local temporal continuity and keeps highly adjacent frames within the same local segment
together. This sequence-chunk protocol is used as the default setting for the main method comparison and ablation studies.
The complementary subject-independent protocol is evaluated separately in Section 6.3 to further examine generalization to
unseen subjects.
For temporal 3D-HPE, this work uses five consecutive radar frames as the network input, and the target pose is defined
on the last frame of the input window. A temporal window is regarded as valid only when the time interval between adjacent
frames is smaller than 300 ms. Under this setting, the mmWave-DensePose dataset provides 533 856 valid five-frame samples,
including 381 716 training samples, 75 889 validation samples, and 76 251 test samples. This setting provides sufficient
temporal context while keeping the prediction target well defined.

Each radar frame was normalized to a fixed number of points before being fed into the network. Unless otherwise

all available observations while maintaining a fixed-size input for batch training. If the number of points exceeded the required
size, farthest point sampling (FPS) was used to downsample the point cloud and preserve its spatial coverage. If the number

point cloud. The point features included 3D position, Doppler information, and SNR.

output setting of the mmWave-DensePose benchmark. For the proposed PAST-Net, the model was trained for 200 epochs

MLE, which is mathematically equivalent to MPJPE, was used as the primary evaluation metric. During evaluation,
MLE (MPJPE) was computed in meters as the average Euclidean distance between the predicted 3D keypoints and the
reference keypoints. PA-MPJPE was additionally calculated after per-pose Procrustes alignment with translation, rotation,
and uniform scaling, without reflection. Lower MLE (MPJPE) and PA-MPJPE values indicate better localization accuracy.
For readability, both MLE (MPJPE) and PA-MPJPE have been converted from meters to centimeters in the comparison tables.
PCK was evaluated using the threshold 𝛿max = 150 mm and is denoted as PCK@150mm, while AUC was computed over
the continuous threshold interval from 0 to 𝛿max and is denoted as AUC@150mm. Both PCK@150mm and the normalized
AUC@150mm are reported as percentages, with higher values indicating better performance.
6.2. Comparison with existing methods
To evaluate the overall performance of PAST-Net, we compare it with representative point-cloud and radar-based 3D-
HPE methods. The comparison methods were selected to include both established point-cloud baselines and recent
representative radar-based approaches available during the preparation of this work. For general point-cloud learning,
DGCNN [62], PointTransformer [60], and PointMLP [61] are included to represent dynamic graph-based feature extraction,
Transformer-based point-set encoding, and multilayer perceptron (MLP)-based point-cloud learning, respectively. For radar-
based 3D-HPE, mmPose-FK [52], Dual-Path CNN–BiLSTM [21], PntPoseAT [53], PoseGraphNet [51], and Global–Local
Network [30] were selected as representative methods. These approaches cover voxelized convolutional processing, recurrent
temporal modeling, attention-based temporal learning, forward-kinematics constraints, graph-based pose priors, and point-
cloud completion with global–local feature learning. Therefore, the selected methods provide broad and complementary
coverage of the main input representations and modeling paradigms relevant to radar point-cloud-based 3D-HPE.
Since several original methods were designed for different radar representations or keypoint settings, they were
reproduced on the mmWave-DensePose dataset. For each reproduced method, the model was trained sufficiently on the
mmWave-DensePose dataset according to the training strategy described in the corresponding original paper. In addition,
model-specific settings were adjusted when necessary to match the input format and the 19-keypoint output setting of the
mmWave-DensePose benchmark. For methods that required voxelized inputs or fixed-size point-cloud inputs, the
voxelization boundary and point normalization were defined according to the spatial range of the mmWave-DensePose dataset.
This setting ensured that the comparison reflected the model design rather than differences in input availability.

reduces MLE and PA-MPJPE by 22.14% and 20.36%, respectively, while improving PCK@150mm by 1.10 percentage points
and AUC@150mm by 7.38 percentage points.

6.3. Subject-independent evaluation
The sequence-chunk protocol used in the main benchmark maintains similar data distributions across the training,
validation, and test sets, but different chunks from the same subject may occur in different subsets. To further evaluate the
generalization ability of the compared methods, we conducted an additional experiment using the subject-independent
protocol defined in Section 4.2. Under this setting, all data from one subject were restricted to a single subset. Therefore, the
test subjects and all of their motion sequences remained unseen during training. All compared methods were retrained using
the same subject-level partition.
As shown in Table 7, PAST-Net achieves the lowest MLE under both evaluation protocols. Under the subject-
independent setting, PAST-Net has an MLE of 4.53 cm. PointMLP becomes the strongest overall baseline with an MLE of
5.31 cm, indicating a 14.78% relative error reduction by PAST-Net. Among the multi-frame baselines, PntPoseAT achieves
28
the lowest MLE of 6.18 cm, while PAST-Net further reduces the error by 26.77%. These results show that the performance
advantage of PAST-Net is preserved when the test subjects and their complete motion sequences are excluded from training.

The two groups of methods exhibited different changes when the partition protocol is changed. All evaluated multi-
frame methods showed increased errors under the subject-independent protocol. The original sequence-chunk protocol
prevents identical local chunks from appearing in different subsets, but different segments from the same continuous recording
may still share subject-specific characteristics, radar reflection patterns, and similar motion dynamics. Multi-frame methods
explicitly learn temporal relationships across consecutive frames and are therefore more sensitive to the removal of these
correlations. The subject-independent protocol requires temporal representations to generalize to completely unseen subjects
and consequently produces higher errors for these methods.
In contrast, the single-frame methods show moderately lower MLEs under the subject-independent protocol. This result
does not indicate that subject-independent evaluation is generally easier. The two partition protocols produce different test-
set compositions with respect to subjects, poses, motions, and radar observations. Since single-frame methods process each
frame independently, they do not directly exploit temporal continuity between different chunks of the same sequence and are
less sensitive to the removal of sequence-level temporal correlations. Their moderate improvement is therefore more
reasonably attributed to variation in the frame-level distribution of the held-out subjects. Overall, the subject-independent
29
experiment provided a stricter assessment of cross-subject generalization and confirms that PAST-Net retains the best
performance among all compared methods.
6.4. Ablation study
After verifying the overall performance, we further analyzed the contribution of the main network designs. The ablation
study contained two parts. First, module-level ablation was conducted to examine the effects of skeleton-aware part-token
spatial feature extraction and part-wise temporal self-attention. Then, input-feature ablation was performed to evaluate
whether radar-specific attributes provide additional information beyond 3D coordinates.
As shown in Table 8, the module-level ablation results separately verify the contributions of the two main designs of
PAST-Net. The baseline directly performed keypoint regression using the global frame-level representation and achieved an
MLE of 5.17 cm. After the skeleton-aware spatial module was introduced, the global representation was reorganized into
multiple latent part tokens, reducing the MLE to 4.39 cm. This improvement indicates that part-token-based spatial feature
organization provides a more effective representation than direct regression from a single global feature. Based on the spatially
organized part tokens, the part-wise temporal module further reduced the MLE from 4.39 to 4.08 cm. Instead of extracting a
unified global temporal feature, this module independently models the temporal evolution of each token type. The additional
improvement indicates that part-specific temporal information provides complementary cues for target-frame pose estimation.
Therefore, the results in Table 8 confirm that both skeleton-aware spatial reorganization and part-wise temporal feature
extraction contribute to the final performance of PAST-Net.

6.5. Effect of input point count
To quantitatively examine the effect of the number of radar point-cloud observations, we conducted a controlled input
point-count experiment while keeping the PAST-Net architecture, training settings, and evaluation protocol unchanged. Each
radar frame was normalized to a fixed input size. FPS was applied when the number of valid points exceeded the specified
input size, whereas zero padding was used when the number of valid points was insufficient. PAST-Net was separately trained
with 25, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150, and 300 input points per frame.
As shown in Table 10, the localization error exhibits a clear overall decrease as the number of input points increases.
Increasing the input size from 25 to 50 points reduces the MLE from 5.23 to 4.38 cm, indicating that extremely sparse
observations provide insufficient spatial constraints for stable keypoint localization. The error continues to decrease as more
points are retained, gradually approaching saturation after approximately 100–150 points. The minor fluctuation between the
100-point and 125-point settings is only 0.003 cm and does not alter the overall trend.

The 300-point setting is used in the main experiments because the vast majority of frames contain fewer than 300 valid

Input point count alone, however, does not fully characterize radar point-cloud quality. The point clouds used in this

improved angular sensing capability of the radar system produces human-related reflections with broader and more continuous
body-region coverage. FPS reduces the number of points while retaining their spatial distribution. Therefore, a downsampled
point cloud with a given number of points is not equivalent to a natively sparse point cloud generated by a sensing system
with lower spatial resolution or weaker human-body detection capability. The experiment demonstrates that increased point
availability benefits pose estimation, while the retained performance under downsampling further reflects the informative
human-body representation provided by the proposed radar prototype.
7. Discussion
The mmWave-DensePose dataset and PAST-Net provide a complete radar-only framework for 3D-HPE. The dataset
improves the spatial observation quality of radar sensing, while PAST-Net improves the representation ability of dense but
non-uniform radar point clouds. This section further discusses the value of dense radar point clouds, dataset reusability,
annotation uncertainty, and engineering applications.
7.1. Value of dense radar point clouds
The dataset analysis in Section 4.1 shows that the customized radar system improves both the quantity and the structural
usefulness of valid human-related radar observations. This property matters for engineering deployment because the
acquisition prototype produces geometric inputs that are directly usable by point-cloud learning models, rather than merely
increasing raw radar data volume or admitting more low-quality detections. The resulting point clouds therefore support both
data-driven model training and interpretable inspection of human-related radar observations.
To quantitatively examine the contribution of observation quantity, the point-count analysis in Section 6.5 evaluates
the pose estimation performance under different numbers of retained radar points while keeping the sensing configuration
unchanged. The MLE generally decreases as more human-related points are provided to the network, with the largest
improvement occurring in the low-density range. The performance then gradually approaches saturation when approximately
100–150 points are available. This trend is consistent with the dataset mean and median point counts of 164 and 165,
respectively, indicating that most frames provide sufficient observations for stable keypoint estimation.
However, the number of input points alone does not fully characterize the sensing quality of a radar point cloud. Although
point density and spatial resolution are related, they describe different aspects of the sensing process. The point-count
experiment controlled the number of observations presented to the network, whereas radar spatial resolution determines
whether reflections from adjacent body regions can be separated during data acquisition. The customized radar provides
range, azimuth, and elevation resolutions of 0.06 m, 1.30°, and 2.24°, respectively, which contribute to improved spatial
separability and body-region coverage in the source observations. Consequently, point clouds downsampled from these dense
observations should not be regarded as equivalent to point clouds natively acquired by a sparse or lower-resolution radar.
Farthest point sampling reduces the number of retained points while preserving the spatial coverage available in the original
dense observation. In contrast, a natively sparse or lower-resolution radar may produce a similar number of points but provide
weaker body-region coverage and lower spatial separability. Therefore, the controlled experiment primarily quantified the
sensitivity of PAST-Net to observation quantity, whereas the sensing advantage of the customized radar arises from both the
effective point density and the spatial quality of the original detections.
Nevertheless, dense radar point clouds do not remove all observation difficulties. Radar reflections are still affected by
electromagnetic scattering properties, motion amplitude, and body-part size. Thus, small or weakly moving regions may still
generate incomplete observations. This observation motivated the design of PAST-Net. Instead of relying only on a single
32
global frame feature, the network introduces skeleton-aware part-token representations and part-wise temporal modeling to
improve pose estimation under non-uniform point distributions.
7.2. Dataset reusability
The mmWave-DensePose dataset is designed not only for PAST-Net but also for future radar-based human sensing
studies. Beyond the radar-only benchmark, the retained RGB images and other auxiliary data increase the reusability of the
dataset. This multimodal structure supports several possible research directions. First, the synchronized image and depth data
provide references for visualization and annotation quality analysis. Second, the image-related data can support cross-modal
learning between radar and vision. Third, the sequence-level organization makes the dataset suitable for temporal modeling,
motion analysis, and sequence-based representation learning. Therefore, the dataset provides a reusable benchmark for both
radar-only and multimodal human sensing tasks.
The explicit separation between the radar-only benchmark and auxiliary dataset resources also keeps future comparisons
traceable.
7.3. Annotation uncertainty and evaluation interpretation
Kinect V2 skeletons are used as reference ground-truth annotations in the proposed benchmark. Previous validation
studies have shown that Kinect V2 skeletal tracking is subject to measurement uncertainty, and its accuracy can be affected
by factors such as joint location, motion type, viewing condition, and self-occlusion [63–65]. To improve annotation quality,
the acquisition system applies a tracking-quality criterion, as described in Section 3.2, and only frames with at least 22 reliably
tracked joints among the original 25 Kinect V2 joints are retained as valid samples. This filtering removes frames with
extensive inferred or unsuccessful tracking and reduces obvious annotation failures under unfavorable sensing conditions.
Nevertheless, Kinect V2 remains a markerless reference system and cannot be regarded as an error-free measurement of
the physical joint centers. Therefore, the localization errors reported in this work should be interpreted as errors relative to
the Kinect-based reference annotations rather than absolute errors relative to the unknown physical joint positions. Since all
compared methods were trained and evaluated using exactly the same reference annotations, the benchmark provides a
consistent basis for controlled method comparison. However, annotation uncertainty may increase the absolute error floor and
reduce the discriminability of very small numerical performance differences. Accordingly, small differences between methods
should be interpreted with appropriate caution.
7.4. Engineering applications
From an engineering perspective, dense radar point-cloud 3D-HPE has potential value in scenarios where visual sensing
is limited by privacy, illumination, or environmental conditions. mmWave radar can sense human motion without relying on
visible texture, so it is suitable for human monitoring, rehabilitation assistance, smart environments, and safety-related human
sensing. Pose-derived keypoints have also demonstrated practical value for automated activity analysis in real engineering
environments [4]. Compared with image-based systems, radar-based systems also provide a more privacy-preserving sensing
form because they do not directly record detailed visual appearance. The acquisition prototype also reflects an engineering-
oriented design. Instead of transmitting raw radar data to the host computer, the radar system embeds the point-cloud
generation pipeline and directly outputs radar point clouds through Ethernet using UDP. This design reduces data transmission
pressure and provides a compact geometric representation for real-time processing. Therefore, it is more suitable for online
3D-HPE than a raw-data transmission scheme with a large data volume.
The real-time applicability was further demonstrated by deploying PAST-Net on a low-compute NVIDIA T400 graphics
processing unit (GPU). As shown in the supplementary video, the deployed system outputs radar point-cloud-based pose
predictions in real time. The demo includes normal motion sequences, occlusion scenarios, unseen subjects, and motions that
are not included in the mmWave-DensePose dataset. In these cases, PAST-Net still produces temporally stable and
anatomically plausible pose predictions from radar point clouds. These results indicate that the proposed system has practical
real-time potential and shows promising robustness under cross-subject, cross-motion, and partially occluded conditions.
8. Conclusions
This paper presented dense mmWave radar point clouds as a practical sensing basis for privacy-preserving 3D-HPE. A
customized radar acquisition prototype was constructed to generate human-related point clouds; the resulting mmWave-
DensePose dataset provides synchronized radar point clouds, Kinect V2 skeleton annotations, and auxiliary image resources
for future extension. To improve keypoint localization from dense but non-uniform radar observations, this paper further
proposed PAST-Net, a network that first extracts frame-level point-cloud features and then reorganizes them into skeleton-
aware part-token representations. These part-token representations provide body-region-guided intermediate features without
requiring explicit body-part labels. Part-wise temporal self-attention then models the motion continuity of each part-token
representation across consecutive radar frames, and the final global-part feature supports target-frame keypoint regression.
33
Experiments on the mmWave-DensePose benchmark showed that PAST-Net achieved the lowest MLE among representative
radar-based 3D-HPE methods, with an average MLE of 4.08 cm. Compared with the strongest reproduced baseline, PAST-
Net reduced the localization error by 22.14%. Ablation studies showed that skeleton-aware spatial modeling, part-wise
temporal modeling, and radar-specific point attributes each contributed to the final performance. Qualitative results further
showed more stable skeleton predictions in weakly observed body regions. These findings indicate that dense mmWave radar
point clouds, together with structured spatio-temporal modeling, enable fine-grained radar-only 3D-HPE. Future work can
extend this benchmark to more complex indoor scenes, higher-accuracy annotations, multi-person settings, and multimodal
or cross-modal learning based on the retained auxiliary resources.
