The surge in the number of nongeostationary satellites has in-
tensified interference issues. Interference detection in satellite com-
munication with onboard processing is critical, as it relies heavily
on accurately identifying interference events to ensure seamless and
reliable data transmission. This article delves into the critical problem
of onboard interference detection and identification within satellite
communication systems. A scenario comprising a satellite that re-
ceives radio frequency (RF) interference from a ground terminal not
belonging to its network is considered. Conventional techniques, such
as energy detection, have shown difficulties in detecting weak inter-
ferences. In this context, artificial intelligence (AI)-based techniques
are a promising solution to enhance performance when addressing RF
interference. In particular, the case of brain-inspired spiking neural
networks (SNNs) for energy-efficient implementations is studied. The
primary contributions of this work encompass the creation of a labeled
dataset for interference detection in noisy channel environments, the
implementation of both SNN and convolutional neural network (CNN)
models for interference detection and classification, and a thorough
evaluation of their performance for onboard interference detection
and identification in actual commercially available AI-acceleration
chipset of Xilinx and Intel’s research neuromorphic processor, Loihi 2.
The results confirm that SNNs implemented on neuromorphic chips
can offer orders of magnitude improvements in energy efficiency com-
pared to CNNs implemented on specialist hardware while maintaining
accuracy.
I. INTRODUCTION
Satisfying the ever-increasing demand for Internet-
based applications poses a number of challenges. Among
these, terrestrial networks are not well suited to deliver
wireless connectivity to remote areas and regions with poor
terrestrial infrastructure [1]. To address these issues and
satisfy the increasing need for global coverage, both the
research community and industry have shown keen interest
in deploying satellite communication (SatCom) constella-
tions [2]. Several prominent companies, including OneWeb,
SpaceX, and Amazon, are notably deploying large-scale
satellite constellations to address this need [2].
Satellite constellations rely on nongeostationary orbit
(NGSO) satellite technology, which benefited from a num-
ber of advances in recent years, leading to a rise in the
quantity of satellites operating in space, as noted in prior
research [3], [4], [5], [6]. Given that these satellites vie
for the same frequency spectrum, the challenge of inter-
ference emerges as a critical factor for their coexistence.
According to a recent report [7], roughly 93% of satellite
operators experience interference annually, with over half
encountering it monthly. Detecting and understanding the
specific portion of the spectrum affected by interference is
a significant initial step in addressing this issue.
In previous studies [8], [9], a satellite interference de-
tection method based on energy analysis was introduced.
The authors specifically explored the energy detection (ED)
technique to determine the presence or absence of inter-
ference. An alternative approach proposed in [10] utilizes
compressive sensing to approximate the signal’s power
spectrum. In addition, other studies, such as [11], concen-
trate on localizing interference by utilizing techniques like
time difference of arrival or frequency difference of arrival.
Currently, the spectrum allocation in SatCom follows a

Many previous works leveraged the use of cognitive
radio (CR) technology as a way to dynamically utilize the
spectrum in SatCom [3], [12], [13], where the services
are divided into primary and secondary, and the secondary
services have to operate within the quality-of-service (QoS)
requirements of the primary system. There are two main
categories of CR networks: underlay and overlay [14]. In
underlay CR networks, the secondary system can oper-
ate over the same frequency and simultaneously as the
primary system, provided that its operation does not re-
sult in substantial interference to the point of hampering
the QoS guarantees of the primary system. In this mode,
some QoS parameters must be communicated between the
different systems, which is usually done by a centralized
third-part manager. This adds overhead, delay, and cost.
On the other hand, in overlay CR networks, the secondary
system continuously monitors the spectrum to detect white
spaces (parts of the spectrum that are not currently being
used by the primary system). This, however, requires the
knowledge of some statistical characteristics of the primary
signal as well as the channels [15]. In other words, different
spectrum detection blocks are needed for different signals,
which is not sustainable as the number of services grows
and varies [16].
Machine learning (ML)-based solutions have recently
been proposed to address issues encountered in SatCom
systems [17]. Such solutions offer a number of desirable
properties, among which their capability of operating in
real time [18], [19], [20], e.g., for interference detection
through analysis of the received signal’s spectrum. These
ML-based solutions have shown considerable improve-
ments compared to conventional techniques, such as ED,
which struggle with detecting weak interferences [21].
Another conventional method is cyclostationary detection,
which can overcome some limitations of ED in low-signal-
to-noise-ratio (SNR) environments. For example, Dimc
et al. [22] explored cyclostationary feature detection for
mobile satellite transmissions. Although this method is
more effective in low-SNR conditions, it demands high time
and energy complexity due to the need to calculate all cycle
frequencies [20]. This complexity poses significant chal-
lenges in fast time-varying scenarios, such as those involv-
ing rapidly moving NGSO satellites, where the interference
impact on the desired signal is relatively brief. In addition,
techniques like matched filtering offer better sensitivity
and specificity in detecting known signal patterns but also
suffer from high computational costs and require precise
synchronization, which is difficult to maintain in dynamic
satellite environments [23]. These limitations highlight the
advantages of ML-based solutions, which provide more
robust and adaptive interference detection capabilities with
lower computational demands. ML-based algorithms have
proven advantageous even with low SNR [24]. Moreover,
ML models can be trained using data on the ground, before
being uploaded onboard the satellite. This allows one to
shift most of the processing to the ground, where computa-
tional power and energy expenditure are not issues [25]. In
addition, a single ML model can be used to detect several
interference signals, which results in a reduction in the
number of detection blocks and thus computational power
needed to detect several interference signals.
Despite these advantages, a significant drawback of
using ML models for satellite onboard processing is their
need for substantial initial training data and periodic up-
dates, which can be challenging to manage remotely and
may require significant bandwidth and time for model up-
dates [18]. Moreover, another issue of using ML models
in satellite onboard processing is their requirement for
substantial computational resources and power, which can
be challenging to manage within the limited onboard capa-
bilities of satellites.
A promising solution to address these limitations, in-
cluding high energy expenditure, large memory require-
ments, and inference latency, can be found in brain-inspired
spiking neural networks (SNNs) [26]. Operating over time
using binary spike-based signaling, SNNs offer exceptional
energy efficiency compared to traditional ML algorithms,
which are notorious for their power consumption—even
during inference [27]. This characteristic becomes particu-
larly valuable in resource-constrained environments such
as satellites, where power budgets are highly restricted
and hardware must be optimized for thermal and radiation
constraints.
Neuromorphic processors (NPs), such as Intel’s Loihi 2,
support event-driven massively parallel processing that nat-
urally aligns with the sparse and asynchronous nature of
satellite signal processing tasks. These chips enable real-
time onboard inference, significantly reduce the need for
offloading computations to ground stations, and can also
support online learning. In addition, SNNs exhibit high
scalability and robustness to noise, making them ideal for
spectrum monitoring and interference detection tasks in
dynamic orbital environments.
Recent successful applications of neuromorphic learn-
ing in communication systems further underscore its poten-
tial [26], [28]. These advantages position neuromorphic-
based deployment as a compelling architectural choice
for enhancing onboard processing performance in next-
generation SatCom systems.
Therefore, in this article, we address the challenge of
detecting and identifying the interference within a specific
subband of the spectrum by treating it as a supervised
classification problem using SNN. Based on our previ-
ous work [29], we provide a more detailed approach of
creating a labeled dataset that reduces the feature space
dimension and implementing a benchmark ML model to
evaluate its performance in onboard interference detection
and spectrum classification. The proposed SNN model
predicts interference in various subbands, enabling oper-
ators to switch to interference-free subbands. This article

A. Background and Related Works
The proliferation of NGSO satellite constellations is
aggravating the space interference scenario. With multi-
ple small satellites flying in different orbits and different
inclinations, and belonging to different NGSO operators,
the event of in-line interference is becoming more likely
to occur, both inter-NGSO and between NGSO and geosta-
tionary orbit (GSO) systems, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Particu-
larly for the uplink scenario, transmissions originating from
ground stations can have an impact on adjacent or nearby
satellites, causing service degradation and potentially dis-
rupting the communication. While the radio regulations are
trying to prevent such extreme cases [31], [32], the actual
power–flux density limits are not convincing to satellite
operators [33]. In this context, the detection of interference
event, as well as extracting information of such interference,
is becoming a powerful tool to dynamically reconfigure

satellite systems and mitigate harmful interference situ-
ations. To do so, the use of ML techniques in SatCom
applications has recently gained significant attention.
1) Conventional ML and Neural Networks: In the
literature, conventional ML algorithms as well as neu-
ral networks were used and reported for interference de-
tection. In [34], a long short-term memory network was
implemented to forecast anticipated signal snapshots and
contrast them with the actual received signals. Similarly,
Henarejos et al. [35] explored the use of deep learning tech-
niques, specifically autoencoders, for detecting interference
in terrestrial-to-satellite communication links. On a broader
scale, Kulin et al. [36] focused on wireless signal identifi-
cation, including modulation recognition and interference
detection, using ML algorithms. Unlike online processing
methods, these approaches offer several benefits by employ-
ing data-driven interference detection techniques.
Jamming represents an additional source of interfer-
ence, distinct from the interference caused by the coexis-
tence of legitimate services. Qin and Dovis [25] explored
a k-nearest neighbor method for detecting and categoriz-
ing chirp jamming signals in Global Navigation Satellite
Services systems. This task poses challenges for traditional
approaches due to the diverse nature of jamming signals,
necessitating postprocessing and human-driven analysis,
which in turn restricts timely detection and response to
jamming attacks. Conversely, ML methods offer automated
detection and classification of jamming signals, render-
ing them superior choices in situations demanding prompt
awareness and response.
2) Spiking Neural Networks: In an attempt to im-
plement low-power AI models, NPs and SNNs have re-
cently attracted interest from both the research community
and industry players. SNNs have hence been proposed
for a number of applications in communication systems.
Skatchkovsky et al. [37] proposed to tackle the joint source-
channel coding problem via a probabilistic spiking au-
toencoder architecture. Traditional autoencoders have been
employed in the past for interference detection, as one
can train the system using noiseless data, which will then
report the presence of interference when the difference
between its inputs and outputs exceeds some threshold [38].
However, this does not include the prediction of which
frequency bands are interference-free, which we obtain here
using supervised classification. The Loihi 2 chip has further
been demonstrated to implement spectral analysis on toy
tasks [39].

III. DATASET GENERATION
To enable effective training and evaluation of both the
SNN and the CNN, we developed a labeled dataset tailored
for the task of onboard interference detection in SatCom
systems.Thedatasetsimulatesrealisticsignalreceptionsce-

free conditions across a range of SNRs. Interference sources
include signals designed to mimic uncoordinated transmis-

and Doppler shifts are incorporated to reflect practical
link conditions in LEO environments. Each signal sample
is associated with a known ground truth label, and care

spatiotemporal features. Given the unique characteristics

schemes were explored to enable biologically plausible
input representations suitable for neuromorphic hardware.
This approach ensures that the dataset is not only suffi-
ciently large but also diverse and representative enough to
train data-efficient SNN architectures without overfitting,
thereby supporting real-time low-power onboard inference.

Now, we assess SNNs as a low-power alternative to

implementing the latter poses challenges due to the sig-

with the highest spike rate. We denote this solution
in the following as “multilabel classification.” Note
that, if we were to account for the possibility of more
than one interference source at once, the target size
would then increase exponentially with the number
of subbands.

decoding technique allows us to detect interference
in more than one subband while keeping the number

E. Neuromorphic Hardware Implementation
WedemonstrateinthisworkapracticaluseoftheLoihi2
chip for interference detection. Our investigation was con-
ducted using Intel’s Loihi 2 within the framework of the In-
tel Neuromorphic Research Community (INRC). Loihi 2 is
a research neuromorphic chip that uses asynchronous spik-
ing neurons to implement fine-grained, event-driven, adap-
tive, self-modifying, parallel computations. Loihi 2 com-
prises multiple interconnected neuromorphic cores, each
accommodating numerous artificial neurons, which receive
spikes from other network components. After accumulating
incoming spikes over a specific time period and surpassing
a predetermined threshold, the corresponding core initiates
spike transmission to linked neurons. Earlier spikes enhance
the strength of existing neuronal connections, while later
spikes diminish these connections, gradually reducing con-
nectivity until activity eventually halts.
Manufactured with the Intel 4 process, Loihi2 features
1 million artificial neurons and 120 million synapses on
each chip. Alongside 128 neuromorphic cores, the chip also
includes six processor cores [44]. The previous iteration of
the chip has demonstrated to provide orders of magnitude
improvements in terms of energy efficiency and latency, as
compared to traditional ML workloads [27].

A. Performance Evaluation for the Reduced-Size DFT
With the CNN
ThissectionassessestheperformanceoftheCNNmodel

tion accuracy and training losses. Prediction accuracy is
visualized using a confusion matrix, which, along with the
receiver operating characteristics (ROC), provides valuable
insights into the progress of neural networks.
In this study, the interference detection involves a total
of five subbands. Class 0, represented as [0, 0, 0, 0, 0], cor-
responds to no interference. Likewise, the remaining classes
are labeled according to interference in their corresponding
subbands. We produced 12 000 data samples per class
for training and 3000 samples for validation. To maintain
fairness, the training dataset is balanced across all classes.
The CNN model was trained over 25 epochs using
batch sizes of 128, 32, and 15. The training of traditional
models was conducted on the high-performance computing
resources provided by the University of Luxembourg [45].
The training setup employed the surrogate gradient descent
(SGD) optimizer, configured with a learning rate of 0.01, a
momentum of 0.9, and Nesterov acceleration. Categorical
cross entropy served as the chosen loss function, while the
model’s accuracy functioned as the metric for evaluation.
The model achieved best training accuracy of 97.2% with
a batch size of 32, while the validation accuracy reached
96.9%. The CNN model demonstrates superior perfor-
mance with the dataset prepared considering reduced size
DFT compared to the full-dimensional DFT, attributed to
its less correlated classification [29]. Thus, we focus on the
reduced size DFT for evaluating the CNN’s performance in
this article.
The area under the curve (AUC), true positive rate
(TPR), and false positive rate (FPR) are important perfor-
mance indices for CNNs because they provide valuable
insights into the model’s ability to discriminate between
different classes [30]. The AUC also represented as area
in simulation figures represents the area beneath the ROC
curve, with values ranging from 0 to 1; higher AUC values
signify a superior model. The TPR is the ratio of true
positive instances (instances that are truly positive and
predicted as positive) to the total positive instances, while
the FPR is the ratio of false positive instances (instances
that are truly negative but predicted as positive) to the total
negative instances. At various classification thresholds, one
can observe the relationship between FPR and TPR through
ROC plots, offering insights into the balance between TPR
and FPR and assisting in the selection of an optimal clas-
sification threshold. It is evident from Fig. 11(a) that the
CNN consistently yields an AUC/area close to 1 across all
classes for the interference detection. Fig. 11(b) presents
the normalized confusion matrix obtained for interference
detection using the reduced-dimension DFT. In the case of
the reduced-dimension complex DFT, we observe a ratio
of 0.99 for class 0 and 0.89 for class 5, achieving a ratio of
1 for classes 1–4 when considering the absolute values of

B. Performance Evaluation of the Neuromorphic Model
To evaluate SNNs as an alternative solution to conven-
tional CNNs, we measure metrics of interest by applying
the spike encoding and decoding strategies discussed in
Sections V-C and V-D. More specifically, we focus on
classification accuracy, as well as execution time and energy
expenditure per example as measures of the performance of
the system. We obtain these by implementing SNNs on the
Loihi 2 chip. Results are summarized in Fig. 12, with further
details provided in the Appendix. The highest test accuracy
is obtained by encoding the STFT of the signal with TEMs,
in which case we obtain as high as 99.8% accuracy. The
worst accuracy is obtained by encoding the DFT of the
signal with TEMs, in which case the neuromorphic model
only reaches 96.6% accuracy for binary decoding.
More generally, encoding the STFT of the received
signal either directly in the spiking domain using R&F
neurons or via TEMs is seen to outperform the encoding
of the DFT. This comes at the cost of larger execution times
and energy expenditures, due to the length of the signals
considered. However, encoding with R&F neurons does not
require any preprocessing of the received signals, which is
not accounted for here. Furthermore, it does not require to
wait for the whole signal to be transmitted, as one would
do to compute the DFT. Finally, this technique naturally
matchesthetemporalityofthesignalwiththetimeoperation
of the SNN. In situations where the computation of the DFT
is costly, models incorporating R&F neurons may prove
more efficient across all metrics.
In neuromorphic systems, the choice of encoding and
decoding strategies plays a crucial role in determining the
overall performance, particularly the balance between la-
tency and energy efficiency. While our current implemen-
tation employs techniques as discussed in Sections V-C
and V-D, alternative schemes such as latency coding, time-
to-first-spike, and rank-order coding can significantly influ-
ence system behavior [46].
Latency coding, where information is encoded in the
timing of the first spike, offers the potential to reduce overall
decision latency but may require more precise spike gen-
eration and timing control [46]. Rank-order coding, on the
other hand, emphasizes the order of spikes rather than their
exact timing, providing a tradeoff between computational
simplicity and inference speed. Decoding strategies also
impact performance; population-based decoding methods
can offer robustness against noise but at the expense of
higher computational load. Future work could explore hy-
brid encoding–decoding approaches that dynamically adapt
based on channel conditions or task requirements.
Overall, careful optimization of these strategies presents
a promising avenue to further enhance the performance of
SNNs, striking a more favorable balance between energy
efficiency and latency.

D. Practical Limitations and Emerging Trends in the SNN
SNNs have gained significant attention for their poten-
tial to deliver energy-efficient computation in neuromor-
phic and low-power hardware platforms. However, several
practical limitations remain, particularly in the context of
real-world implementation for onboard satellite systems.
1) Convergence and Training Stability: One of the
fundamental challenges in deploying SNNs is their con-
vergence behavior during training. Due to the discrete and
nondifferentiable nature of spikes, standard backpropaga-
tion cannot be directly applied. While SG methods have im-
proved convergence, they often require meticulous hyperpa-
rameter tuning and longer training durations compared to
traditional artificial neural networks (ANNs). In addition,

convergence can be sensitive to input encoding strategies
and network depth, particularly when deploying SNNs for
time-series classification tasks such as interference detec-
tion.
2) Emerging SNN Learning Algorithms: Recent ad-
vances in SNN learning aim to address these limitations
and improve accuracy, robustness, and efficiency.
1) SGD:Itapproximatesspikegradientstoenableback-
propagation in SNNs. This method has shown im-
proved convergence but may introduce tradeoffs in
biological plausibility.
2) STDP: It is a biologically inspired unsupervised
learning rule that adapts synaptic weights based
on spike timing. While energy efficient, STDP of-
ten requires additional supervisory mechanisms for
complex tasks.
3) Event-driven backpropagation: It introduces tempo-
ral credit assignment in an event-based framework,
allowing SNNs to retain temporal dependencies
without dense updates. This method is promising but
still maturing in terms of scalability.
4) Hybrid SNN-ANN models: Combining ANN layers
(for feature extraction) with spiking layers (for en-
ergy savings) has been explored to balance perfor-
mance and efficiency. However, hardware co-design
and compatibility remain key challenges.
3) Data Volume Sensitivity and Scenario Variability:
Although SNNs offer clear advantages in energy efficiency
and event-driven processing, their practical deployment in
satellite systems faces certain challenges. A primary con-
cern is the volume and diversity of training data required
to ensure robustness under varying interference conditions.
Environmental factors such as SNR variation, non-Gaussian
noise, or diverse interference types can significantly in-
fluence the size and complexity of the dataset needed for
generalization.
In addition, the training of SNNs is still maturing
compared to conventional deep learning methods. While
this work demonstrates promising results using simulated
datasets, field deployment may necessitate retraining or
adaptation using real-world data. Emerging trends such
as hybrid ANN-SNN architectures, online learning, and
advancements in neuromorphic hardware continue to en-
hance the feasibility and performance of SNN-based sys-
tems in practice. Future work will involve comprehensive
field validation and extended data collection across diverse
real-world operating conditions to further assess the ro-
bustness and generalizability of the proposed SNN-based
approach.
Overall, while the SNN paradigm offers significant
potential, especially for embedded and satellite systems,
further research is necessary to improve training reliability,
support larger model scales, and ensure consistent perfor-
mance across varying environments.
VII. CONCLUSION
In this study, we present an in-depth analysis of CNN
models and neuromorphic models using SNNs imple-
mented on a GSO satellite, with the aim of interference
identification and spectrum classification. Using a large
dataset and rigorous comparative tests, we evaluate the
accuracy, latency, and power consumption of both models.
Our results show that SNNs exhibit high flexibility in
signal encoding and decoding, consistently outperforming
conventional ML models in energy efficiency. Specifically,
SNNs implemented on the Loihi 2 chip achieved accuracy
levels comparable to CNNs with significantly lower power
consumption. This energy efficiency is especially crucial
for satellite operations, where energy resources are limited.
The SNN models also demonstrated competitive per-
formance in terms of latency. In half of the scenarios
tested, SNNs outperformed CNNs, making them suitable
for applications requiring fast response times. Coding tech-
niques that show less favorable latency results for SNNs
could still be beneficial for larger scale problems where
DFT computation is inherently slow. This suggests that
as data complexity and size increase, SNNs could be-
come even more advantageous compared to traditional
CNNs.
Beyond energy efficiency and latency, SNNs offer addi-
tional advantages by taking advantage of specialized neu-
romorphic hardware. They are ideally suited to process the
sparse temporal data typical of SatComs and offer a robust
solution for onboard interference detection. Despite current
limitations, such as the need for specialized hardware and
the steep learning curve of neuromorphic computing, on-
going research and technological advances are expected to
mitigate these challenges, facilitating wider adoption. Ex-
perimental results underscore the potential of neuromorphic
computing and SNNs to significantly improve interference
detection in SatCom systems, resulting in improved effi-
ciency and sustainability.
To build on the promising results of this study, several
future research directions are proposed.
1) Investigate the scalability of SNN models for larger
and more complex datasets to further validate their
performance in more challenging interference sce-
narios. This includes developing simulation frame-
works that emulate LEO and MEO environments, ac-
counting for dynamic factors such as Doppler shifts,
rapid changes in link conditions, and handovers.
These simulations will allow testing of SNNs’ in-
ference stability, adaptability, and energy efficiency
under orbital dynamics that are not present in GSO
systems.
2) Develop and test neuromorphic hardware solutions
that are resilient to the radiation harsh environments
encountered in space. Ensuring that NPs can operate
reliably in these conditions is critical to their deploy-
ment in satellite systems.
3) Explore hybrid models that combine the strengths
of CNNs and SNNs to take advantage of the high
