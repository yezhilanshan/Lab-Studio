Abstract
Millimeter-wave (mmWave) point clouds have attracted in-
creasing interest in human sensing due to their robustness,
privacy preservation, and low cost. However, their practi-
cal adoption is hindered by the inherent sparsity of data and
the lack of large-scale annotated dataset. We revisit gener-
ative modeling and propose a unified flow-matching frame-
work mmWaveFlow that unifies enhancement and gener-
ation of mmWave point clouds by learning an invertible
transport between dense and sparse point clouds. We lever-
age paired data and a Cross-modal Latent Alignment mod-
ule to enforce semantic alignment and bridge the modal-
ity gap. We find that condition-free flow matching is more
vulnerable to latent path crossings, which impair trans-
port. Therefore, we propose Origin-Aware Flow Match-
ing (OA-Flow) by conditioning transport on the path ori-
gin to mitigate ambiguity in bidirectional transport. Re-
sults of experiments across multiple datasets demonstrate
the effectiveness of mmWaveFlow for mmWave human point
clouds generation and enhancement. We also observe con-
sistent gains in downstream tasks, highlighting the promise
of our framework for human sensing. Codes are available
at https://github.com/suchang-99/mmWaveFlow.
1. Introduction
Human sensing is a core area in computer vision, cover-
ing various tasks from pose estimation to action recognition.
It supports diverse domains such as embodied AI, human-
computer interaction, and character animation.
Currently, contactless human sensing is evolving rapidly,
freeing users from wearable sensors. Point clouds captured
by mmWave devices stand out among sensing technologies
due to their strong privacy preservation compared to cam-
eras and lower cost than LiDAR, making them a promising
direction for research [51]. However, it presents two pri-
mary challenges: the point clouds are typically sparse and

noisy, and large-scale annotated datasets remain limited.
Recent advances in generative models have led to
diffusion-based methods[33, 52] that aim to enhance
mmWave point clouds to LiDAR-like quality. However,
most methods target scene-level tasks like autonomous driv-
ing, which are not well-suited for fine-grained human sens-
ing tasks. Moreover, they typically sample from Gaussian
noise and treat mmWave data as a conditioning signal, po-
tentially overlooking its inherent structure information.
To address data scarcity, other work[7, 9, 44] simu-
lates mmWave data from human meshes via physics-based
model. However, such approaches rely on high-fidelity 3D
meshes, which limits their scalability and diversity.
While generation and enhancement of mmWave point
clouds are typically treated as separate tasks, we argue that
they are inherently connected. We propose a unified per-
spective: these two tasks are essentially a bidirectional map-
ping between sparse and dense point clouds, as shown in
Fig. 1. Our key insight stems from the observation that flow
matching enables learning invertible transports, which natu-
rally achieve bidirectional mapping between arbitrary distri-

However, learning such a transport is challenging due to
geometric asymmetry: mmWave data are sparse and noisy,
while dense data provide detailed geometric information.
These two modalities exhibit significantly different pat-
terns, hindering the learning of a reliable transport. More-
over, without proper conditioning, the constructed paths
may cross, introducing inconsistent supervision that further
degrades transport quality. Consequently, designing a flow
matching framework tailored to such asymmetric tasks re-
mains an open problem.
To address these problems, we propose two key modules:
(1) Cross-modal Latent Alignment: We employ a shared
cross-attention module to project data from two modalities
into a unified semantic space. These features are then opti-
mized with an InfoNCE objective, enforcing robust seman-
tic correspondence while suppressing spurious matches.
(2) Origin-Aware Flow Matching: We augment flow
matching by conditioning on the path origin, which miti-
gates path crossing issues during cross-domain transport.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to use flow
matching to unify enhancement and generation of sparse
mmWave point clouds for human sensing.
• We propose an end-to-end flow framework for bidi-
rectional mmWave-dense point cloud translation. Our
framework uniquely integrates cross-modal latent align-
ment and origin-aware flow matching, learning consistent
representations without reliance on pretrained models or
high-quality modality prerequisites.
• We conduct extensive experiments on public datasets.
Results show that mmWaveFlow achieves state-of-the-
art performance in mmWave point cloud generation and
enhancement, and suggest its potential for extension to
scene-level tasks. It also improves downstream task per-
formance, further demonstrating its effectiveness.
2. Related Work
Diffusion and Flow Matching. Over the past decade,
diffusion models [20, 37–39] have shown strong perfor-
mance across diverse generative tasks. Diffusion models
progressively add noise to clean source data and train neu-
ral networks to reverse this process. Flow matching mod-
els [2, 24, 26] extend this paradigm by directly modeling
the velocity fields that define flow paths between source
and target distributions via an ordinary differential equa-
tion (ODE). Flow matching theoretically allows for arbi-
trary distributions as the source distribution, which can then
be used for direct evolution. Several methods have been
proposed in this field, such as InterFlow [2], BBDM[23],
and Schrödinger Bridge [36, 40, 55]. They provide impor-
tant theoretical support for bridging two arbitrary distribu-
tions. Recently, CrossFlow[25] and FlowTok[19] extended
this idea to more challenging cross-modal generation (e.g.,
text-to-image and image-to-depth). However, most existing
studies focus on images and text, leaving the transport be-
tween point cloud distributions largely unexplored.
Enhancement of mmWave Radar Point Clouds. Re-
cent studies have explored enhancing mmWave radar point
clouds using both classical signal processing methods and
learning-based methods. On the signal processing side,
methods such as mmEye [50] refine the standard Fast
Fourier Transform processing pipeline. Integrating infor-
mation from multiple frames has become a common strat-
egy for enhancing mmWave point clouds [3, 11]. Learning-
based methods primarily rely on cross-modal supervision
provided by RGB images [46] or LiDAR data [8, 17, 18].
Recent work applies diffusion models to mmWave enhance-
ment by conditioning on mmWave signals and supervis-
ing with LiDAR bird’s eye view (BEV) images [16, 27,
33, 41, 47, 52]. Using BEV images as supervision sig-
nals enables effective reuse of existing diffusion models
for image generation, but projecting point clouds into BEV
inevitably discards height and fine-grained geometric de-
tails. Furthermore, most existing methods are developed for
autonomous-driving scenarios, which focus on large-scale
environments rather than the fine-grained pose and shape
information required for human sensing.
Generation of mmWave Signals. Compared with im-
ages and text, mmWave data are considerably less intu-
itive to interpret, resulting in limited large-scale labeled
datasets. Conventional mmWave signal generation schemes
are mainly based on modeling and simulation. A se-
ries of recent methods [1, 12, 53] integrate vision-based
human reconstruction techniques with signal propagation
models to synthesize signals for human sensing. However,
these pipelines struggle to accurately model complex en-
vironments and material-dependent scattering (e.g., differ-
ent clothing), which severely constrains their performance
in real-world deployments. The mmGPE [44] attempts to
mitigate this sim-to-real gap by leveraging an adversar-
ial module to align simulated and real data distributions.
Other studies [21, 56] design more expressive propagation
and transmission models to generate more realistic signals.
NeRF2
[54] models the signal propagation space with a
deep neural network and is subsequently used to synthe-
size mmWave signals. RF-Diffusion [9] leverages diffusion
models to learn the conditional distribution of mmWave sig-
nals given specific scene configurations and human motion.
Despite these advances, the generalization capability of cur-
rent methods to unseen subjects, poses, and environments
remains limited. Therefore, mmWave signal generation is
still far from being solved and requires further investigation.

3. Preliminaries
We consider a generative model that defines a mapping be-
tween samples z0 from a source distribution p0 to samples
z1 of a target distribution p1 via the ODE on time t ∈ [0, 1]:
dzt = vθ(zt, t)dt. Here, vθ is the velocity field parameter-
ized by a neural network with weights θ. Flow matching
defines a simple forward process by linearly interpolating:
  z_t=tz_1+(1-t)z_0, \label {equ:interp}  (1)
The vθ(zt, t) is trained by minimizing the mean squared er-
ror (MSE) between its output and the ground truth ˆ
vt:
  \hat {v}_t=\frac {dz_t}{dt}=z_1-z_0.  (2)
Once trained, the learned velocity field vθ defines an invert-
ible, continuous flow between p0 and p1. It enables bidirec-
tional transport through the same ODE:
  \quad & z_1 = z_0 + \int _0^1 v_\theta (z_t,t)dt. (3)
Integrating forward in time from z0 ∼ p0 transports p0 to
p1, while integrating backward in time from z1 ∼ p1 trans-
ports p1 back to p0. In practice, the integral is evaluated
numerically by discretizing time into K steps with step size
∆t = 1/K (e.g., Euler updates zt+∆t = zt + ∆t vθ(zt, t)).
In principle, flow matching allows an arbitrary choice of
source distribution p0. However, prior work [13, 32, 35]
typically starts from Gaussian noise z0 ∼ N(0, 1). To
enable conditional generation, the velocity field is condi-
tioned on additional information c via cross-attention mech-
anisms [13, 35], i.e., the model learns vθ(zt, t, c) instead of
an unconditional vθ(zt, t).
4. Methodology
4.1. Overview
Problem Formulation. Let (X0, X1) be a pair of point
clouds, where each is an unordered 3D point set, represent-
ing partial observations of the same human subject captured
at approximately the same time. Here, X0 denotes a dense
point cloud (e.g., from an RGB-D sensor), and X1 denotes a
sparse point cloud (e.g., from an mmWave radar). Our goal
is to learn an invertible transport inducing a bidirectional
mapping ϕ to make ϕ(X0, ∅) ≈ X1 and ϕ(∅, X1) ≈ X0
where ∅ is a placeholder indicating the mapping direction.
mmWaveFlow. The overall pipeline of mmWaveFlow is
shown in Fig. 2. The encoders E0 and E1 map data from dif-
ferent modalities into latent representations with the same
dimensionality. To encourage interaction and alignment
between modalities, we introduce shared learnable queries
that perform cross-attention on the modality-specific latent
representations, promoting a coherent and aligned latent
space while enhancing feature robustness. Then decoders
D0 and D1 decode the latent representations back to the
data space. Together with their corresponding encoders,
they form two codecs that serve as the feature extraction
backbone. Beyond this backbone, we further employ a flow
matching module to learn the transport between the latent
distributions of the two modalities, where an origin-aware
flow matching module is incorporated to alleviate potential
path crossings during training.
4.2. Cross-modal Latent Alignment
The alignment is performed at two levels: dimensional
alignment and semantic alignment.
Dimensional Alignment. Flow matching typically requires

sionality, and the source distribution in flow matching is
Gaussian noise whose dimensionality can be freely spec-
ified. However, in our setting, the source is a real data
distribution whose dimensionality is not under our control.
Therefore, we introduce a Variational Autoencoder (VAE)
to transfer the entire generation process to the latent space.
This not only improves computational efficiency [35], but
also increases the flexibility of mmWaveflow with respect
to the input size, as sparse and dense point clouds often
contain different numbers of points. In addition, recent
token-based 3D generative models [10, 49] have demon-
strated strong performance. Motivated by this, we represent
each point cloud as a set of N latent tokens of dimension
D. Given paired inputs X0 and X1, we use two encoders
with identical architecture but separate parameters to ob-
tain latent tokens f0 = E0(X0) and f1 = E1(X1), where
f0, f1 ∈ RN×D
.
Semantic Alignment. Using a non-Gaussian source distri-
bution in flow matching may pose a risk of semantic mis-
alignment. Currently, most point cloud encoders operate at
the point or patch level and produce a set of latent tokens.
Since point clouds are unordered, these tokens do not fol-
low a canonical ordering across different point clouds. As
illustrated in Fig. 3, tokens at the same position may corre-
spond to different body parts for different inputs, leading
to semantic misalignment. However, flow matching per-
forms element-wise linear interpolation between the source
and target latent representations, implicitly assuming token-
wise alignment. When this assumption is violated, the in-
terpolation may mix unrelated local features, thereby cor-
rupting the supervision signals for the velocity field.
Therefore, semantic alignment of latent representations
across modalities is essential for constructing meaning-
ful interpolation paths and enabling coherent cross-modal
translation. Existing work [19, 25] often operates in a dif-
ferent setting, where one modality (e.g., RGB or language)
is equipped with a powerful pretrained encoder and serves
as a semantic anchor. The other modality is then aligned

4.3. Origin-Aware Flow Matching
Previous studies [19, 25] argue that once the Gaussian
source p0(z) is replaced by a conditional source p0(z | c),
the condition c can be absorbed into the source distribu-
31369
tion. In this view, conditional generation simply samples
z0 ∼ p0(z | c) and then evolves it with an unconditional ve-
locity field v(zt, t) instead of v(zt, t, c), thus reducing the
space–time complexity. However, we observe latent path
crossings in this condition-free setting. Recall that flow
matching constructs paths between the source and target
distributions by linear interpolation, as in Eq. (1). In high-
dimensional spaces, linear trajectories of different paired
samples may pass through the same intermediate state zt.
At such crossings, an unconditional velocity field receives
incompatible supervision and tends to progress toward an
averaged direction, resulting in biased velocity estimation.
As illustrated in the top of Fig. 4, condition-free flow suffers
from latent path crossings: different training paired sam-
ples induce trajectories that cross in latent space, causing
the model to learn an incorrect velocity field around these
crossings. This leads to severe degradation or even failure
in multi-step integration. In the single-step case, however,
the update is dominated by the displacement encoded at the
origin, so the transport can still reach the correct target, even
though the underlying velocity field is imperfect.
To mitigate this problem, we propose origin-aware flow
matching, which conditions the velocity field on the path
origin. Preserving origin information allows the model to
disambiguate crossing paths and learn more accurate veloc-
ity fields. As illustrated in the bottom of Fig. 4, it greatly
reduces path crossings and produces smoother flows.
During training, we randomly select z0 or z1 as the ori-
gin condition with equal probability. This symmetric con-
ditioning strategy enables the model to handle both forward
and backward flows while maintaining origin awareness.
The flow-matching loss is given by:
  \mathcal {L}_{\text {Flow}}=MSE(v_\theta (z_t,t,z_k),\hat {v}_t), k \sim \operatorname {Bernoulli}(0.5)  (5)
This approach mitigates the path crossing problem with
minimal added complexity. Conditioning on the origin pro-
vides unambiguous guidance at intersection points, leading
to more stable training and higher-quality generated results.
4.4. Training mmWaveFlow
Training objective. We train the VAE with a combina-
tion of Chamfer Distance (CD) and Earth Mover’s Distance
(EMD), together with a standard KL regularization term.
The overall VAE loss is defined as:
  \begin {aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\text {VAE}}&=\sum _{i=0}^{1} [ CD(\mathcal {D}_i(z_i), X_i) + EMD(\mathcal {D}_i(z_i), X_i)\\ &+ \lambda _{\text {KL}} KL\left (\mathcal {N}(\mu _{z_i}, \sigma _{z_i}^2) \parallel \mathcal {N}(0, 1)\right )], \end {aligned} 
(6)
where D is the decoder, zi is the latent representation for
the modality i, and λ is the weight of the KL regularization
term. The overall training objective is defined as:
  \begin {aligned} \mathcal {L}= \lambda _{\text {VAE}}\mathcal {L}_{\text {VAE}}+\lambda _{\text {Flow}}\mathcal {L}_{\text {Flow}}+\lambda _{\text {align}}\mathcal {L}_{\text {align}} \label {equ:overallloss} \end {aligned}  (7)
Training strategy. Following prior work [25], we first
jointly train the VAE and the OA-Flow module using the
combined objective L. We stop updating the VAE once it
approximately converges (e.g., after 10 consecutive epochs
with no decrease in reconstruction loss), then freeze it and
continue training only the OA-Flow module. Furthermore,
this joint optimization can be viewed as a multi-task learn-
ing problem, and therefore we employ gradient surgery [48]
to improve convergence stability and overall performance.
5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental Setup
5.1.1. Datasets
Three common and low-cost sources of dense human point
clouds are depth cameras, LiDAR, and simulation from
parametric human models (e.g., SMPL-X [30]). Accord-
ingly, we select three public datasets for evaluation, each
containing mmWave point clouds with dense point clouds
derived from these three sources, respectively.
mmBody [6]. The dataset includes over 100 motions from
20 subjects. The test set includes scenarios captured un-
der challenging conditions such as rain and poor lighting,
which can be used to evaluate the robustness of our method.
We exclude smoke and occlusion scenes as the dense point
clouds are severely degraded or missing. This results in
39,892 training samples and 20,207 testing samples.
MM-Fi [45]. The dataset comprises recordings from 40 hu-
man subjects, covering 27 categories of daily and rehabili-
tation activities. To mitigate the sparse nature of mmWave
point clouds, we accumulate the point clouds of every four
consecutive frames into a single composite frame and dis-
card any composite containing fewer than 50 points, i.e.,
an average of fewer than 10 points per raw frame, which
we consider too sparse. After this filtering, the dataset com-
prises 207,610 training samples and 69,271 testing samples.
mRI [4]. The dataset focuses on simple rehabilitation ex-
ercises performed by 20 subjects in home-monitoring con-
ditions. Ground-truth 3D joint labels are captured by two
Kinect cameras. After alignment and preprocessing, we ob-
tain 102,817 training samples and 25,649 testing samples.
The mmWave point clouds in mmBody are captured by
an Arbe Robotics sensor, while those in MM-Fi and mRI
are collected from the Texas Instruments IWR6843AOP
and IWR1443Boost mmWave radars, respectively. Among
these, the IWR6843AOP is one of the most common and
cheapest commercial mmWave devices.

ality D = 128. The flow network is a 30-layer DiT [31].

5.2. Main Results
To our knowledge, there is no existing work employs a
single model for both enhancing and generating mmWave
human point cloud. Therefore, we select five methods
with open-source code from different fields as baselines.
Specifically, we compare mmPoint [42] (aimed at dense
human point cloud generation using mmWave signals),
RadarHD [33], RadarDiff [52] (both designed for high-
resolution point cloud generation), LiDiff [28] (focused on
3D LiDAR point cloud completion), and Tiger [34] (target-
ing 3D point cloud generation).
Due to the large size of mmWave raw signals, many
datasets such as MM-Fi do not provide the raw signals.
Moreover, LiDAR BEV images may lose information. For a
fair comparison, we replace the mmWave spectrograms and
LiDAR BEV images involved in the comparison methods
with point clouds. For all models affected by this change,
such as UNet in RadarDiff, are replaced with DiT, which
is the same as mmWaveFlow. The inference steps for the
baseline diffusion and flow matching models are set to 50.
The main results are listed in Tab. 1. All baselines
need to be trained twice for M2D and D2M tasks, respec-
tively, while mmWaveFlow only needs to be trained once.
RadarHD ranks the lowest in performance, while mmPoint
and RadarDiff perform moderately. This may be due to the
limited expressive power of the cascaded refinement net-
work used by mmPoint may be inferior to the current dif-
fusion/flow matching model, which limits its performance.
RadarHD and RadarDiff are designed for outdoor scenarios
in autonomous driving settings and have limitations in the
handling of human point clouds. In addition, although LiD-
iff achieves the best result in one evaluation metric (Cham-
fer Distance in the M2D task) on the MM-Fi dataset, its
overall performance ranks second to last. This can be at-
tributed to the fact that LiDiff is designed for the comple-
tion of LiDAR point clouds. It performs well on LiDAR
data provided by MM-Fi, but performs poorly in generating
mmWave-like point clouds. Furthermore, Tiger, as a uni-
versal 3D generative model, may still lack some targeted
designs to handle mmWave point cloud generation and en-
hancement tasks. In contrast, the point clouds produced by
mmWaveFlow consistently retain high quality across both
tasks, demonstrating its strong and stable performance.
We also visualize several examples of generated and en-
hanced mmWave point clouds in Fig. 5.
5.3. Downstream Task Evaluation
We further evaluate the potential of point clouds generated
and enhanced by mmWaveFlow on two downstream tasks:
human mesh recovery and action recognition. This evalua-

5.5. Empirical Analysis of Potential Path Crossings
We randomly sample 1,000 pairs of samples from
three datasets and obtain the corresponding latent tokens
{(z0,i, z1,i)}1000
i=1 . For each pair, we linearly interpolate

kens zt,i. The corresponding real velocity field is denoted
as ẑi. We then compute the cosine similarity between all to-
ken vectors in zt,i and ẑi, and average over tokens to obtain
the sample-level similarities si,j and ŝi,j. when si,j > 0.95
and ŝi,j < 0.7, we consider it as a potential path crossing
and count one occurrence. Fig. 6 shows how the number of
potential path crossings varies over time t across the three
datasets. At t = 0, we have zt = z0, i.e., the latent to-
kens of the dense point cloud. Because three datasets have
high data quality and the learned feature space is relatively
compact, similar samples naturally exist. In contrast, the
feature space of z1 is noticeably looser. It is worth not-
ing that for all three datasets, the number of potential path
crossings first increases and then decreases, which clearly
indicates that the path crossing problem still exists even in
high-dimensional spaces. Note that we only counted 1,000

5.6. Extensibility
The mmWaveFlow can be extended to more general settings
easily. The mmWaveFlow is further evaluated on the Col-
oRadar dataset [22] under the Arpg Lab scenario, adjust-
ing only the input and output dimensions of the VAE. It
achieves a CD of 0.85 m (M2D) and 1.64 m (D2M), com-
pared to 0.96 m of RadarDiff and 1.73 m of RadarHD, as
reported in [33] for the M2D task.
6. Conclusion
In this paper, we present mmWaveFlow, a unified frame-
work for enhancement and generation of mmWave human
point clouds. By leveraging shared queries and cross at-
tention, we project latent representations from different do-
mains into the same feature space to achieve cross-modal
latent alignment. Furthermore, we show that condition-free
training is vulnerable to potential path crossings which was
overlooked in previous work. More importantly, we pro-
pose the origin-aware flow matching to mitigate this prob-
lem by conditioning the velocity field on the path origin. Ul-
timately, mmWaveFlow demonstrates excellent generation
performance on dense point clouds from different sources
(RGB-D, LiDAR, and simulation) and datasets from various
mmWave devices. Extensibility experiments further sug-
gest that mmWaveFlow can be readily extended to scene-
level point cloud generation and enhancement. The evalua-
tion results on downstream tasks also highlight the potential
of mmWaveFlow as a unified framework for human sensing.
