Quantization-Aware Training of Spiking Neural
Networks for Energy-Efficient Spectrum
Sensing on Loihi Chip
Shiya Liu , Nima Mohammadi , Graduate Student Member, IEEE, and Yang Yi , Senior Member, IEEE
Abstract—Spectrum sensing is a technique used to iden-
tify idle/busy bandwidths in cognitive radio. Energy-efficient
spectrum sensing is critical for multiple-input-multiple-output
(MIMO) orthogonal-frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM)
systems. In this paper, we propose the use of spiking neural
networks (SNNs), which are more biologically plausible and
energy-efficient than deep neural networks (DNNs), for spec-
trum sensing. The SNN models are implemented on the Loihi
chip, which is better suited for SNNs than GPUs. Quantization
is an effective technique to reduce the memory and energy
consumption of SNNs. However, previous quantization meth-
ods for SNNs have suffered from accuracy degradation when
compared to full-precision models. This degradation can be
attributed to errors introduced by the coarse estimation of
gradients in non-differentiable quantization layers. To address
this issue, we introduce a quantization-aware training algo-
rithm for SNNs running on Loihi. To mitigate errors caused
by the poor estimation of gradients, we do not use a fixed
configuration for the quantizer, as is common in existing SNN
quantization methods. Instead, we make the scale parameters
of the quantizer trainable. Furthermore, our proposed method
adopts a probability-based scheme to selectively quantize indi-
vidual layers within the network, rather than quantizing all
layers simultaneously. Our experimental results demonstrate that
high-performance and energy-efficient spectrum sensing can be
achieved using Loihi.
Index Terms—Spectrum sensing, spiking neural networks,
quantization, quantization-aware training.

It is challenging to train quantized SNN models due to

II. BACKGROUND
A. Comparison of DNNs and SNNs
DNNs and SNNs are both inspired by the brain, but they
diverge significantly in their neural computations. In biolog-
ical systems, neurons communicate through sparse, uniform
amplitude spike trains of action potentials [5], while DNNs
use continuous real numbers for information transfer. This con-
trast has led to the emergence of SNNs, where information is
conveyed via a sequence of event-driven 1-bit spikes, lever-
aging spike latency and rates for information encoding and
transmission.
The leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) model [22] is the most
commonly used neural model in SNN. It can be expressed in
a differential form as,

III. SPECTRUM SENSING FOR 5G
COMMUNICATION SYSTEM
The radio spectrum, often hailed as one of the most pre-
cious resources, has seen its value skyrocket as it becomes
increasingly scarce. This escalating value has transformed the
radio spectrum into a lucrative investment, one that might be
even taken out of use and warehoused for profit. Typically,
portions of the radio spectrum are auctioned off, granting the
highest bidder, known as the licensed user, exclusive rights to
the specific radio band. However, despite their exclusive rights,
PUs often under-utilize their licensed spectrum portions. This
under-utilization leads to the emergence of “spectrum holes”
or unoccupied bands within the spectrum. The presence of
these spectrum holes underscores the inefficiency in spectrum
usage, especially when the demand for wireless communica-
tion channels is soaring. This scenario accentuates the pressing
need for spectrum occupancy detection. By identifying and
leveraging these unused spectrum portions, we can optimize
the overall utilization of the radio spectrum, ensuring that this
invaluable resource is used to its fullest potential, benefiting
both industries and end-users.
The evolution of wireless communication systems, par-
ticularly the transition from 4G to 5G, has been propelled
by users’ growing demands for higher data rates, reduced
latencies, and more dependable connections [19], [20]. In
response, the industry has explored techniques to optimize
the use of the available spectrum, enhance signal quality,
and boost throughput. MIMO-OFDM has risen as a piv-
otal technology in this pursuit. This is due to the supe-
rior data rates offered by MIMO and the resilience of
OFDM against frequency-selective fading [18]. Consequently,
MIMO-OFDM has been selected as a foundational technol-
ogy for both LTE-Advanced and 5G NR. Building on this
foundation, our study assumes a primary system based on
MIMO-OFDM. We then evaluate our model using signals
captured from the radio band on which this primary system
operates.

B. Underutilization of Spectrum
One of the challenges in MIMO-OFDM systems is the
underutilization of subcarriers, not due to the system’s inabil-
ity to exploit all subcarriers simultaneously, but because the
primary system (PUs) might not have data to transmit at
all times, leading to spectrum inefficiencies [24]. To address
this underutilization, MIMO-OFDM systems can allow ‘sec-
ondary’ users (SUs) to access the unoccupied subcarriers in a
dynamic spectrum sharing (DSS) context, as long as it doesn’t
interfere with the primary system’s operations.

However, there are constraints on SUs. They are only

indeed vacant. If a PU begins transmission on a previously
unoccupied subcarrier, the SU must vacate it. To determine

system. This sensing can be challenged by factors like fading
wireless channels and low signal-to-noise (SNR) ratios, which
can significantly impact the effectiveness of spectrum sensing.
C. Spiking Neural Networks for Spectrum Sensing
There are three classical spectrum sensing methods, which
are matched filtering, energy detection, and cyclo-stationary
feature detection. However, these methods have been shown
to have inadequate detection rates at low SNRs, might require
prior knowledge of the signal, or not be computationally effi-
cient [25], [26], [27]. To overcome these challenges, machine
learning-based methods have been studied [28], [29], [30].
The advantages of machine learning-based methods over clas-
sical methods are summarized as follows. Firstly, the machine
learning-based methods are more adaptive since the surround-
ing environment (e.g., the fading channel) of the cognitive
radio could be learned. Secondly, the decision boundaries of
the spectrum could be better found by machine learning-based
methods [29], [30].
In this work, we introduce an energy-efficient SNN-based
method for spectrum sensing and implement the method on
a Loihi chip. The architecture of spectrum sensing for the
MIMO-OFDM system using SNNs is shown in Fig. 1. To
the best of our knowledge, this is the first work for solving
spectrum sensing in a 5G communication system using Loihi.
Our results demonstrate that a high-performance energy-
efficient SNN model for spectrum sensing can be achieved on
Loihi.
IV. LOIHI CHIP
A. Neuromorphic Computing Chips
Loihi is a neuromorphic research test chip designed by Intel
Labs. It uses asynchronous event-driven fine-grained parallel
computations to improve the speed and the power efficiency
of SNN models [6]. It consists of 128 “neurocores” and each
“neurocore” has 1024 neurons. The chip is fabricated on Intel’s
14 nm process.
Compared to other neuromorphic processors such as
Neurogrid [31] and BrainScaleS [32], Loihi has the follow-
ing advantages. Firstly, Loihi exploits barrier synchronization
to enable chips and cores in a mesh can operate independently
and utilizes barrier messages to synchronize the system to a
global time step. Secondly, Loihi exploits asynchronous event-
driven parallel computations to improve the speed and power
efficiency of SNN models. Thirdly, the behavior of the chip
is guaranteed to be reliable and predictable compared to solu-
tions in the mixed-signal, purely analog, or fully asynchronous
digital domains. Fourthly, this chip is fabricated on advanced
process technology, which makes the chip run faster and has
less silicon area. Lastly, Loihi has better community support
and documentation.
B. Challenges of Deployment on Loihi
Because of the hardware limitations and the non-
differentiable spike activities, mature training algorithms such
as backpropagation cannot be implemented on Loihi. Also,
Loihi only supports integer-only inference with 1-9 bits
precision. Therefore, an off-chip quantization-aware training
algorithm for quantized SNN models is needed. However,
building an off-chip training algorithm to train quantized SNN
models for Loihi is challenging. The accuracy of existing
quantization-aware training algorithms [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]
is still compromised when compared to their full-precision
(FP) counterparts. This decline in accuracy can be primarily
attributed to the imprecise estimation of gradients in quantiza-
tion layers that are non-differentiable. Furthermore, despite the
potential of the unsupervised STDP learning algorithm [13]
to eliminate the need for gradient backpropagation across
layers, these algorithms do not enable the achievement of
high-performance models.
To build an off-chip training algorithm for quantized SNNs
on Loihi, the training algorithm needs to address the follow-
ing issues. Firstly, the training algorithm needs to resolve the
non-differentiable spike activities of SNN models and can

D. Quantization-Aware Training for Spiking Neural
Networks With Probability-Based Scheme
In this subsection, we introduce the quantization-aware
training for SNN models on Loihi. The proposed algorithm
adopts a probability-based scheme to selectively quantize indi-
vidual layers within the network as opposed to quantizing all
layers simultaneously. During the forward pass of each train-
ing step, a layer has a certain probability p to be quantized by
the simulated quantization function Q(·) defined in Eq. (17).
The probability p is a hyperparameter. Therefore, only part of
the layers is quantized during the forward pass rather than all
layers are quantized. During the backward pass, if the weights
of a layer are quantized during the forward pass, Eq. (16)
and Eq. (18) are used to approximate gradients for weights
and scale parameter s, respectively. Otherwise, Eq. (11) is
adopted to compute the gradient. As illustrated in Fig. 4, a
6-layer Spiking SNN model is presented. The representation
employs a color scheme wherein green boxes signify quantized
layers, while blue boxes denote regular, unquantized layers.
During a single training iteration, quantization is exclusively
applied to layers 1 and 3, while all remaining layers are left
unquantized.

E. Input Spike Encoding
A fundamental challenge in SNNs is the conversion of
real-valued inputs into binary spikes. Various coding schemes
have been proposed, including temporal, phase, Burst, and rate
coding methods [35], [36], [37], [38], [39].
Temporal coding assigns a single spike to each neu-
ron, where spike latency varies inversely with input mag-
nitude [37]. When input exceeds a threshold, a spike is
generated, inhibiting subsequent spikes from the same input.
This scheme maps inputs to precise spike timing, offering
advantages in speed and energy efficiency.
Phase coding, as outlined in [38], incorporates tempo-
ral information into spike patterns by assigning varying
weights to different time steps, featuring periodic changes
in spike weight. The number of phases is determined by
the input’s highest magnitude. Phase coding is robust against
input noise, offering a resilient solution for handling input
perturbations.
Burst coding, as investigated by Park et al. [39], conveys
information through short bursts of spikes, improving synap-
tic communication reliability. This coding utilizes the number
of spikes in a burst and the inter-spike intervals to encode
information.
Rate coding converts real-valued inputs into spike trains,
with spike count directly reflecting input magnitude [35].
Inputs are treated as firing rates, resulting in Poisson
spike trains matching input values. This approach encodes
information in spike frequency, representing diverse input
intensities via neuronal firing rates.
In this study, we have chosen to adopt rate coding as the
input encoding method. Rate coding is favored for its robust-
ness, particularly in the context of deep SNN models. While
temporal coding, phase coding, and burst coding have found
applications in shallow networks, their scalability becomes
challenging when confronted with increases in both network
and dataset dimensions [40]. In contrast, rate coding is well-
suited for large-scale settings, and many recent state-of-the-art
SNN models utilize this coding scheme [41]. In our encod-
ing approach, we initially normalize the input data using the
equation,
x =
x − a
b − a
, (19)
where x represents the real-valued input, and a and b denote
the lower and upper bounds of the input, respectively. The
normalized input is subsequently transformed into a Poisson
spike train through a Poisson encoder.

A. Experimental Setup
In our experimental setup, the SNN model is constructed
with two fully connected layers, each consisting of 96 neu-
rons. For the spectrum sensing task, the input and output sizes
are defined as 2. In the MNIST classification task [42], the
input size is 784 and the output size is 10. Similarly, in the
SVHN classification task [43], the input size is 3024 and the
output size is 10. To execute the SNN model, we employ a
single Nahuku Loihi chip. The input data is encoded using a
Poisson encoder. The LIF neuron utilized in our model has a
voltage decay time constant of 10e-3 and a current decay time
constant of 5e-3. The overall architecture of the SNN model
is shown in Fig. 5. The floating-point DNN model used in the
spectrum sensing task has the same architecture as the SNN
model. The DNN models are implemented using Pytorch [44].
Our GPU experiments are conducted on an NVIDIA RTX
A2000. To measure the power consumption, both the total

VII. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we propose an off-chip QAT algorithm for
SNN models running on Loihi. Our QAT algorithm learns
the quantization mapping and makes the scale parameters of
the quantizer trainable. Moreover, the proposed method adopts
a probability-based scheme to selectively quantize individual
layers within the network as opposed to quantizing all layers
simultaneously. The result on Loihi shows that SNN mod-
els trained by the proposed QAT algorithm can achieve better
results than models trained by other algorithms. Meanwhile,
we introduce an energy-efficient SNN-based method for spec-
trum sensing on the MIMO-OFDM system and demonstrate
that energy-efficient spectrum sensing can be achieved on
Loihi. On the spectrum sensing task, the SNN model on Loihi
trained by the proposed QAT algorithm achieves compara-
ble accuracy as the DNN counterpart while having 16 times
less energy consumption per sample. On the MNIST classi-
fication task, our model has better accuracy than the models
trained by eWB, HAQ, EnforceSNN, QSTDP, and Q-SpiNN.
The energy consumption per image of our model on Loihi is
339X, 99X, 46X, 119X, 110X, and 67X less than the eWB,
HAQ, EnforceSNN, QSTDP, Q-SpiNN, and our SNN model
on GPU.
