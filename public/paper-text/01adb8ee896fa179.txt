With the rapid growth of Internet of Things (IoT) networks, ubiq-
uitous coverage is becoming increasingly necessary. Low earth orbit
(LEO) satellite constellations for the IoT have been proposed to pro-
vide coverage to regions where terrestrial systems cannot. However,
LEO constellations for uplink communications are severely limited by
the high density of user devices, which causes a high level of cochannel
interference. This research presents a framework that utilizes spiking
neural networks (SNNs) to detect IoT signals in the presence of uplink
interference. The key advantage of SNNs is the extremely low power
consumption relative to traditional deep learning (DL) networks.
The performance of the spiking-based neural network detectors is
compared against state-of-the-art DL networks and the conventional
matched filter detector. Results indicate that both DL and SNN-based
receivers surpass the matched filter detector in interference-heavy
scenarios, due to their capacity to effectively distinguish target sig-
nals amid cochannel interference. Moreover, our work highlights the
ultralow power consumption of SNNs compared to other DL methods
for signal detection. The strong detection performance and low power
consumption of SNNs make them particularly suitable for onboard
signal detection in IoT LEO satellites, especially in high interference
conditions.

I. INTRODUCTION
Internet of Things (IoT) use cases are growing at an
unprecedented rate, where terrestrial networks are unable
to supply coverage for applications like smart farming and
parcel tracking in rural remote areas, and areas with extreme
environments [1]. To accommodate the growing need for
global coverage, low earth orbit (LEO) satellite constella-
tions have been of great interest to the research community
and industry alike [2] where many large companies, such as
OneWeb, SpaceX, and Amazon [3], [4], [5], are currently
deploying mega satellite constellations. For an alternative
method to provide global coverage, geosynchronous orbit
(GEO) satellites could also be used. However, LEO satellite
links have a lower overall propagation delay and a lower
propagation loss relative to GEO links [6]. Moreover, GEO
satellites require a higher cost for deployment, and a typi-
cal GEO link requires large transmit/receive antennas and
stronger transmit power, which makes them inadequate for
networks constrained with low cost and low energy such as
IoT networks.
However, LEO satellite networks suffer from high
Doppler due to the satellite motion relative to the ground
user as well as a high level of cochannel interference.
The high level of interference is caused by other devices
operating in the same time–frequency resources due to
the wide coverage area of the satellite. High interference
in multiple access LEO networks is caused by a large
number of cochannel transmissions, particularly in shared
bands such as industrial, scientific, and medical band [7],
which is open to the general public for transmission and
does not require paid licensing. The devices in these bands
lack standardization and coordination, which exacerbates
interference [2]. In these interference-limited scenarios,
typical detection (demodulation) methods result in a high
symbol error rate (SER). To mitigate cochannel interfer-
ence, methods such as frequency reuse, dynamic spectrum
allocation, and beam multiplexing techniques are typically
used. However, the levels of interference remain significant.
To tackle the elevated SER due to cochannel interference,
deep learning (DL) signal detection methods have been
suggested in [8], [9], and [10] for effective signal detection
in scenarios dominated by interference.
Generic DL methods such as the simple artificial neu-
ral network (ANN) and the convolutional neural network
(CNN) have shown strong performance in various appli-
cations ranging from natural language processing [11] to
image classification [12]. However, they consume a sub-
stantial amount of power depending on the size of the
architecture [13]. Due to the large power consumption,
generic DL networks are typically designed to perform
localized computing tasks, i.e., edge computing, and thus
do not suit resource-limited devices. Conversely, the human
brain can process complicated dynamic events at only an
average of 20-W power [14]. Traditional processing such
as the largest neural network (NN) model, dubbed the
Megatron–Turing natural language generation model with
530 billion parameters [15] uses multiple high-end GPUs

the spiking behavior in human brains. Accordingly, the
spiking neural network (SNN) is set to dramatically reduce
power consumption [17] by encoding data with intermittent
spiking that only produces an output when the input exceeds
a certain threshold. This means that SNNs only consume
powerwhenthereisanevent(i.e.,aspike)attheneuron,thus
raising the level of efficiency closer to that of the biological
brain. Due to the exceptional energy efficiency of the SNN
relative to conventional DL networks, an SNN-based IoT
signal detector would be ideal for a satellite receiver due
to its limited power resources. In addition, SNNs can offer
lower latency due to their event-driven nature, as well as
higher robustness to noise compared to conventional DL
networks [18].
In this article, we demonstrate the performance of
spiking models and their applicability to modern commu-
nications systems, specifically for chirp signal detection,
which we believe is a foundational aspect of communication
that inherently contributes to reliability. While various NN
architectures have been explored for signal detection in
terrestrial networks, to the best of our knowledge, this is
the first study that delves into the application of SNNs
for satellite IoT (S-IoT) signal detection. Given the unique
challenges posed by S-IoT systems, such as high cochannel
interference and power constraints [19], SNNs, with their
ultralow power consumption and efficient signal processing
capabilities, present a promising solution. After highlight-
ing the challenges of high cochannel interference in S-IoT
systems, it is essential to underscore the significance of
synchronization in addressing these issues. The synchro-
nization scheme we developed for SNN detection is pivotal
in ensuring accurate signal detection in an S-IoT context.
The synchronization method ensures the alignment of the
received signal with the expected timing and frequency
characteristics, paving the way for more reliable and ef-
ficient detection of uplink IoT signals in a LEO satellite
scenario.
We present a comparison of traditional detection, con-
ventionalDLmodels,andspikingmodelsforsatelliteuplink
signal detection of devices that utilize the chirp-based mod-
ulation scheme. As a practical implementation, we consider
the LoRa modulation scheme, which is a popular chirp-
based modulation and a technology of interest in terrestrial
and S-IoT networks. Nevertheless, the proposed detector,
based on an NN architecture, can be adapted to various sig-
nal types as it can accept different sequences, such as other
time-sampled signals, as input. The comparison evaluates
the performance of how conventional and spiking models
can improve the SER in an environment with high levels
of cochannel interference. Our study significantly reduces
the power consumption of SNNs for signal detection in
S-IoT scenarios compared to conventional methods. This
reduction has multiple real-world impacts: it lowers op-
erating and hardware costs for satellite providers, extends
satellite lifespan due to more efficient power use, decreases
environmental impact through reduced space debris, and
fosters innovation in energy-efficient computing. Overall,
implementing SNNs could revolutionize the satellite com-
munication industry and stimulate broader technological
advancements. In addition, the performance assessment
relies on the emulation of a LEO satellite constellation
with ground users that employ a chirp signal modulation
scheme, where emulation of the chirp signal is performed
with a MATLAB-scripted emulator [20], [21] developed
earlier by our research team. The results show a consid-
erable improvement in the SER of conventional DL and
spiking-based signal detectors compared to traditional non-
coherent detection in the uplink of a LEO constellation. The
contribution of this work is summarized as follows.
1) It highlights the performance benefit of using
spiking-based/DL networks for accurate signal de-
tectioninanenvironmentwithahighlevelofcochan-
nel interference.
2) It demonstrates the applicability of power-efficient
spiking-based networks in detecting uplink IoT sig-
nals in a LEO satellite scenario with high cochannel
interference levels.
3) It assesses the power efficiency performance of spik-
ing networks based on neuromorphic hardware rela-
tive to conventional DL models running on standard
hardware.
4) It develops a synchronization scheme that is novel in
the S-IoT context.
5) It tests the idea from [8], which shows a hybrid
network that switches between the conventional
matched filter and DL signal detection based on
the inferred interference level. The aim is to further
increase the performance of the spiking-based re-
ceiversbyhybridizingthenetworkwithconventional
detection techniques.

II. RELATED WORKS
In the realm of S-IoT, understanding and enhancing
LEOIoTnetworkuplinkperformanceisparamount.Central
to this challenge is the issue of cochannel interference. This
section reviews pivotal research in this domain, paving the
way for our exploration into SNNs.
The uplink performance of LEO IoT networks has been
analyzed using stochastic geometry in [22] and simulation
in [23]. In these research works, cochannel interference
is concluded to heavily limit the performance. To combat
the performance degradation arising from cochannel in-
terference, many interference mitigation techniques have
been studied in past research works. One method is to
use coordinated multiple-access techniques, i.e., scheduling
similar to cellular networks [24]. However, using coordi-
nated multiple-access techniques is difficult to implement
in LEO satellite constellations given the high Doppler shift
as well as the higher propagation delay compared to con-
ventional terrestrial networks. Other interference mitigation
approaches such as cognitive radio (CR) [25] have been uti-
lized; however, CR is not ideal for low-power user devices
due to the extra power needed for spectrum sensing.
One prominent technology that is positioned for the
S-IoT is chirp-base modulation. The performance of LoRa
(as the implementation of chirp-based modulation) under
interference conditions has been investigated in multiple
studies, such as in [22] and [26]. While the traditional
detection of LoRa shows some robustness to interference,
the network cannot be directly scaled to practical satellite
scenarios given typical traditional noncoherent detector re-
ceivers. An uplink LEO satellite communication scenario
is an example of a high interference environment, where
the satellite receiver has to deal with a high number of
radio frames, due to its inherent large swath of cover-
age. An IoT-over-satellite scenario concentrating on LoRa
modulation is explored in [27], where it is concluded that
high volumes of LoRa traffic significantly deteriorate the
system’s performance. Yang et al. [28] propose a folded
chirp shift keying (FCSK) system that, like LoRa, employs
chirp-based modulation for transmitting low-bit-rate data.
Although their approach demonstrates enhanced resilience
to Doppler effects, FCSK’s detection error rate is inferior
to that of LoRa. This is primarily attributed to FCSK’s
variable chirp rate, which renders it more vulnerable to
interference from other chirp rates. Nonetheless, receivers
need to be significantly redesigned such that they can deal
with non-Gaussian impairments.
DL holds significant potential as a tool for signal de-
tection in wireless communication, as underscored by a
seminalpaperin[9]. The capabilityofsignaldetectioninthe
presence of cochannel interference is further demonstrated
in [8] and [10]. In the domain of wireless communication,
DL has been adeptly employed to fortify interference mit-
igation algorithms and amplify overall performance [29].
Another application of DL tools is shown in [30], which
leverages DL networks to identify wireless devices using
chirp signal processing. However, DL as a tool for signal
detection has not been extensively showcased in an S-IoT
scenario. Onboard a satellite, a DL receiver has been shown
in [31]; however, the work focuses on high-throughput
transmissions and the nonlinear impairments produced by
the hardware. While conventional DL works well at de-
tecting signals with non-Gaussian noise, the high power
requirements of complex DL models are not ideal for use
in systems with limited power availability such as onboard
processors in LEO satellites. A spiking-based system would
reduce the energy requirements while still providing high-
performance characteristics for signal detection [17].
III. SPIKING NEURAL NETWORKS
An advantage that SNNs have over generic NNs is their
addition of a spatiotemporal dimension in the spike trains
instead of single-element vectors used in NNs that carry
the weights and biases. The spike trains allow the SNN to
be effective at carrying a lot of information while using
fewer neurons relative to generic NNs [32]. As a result,
an SNN would perform particularly strongly at processing
temporally rich data streams, such as wireless signals [33].
Also, the SNN network is only energized when the spikes
are generated/received, unlike typical NNs where the entire
network needs to be continuously run for every new input.
These advantages have been used for several previous
research works in communications such as in automatic
modulation classification (AMC) [34], where the authors
efficiently perform AMC on RF domain data using an SNN
and improve memory utilization. Heavily related research
works in [35] and [36] showcase neuromorphic sensing and
communications, where they demonstrate an SNN transmit-
ter and receiver for energy-efficient communications. How-
ever, the papers do not look into satellite applications and
also do not compare the energy efficiency of spiking-based
networks with the conventional NN. Research work on us-
ing SNNs for signal detection is proposed in [37] to improve
human body communication by harnessing the aforemen-
tionedlowpowerconsumptionandsuperiorperformanceon
spatiotemporal data of the SNN. Another research work for
detecting signals with SNNs is demonstrated in [38], where
anSNNisusedfordetectingradarsignals.Notwithstanding,
to the best of our knowledge, there has not been any research
work showing the prospect of SNNs for the signal detection
of uplink for IoT-over-satellite communications. The prime
benefit of an SNN-based receiver would be its ability to
deal with non-Gaussian interference while simultaneously
exhibiting low power consumption.
The spiking neuron is heavily inspired by biologi-
cally plausible neuron models such as the Hodgkin–Huxley
model [39]. However, while the Hodgkin–Huxley model is
accurate, it is difficult to compute due to their nonlinear be-
havior, fine temporal resolution, and the complex differen-
tial equations needed to describe them [40]. Consequently,
leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron models can be used
instead. The LIF neuron model takes the sum of weighted
input pulses, which, in turn, are integrated over time with
a certain exponential decay, i.e., leakage. An illustration

randomly distributed ground IoT devices that operate using
the chirp modulation scheme. Each ground IoT device is
configured to connect to the nearest satellite if the device
lies within the satellite’s footprint, where each satellite is
part of a large constellation. The satellites are simulated
to orbit a perfectly spherical earth as an approximation.
The model assumes that all IoT devices use chirp-based
modulation without loss of generality, which is performed
using a MATLAB scripted emulator [21] to generate the

B. IoT Access System
For this research work, we are interested in the per-
formance of signal detection in a satellite scenario where
received signals are prone to cochannel interference from
uncoordinated signal transmissions. One popular access
system, dubbed pure-ALOHA, does not take into consid-
eration any scheduling or whether another device is trans-
mitting. As such, pure-ALOHA tends to allow for signal
collisions that compromise the signal receiver to accurately
detect the signal. In addition, pure-ALOHA does not take
into account the availability of a satellite; thus, packets can
be lost. However, we only care about signal detection in
a satellite scenario, so we put more emphasis on detection
rather than satellite availability. Nevertheless, the advan-
tage of pure-ALOHA is its simplicity; it requires a low
overhead, which allows for less power consumption and,
therefore, a longer battery life for IoT devices. Accordingly,
we assume a pure-ALOHA access model for this research
work, where each device uses the same channel resources
and transmits them as soon as the data are available. As an
alternative, scheduled ALOHA could be used, where each
user device would require a synchronized clock to regulate
the transmission times in a periodical or event-triggered

V. SPIKING-BASED DETECTION NETWORKS
The main contribution of this article is to present novel
spiking-based receivers in a practical S-IoT scenario. In
the following section, we discuss the spiking-based NN
receivers used in this article: 1) SNN; 2) convolutional
SNN (CSNN); and 3) HybNet that was adopted from [8]
to use spiking-based networks instead of conventional DL
networks.
A. SNN Detector
To implement the SNN, we utilize an open-source
Python library (SnnTorch [40]), which encodes the input
data into a spike train to accommodate spiking-based net-
works. The encoding is done in our work via constant
current injection, where each data sample is treated as a
constant current input over each time step, where 50 time
steps are used. The encoding of the input can treat static data
as a direct current (dc) input and consistently feed the same
features to the input layer of the SNN at each time interval.
However, other popular examples of encoding the input data
include rate encoding, latency encoding, and delta modu-
lation. These methods could be utilized to further extort
temporal information for SNNs [49]. Decoding the output
from spikes to real numbers is done by a process called
spike decoding. Examples of decoding methods include rate
decoding and latency decoding. A more expansive analysis
of different encoding and decoding strategies can be found
in [40]. For this work, mean squared error (MSE) count loss
is employed, which, in effect, is rate decoding. MSE count
loss was used as it demonstrated desirable performance
when training the network. The membrane potential of the
correct class is encouraged to increase the number of spikes,
while the incorrect classes are encouraged to reduce the total
spike count over time for each neuron to achieve high-level
performance [50]. In addition to the SNN, a second network
based on the CNN called the CSNN is also investigated
to combine the superior feature extraction and the power
efficiency of the CNN relative to other DL networks, such as
the ANN [51], with the ultralow power consumption of the
SNN. A CSNN performs convolution operations on spikes
2Note that this model does not take into account the earth’s movement and
assumes that the earth is a perfect sphere.
(or events) generated by individual neurons instead of tra-
ditional continuous-valued activations. The convolutional
filters in a CSNN are designed to detect specific temporal
patterns in the spike sequences. This allows CSNNs to
process spatiotemporal data effectively and learn to extract
relevant features from input stimuli.
A challenge in SNNs is the “dead neuron problem” as
highlighted in [40], which markedly reduces performance
due to the nondifferentiability of spikes. The “dead neuron
problem” occurs when the membrane potential is 0; thus,
the neuron does not fire and, therefore, does not contribute
to the loss function in the training stage. The issue of
the nondifferentiability of the neuron can be addressed by
smoothing out the Heaviside step function in (1) with the
sigmoid function σ (x), which is known as the surrogate gra-
dient approach [52]. Smoothing out the Heaviside function
with the sigmoid function can override the derivative of the
Heaviside function, which is the Dirac delta function. The
smoothed-out Dirac delta function would then take time to
fall to 0, therefore contributing to the training loss function.
The use of surrogate gradient descent algorithms in SNNs
caneffectivelyaddressthedeadneuronproblembyallowing
for gradient-based optimization and weight updates, even if
some neurons are not generating spikes.
The two spiking-based networks mentioned above
(SNN and CSNN) are also compared to the conventional
ANN and CNN with the same dimensions as the spiking-
based network counterpart. The same dimensions are used
for a fair comparison between the spiking-based networks
and conventional DL networks. A summary of the networks
used in this work is summarized in Table I. Note that some
changes could be made to the network parameters to further
increase the performance. Nevertheless, the dimensions
chosen for this work showed solid performance, and the
purpose of this work is not to showcase ideal network
dimensions.
B. HybNet
To further improve the error rate detection performance

the spiking-based networks so that it switches between two
different detection branches: 1) a spiking-based network (in
our case, we select the CSNN due to its strong performance
and lower power consumption compared to the ANN, CNN,

detection). The advantage of utilizing the HybNet architec-
ture is that in noise-limited scenarios, the matched filter
path is chosen by the supervisor switching network to
detect the signal. Conversely, when the receiving signals
are interference limited, detection using the spiking-based
network is chosen by the supervisor switching network.
Note that this supervisor switching network is trained with
a CSNN network, which we dub as the selector CSNN, and

For this work, we incorporate the LoRa modulation

We utilize the LoRa MATLAB emulator [21] developed

1) signal generator and 2) conventional detector based on
noncoherent detection. Note that, for this work, there is no
additional error correction involved, just the generation of
a chirp-based packet with a random symbol payload and a
receiver that detects the payload packet with a noncoherent

would decrease the error rate. As such, in this article, we
show signal detection performance without error correction
as the lower bound.
A. LoRa Modulation
LoRa uses the chirp spread spectrum technique [20]
to modulate the transmitted symbols. This is achieved by
shifting the start of the chirp according to the transmitted
symbol value, and then, the frequency is cyclically swept
within a given bandwidth B. According to the LoRaWAN
specifications, only a few discrete values of the bandwidth
are permitted, where B ∈ {125, 250, 500} kHz. Another pa-
rameter that controls the chirp rate of each symbol is called
the spreading factor (SF), which is also restricted in the
LoRaWAN specifications to SF ∈ {7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12}. The
chirp slope, in the context of frequency modulation, is rep-
resented as the second derivative of the phase with respect
to time. For LoRa modulation schemes, this relationship
can be expressed as d2

B. LoRa Detection
The detection of a LoRa signal involves a two-step
process. In the first step, the signal is dechirped by mixing
the LoRa signal with an inverted chirp. The dechirping
process converts the LoRa signal into an MFSK signal,
which is a modulation type that encodes symbols with M
equally spaced frequency tones. The second step can then be
detected using either conventional coherent or noncoherent
detection methods. The coherent detection method based on
matched filtering is proven to be an optimal detector under
AWGNchannelconditions[55] andisalsoshowntoachieve
better performance compared to the noncoherent detector in
the presence of LoRa-on-LoRa interference [26]. However,
coherent detection requires perfect synchronization in both
time and frequency [55] for coherent detection to perform
well [56]. Therefore, coherent detection is not appropriate
for a LEO satellite communication scenario due to the
high Doppler frequency shift on the transmitted signal.
Consequently, we use noncoherent detection in this article.

VII. FRAME SYNCHRONIZATION
In the context of applying SNN and CSNN for S-IoT sig-
nal detection, frame synchronization is pivotal. The design
of our synchronization method is tailored to be compatible
with the characteristics of these NN detectors to enable them
to effectively process and detect signals in the presence of
interference and Doppler shifts.
The preamble in the IoT frame serves a dual purpose:
it allows the detector to find the start of the chirp but also
provides a reference point to compensate for the Doppler
offset. Given the pronounced Doppler shifts in LEO orbits,
our synchronization method, inspired by range Doppler
matched filtering in radar signal processing [57], is designed
to allow for accurate signal detection. By matching the
ideal templates of the known preamble, each with a slightly
different Doppler shift, we ensure that the SNN and CSNN
can adaptively respond to varying Doppler conditions. Note

only activating when inputs surpass a certain threshold.
This functionality results in efficient energy utilization,
a critical attribute in the energy-constrained environment
of LEO satellites. Moreover, the ability of spiking-based
networks to handle temporal dynamics facilitates the pro-
cessingofreal-timeorstreamingdata,typicallyexperienced
in satellite communication systems. Conventional DL net-
works generally require a complete data batch for process-
ing, potentially causing delays in real-time scenarios. In
essence, the selection of spiking-based networks over DL
networks for LEO scenarios goes beyond mere substitution.
It entails choosing an architecture better suited to meet
the unique challenges presented by such environments, de-
spite acknowledging the associated complexities of training
spiking-based networks and the lack of established training
methodologies.
Neuromorphic hardware, like Loihi [61], inherently
supports parallelism. Just as the human brain processes
information through billions of neurons working simultane-
ously, neuromorphic chips are designed to handle multiple
neural computations concurrently. This parallel processing
capability allows them to mimic biological neural systems
closely, leading to rapid data processing with minimal en-
ergy consumption. The architecture of neuromorphic chips,
with interconnected spiking neurons, facilitates this par-
allelism, making them particularly well suited for SNNs.
These results reinforce the idea that a spiking network
could be used for signal detection, particularly in resource-
constrained applications such as in a LEO satellite.
Note that we do not show the energy consumption of
spiking-based networks on traditional “nonspiking” hard-
wareplatformsinTableIVduetotheinherentinefficiencies.
The true potential of the energy efficiency of spiking-based
networks manifests predominantly on neuromorphic sys-
tems. These neuromorphic platforms are characterized by
a notably higher core count than their conventional coun-
terparts, a feature essential for capitalizing on the inherent
parallelism of spiking networks. As a result, they can oper-
ate at a markedly reduced energy expenditure per inference
relative to classical ANNs and CNNs [40]. When consider-
ing traditional hardware, the deployment of spiking-based
networks does not confer any energy benefits, leading to
their omission from energy consumption metrics on such
systems. On a related note, standard CNNs and ANNs lack
direct compatibility with specific neuromorphic processors,
rendering energy consumption comparisons nonviable.
While we evaluate the power consumption across GPU
and Loihi platforms, it is important to recognize that the en-
ergy efficiency of these computational units can be swayed
by factors such as manufacturing techniques. Drawing di-
rect comparisons between analog systems like SNNs and
digital ones like ANNs and CNNs presents inherent chal-
lenges due to their operational and design differences. An
analytical quantification of the computational complexity
between SNNs and traditional networks is intricate, given
their unique operational characteristics.

IX. CONCLUSION
In this article, we presented a spiking-network-based
receiver for detecting IoT signals in an S-IoT uplink sce-
nario. For the receiver, we investigated the SNN and the
CSNN and compared the error rate performance against
conventional ANN and CNN receivers, as well as con-
ventional noncoherent detection. The findings reveal that
both spiking-based and DL receivers exhibit resilience to
cochannel interference. Notably, the spiking-based net-
works display impressive detection performance in high
interference scenarios, while consuming several orders of
magnitude less power per detection than traditional ANN
and CNN receivers. Particularly, the CSNN stands out in
terms of energy efficiency, offering a compelling advantage
for applications where power conservation is paramount.
To further improve the detection performance of the spik-
ing networks, we adopt the HybNet framework from our
previous work to switch between the traditional detection
methods and the spiking-based receiver.
