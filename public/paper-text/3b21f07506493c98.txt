One Model to Fit Them All: Universal IMU-based Human Activity
Recognition with LLM-assisted Cross-dataset Representation
QINGXIN WEI, Zhejiang University, China
JIAMING HUANG, Zhejiang University, China
YI GAO, Zhejiang University, China
WEI DONG, Zhejiang University, China
Human Activity Recognition (HAR) is essential for pervasive computing and intelligent interaction, with broad applications across
various fields. However, there is still no one model capable of fitting various HAR datasets, severely limiting its applicability in
practical scenarios. To address this, we propose oneHAR, an LLM-assisted universal IMU-based HAR system designed to achieve
"one model to fit them all" — just one model that can adapt to diverse HAR datasets without any dataset-specific operation. In
particular, we propose Cross-Dataset neural network (CDNet) for the "one model," which models both the temporal context
and spatial relationships of IMU data to capture cross-dataset representations, encompassing differences in device, participant,
data collection position, and environment, etc. Additionally, we introduce LLM-driven data synthesis, which enhances the
training process by generating virtual IMU data through three carefully designed strategies. Furthermore, LLM-assisted adaptive
position processing optimizes the inference process by flexibly handling a variable combination of positional inputs. Our model
demonstrates strong generalization across five public IMU-based HAR datasets, outperforming the best baselines by up to 46.9% in
the unseen-dataset scenario, and 6.5% in the cross-dataset scenario.

1 INTRODUCTION
Human Activity Recognition (HAR) is a pivotal foundation of pervasive computing and intelligent interaction, acting
as a bridge that connects humans with the digital ecosystem. It has found widespread application across various fields,
including health monitoring systems that track vital signs [25, 52, 58], smart homes that adapt to our behaviors to
enhance comfort [22, 26, 47], and even metaverse platforms that provide immersive experiences [56]. HAR involves
various data types like Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) signals, vision signals [3, 43], radio signals [4, 76], and
physiological signals [21, 50], etc. Unlike other data types, IMUs provide broader coverage due to their wearability
while maintaining high precision across this wide area, remaining unaffected by environmental factors such as
indoor/outdoor locations or obstructions. As a result, IMU-based HAR has become a highly focused and critically
important research direction [7, 49].
IMU-based HAR research has explored many methodologies. Initially, traditional machine learning approaches
such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forests (RF), and Logistic Regression [6, 10, 16, 20, 78] were
predominantly employed. As data volume and label diversity increased, deep learning methods demonstrated

Unlike the above methods, our goal is to achieve "one model to fit them all" — a single model that can adapt
to various HAR datasets without requiring any dataset-specific operation. The disparities among these datasets
encompass various factors, such as differences in device (e.g., type, sampling rate, noise), participant (e.g., body shape,
age), and data collection position (e.g., waist, ankle), etc. To this end, we propose Cross-Dataset neural Network (CDNet)
to capture cross-dataset representations. Building upon CDNet, we present oneHAR, an LLM-assisted system that
enables accurate and universal IMU-based HAR using only one model.
To achieve this, we need to address two key challenges:
Challenge I. Learning Generalizable Representations Across Diverse Data Distributions. As discussed
earlier, each dataset has its own set of domains (i.e., factors), and each domain can take various forms. For example, the
device-type domain may include smartphones, smartwatches, and smart glasses. When datasets are combined, they
introduce a wide variety of domains, leading to significant distribution shifts in IMU data, even for the same activity.
Therefore, learning a generalizable representation that remains unaffected by the effects of these distribution shifts is a
critical challenge in achieving the universal HAR. To address this, we propose CDNet (Section 4), a novel neural network
for learning generalizable representation. We divide these domains into intra-dataset and inter-dataset domains based
on the distribution shifts. For intra-dataset domains, position is one of the most significant factors. Even for the same
activity, the data recorded by sensors at different body positions can vary significantly due to differences in sensor
axis orientation, motion amplitude, and movement patterns. For example, during walking, a sensor placed at the waist
primarily captures the overall body motion with smaller amplitude changes, mainly in the vertical direction, reflecting
the body’s up-and-down movement. In contrast, a sensor placed at the ankle detects larger amplitude movements in

Challenge II. Handling Severe Data Imbalance Across HAR Datasets. Merging multiple datasets often leads
to domain-level imbalance, where certain domain categories are overrepresented while others are scarce. For instance,
data may be collected from diverse device types (e.g., smartphones, smartwatches) that exhibit varying levels of
accuracy and noise characteristics. Additionally, sampling rates can vary widely, from 20Hz to 200Hz, affecting data
consistency. Furthermore, certain activities are overrepresented (e.g., walking) while others are underrepresented (e.g.,
cycling), further skewing the data. When datasets with such severe imbalances are directly applied to a unified model,
the resulting noise can significantly degrade model accuracy. Therefore, addressing these data imbalances is crucial.
We designed a set of LLM-assisted mechanisms in both the training and inference phases of oneHAR to address this
critical challenge. Specifically, for the training process, we propose the LLM-Driven Data Synthesis (Section 5.2). The
core idea is to generate diverse textual descriptions of activities using Large Language Models (LLMs), which are
then converted into virtual IMU data streams. Most importantly, we have designed three strategies—Data Quality
Assurance, Data Position Simulation, and Proportional Data Synthesis—to ensure the reliability and usability of the
model for downstream tasks, addressing aspects of quantity, position, and quality. During inference, the architecture
of CDNet allows for the input of a variable amount and combination of positional data. To effectively handle this
variability, we introduce the LLM-assisted Adaptive Position Processing (Section 5.3), which can adaptively integrate
different positional configurations by evaluating each position’s contribution and inter-positional correlations.

RealworldHAR [54], UCI-HAR [45], and USC-HAD [70], and conducted two comprehensive sets of experiments, as
shown in Figure 2. Our model outperforms the best baselines by up to 46.9% in the unseen-dataset scenario and 6.5%

The major contributions of this work can be summarized as follows.
• We propose oneHAR, an LLM-assisted system that enables accurate and universal IMU-based HAR using just
one model. It employs a meticulously designed architecture called CDNet learning generalizable representations
to addresses distribution discrepancies across diverse HAR datasets.
• We introduce LLM-driven data synthesis, which generates virtual IMU data to tackle the challenge of data
imbalance across HAR datasets. It incorporates three carefully designed strategies to control the quality,
positions, and quantity of the synthesized data, thereby enhancing the dataset’s diversity and utility.
• We extensively evaluate oneHAR using five widely adopted HAR datasets. The results demonstrate that our
method not only maintains SOTA average performance across all datasets, but also exhibits the strongest
generalization ability in unseen environments, compared with baselines.

2 RELATED WORK
2.1 Single-dataset Human Activity Recognition
Human Activity Recognition (HAR) has been extensively studied, with early research predominantly focusing on
models trained using single-sensor data collected from a single dataset [6, 10, 16, 20, 78]. To enhance accuracy,
subsequent efforts explored multi-sensor fusion within a single dataset, aiming to exploit complementary information
across different sensor modalities. DeepSense [67] proposed a hierarchical deep learning framework that jointly
models spatial and temporal dependencies across different sensor modalities through the integration of convolutional
and recurrent neural networks. DeepFusion [65] further refined this approach with a quality-aware fusion mechanism,
dynamically adjusting sensor contributions based on reliability to improve performance in noisy environments. Further
developments expanded fusion strategies to multi-sensor configurations within a single dataset. SenseHAR [27]
proposed a virtual activity sensor framework that learns shared latent representations from multiple physical sensors,
mitigating reliance on specific sensor setups. GlobalFusion [37] incorporated a dual-attention mechanism to selectively
emphasize informative features while suppressing noise, achieving better feature alignment across modalities.
Despite these advancements, all aforementioned methods are trained and evaluated on a single dataset. When
applied to data from other different datasets, their performance typically degrades significantly, motivating the need
for research into cross-dataset human activity recognition.
2.2 Cross-datasets Human Activity Recognition
Regarding the cross-domain challenges in human activity recognition (HAR), existing methods primarily focus on
cross-dataset research to enable models to generalize effectively to unseen domains. Two prominent approaches in
this area are domain adaptation and domain generalization.
Domain adaptation [5, 17, 73] aims to bridge the distribution gap between source and target domain datasets by
aligning their representation spaces. Domain-Adversarial Neural Network (DANN) [18] introduced an adversarial
training framework to minimize the domain discrepancy by aligning features through a domain discriminator [8],
which has become a foundational method in domain adaptation. Other techniques, like subdomain adaptation [38, 77],
refine this alignment by addressing fine-grained differences within subdomains, further enhancing model robustness.
Despite these advancements, this method often assumes the availability of target domain data during training, which
may not be practical in many real-world scenarios. And the effectiveness of domain adaptation tends to degrade
when applied to unseen target domains that are significantly different from the source domains.

Given these practical constraints, domain generalization [34, 59, 74] becomes a more promising approach. It
overcomes this limitation by learning domain-invariant representations across multiple source domains to generalize
effectively to unseen target domains. Among these, feature alignment-based methods aim to minimize domain-specific
variations by aligning features across multiple source domains. An example is Deep Domain Confusion (DDC) [29, 55],
which introduces a maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) [14, 35] loss to align source domain features, effectively
reducing domain shifts and improving generalization capabilities. Complementing it, data augmentation-based
methods enhance robustness by generating domain-diverse samples during training. For instance, MixStyle [75]
synthesizes new domain variations by mixing feature statistics, such as mean and variance, across different source
domains, creating a richer training set and improving model adaptability to novel domains. Furthermore, adversarial
learning-based methods extend adversarial learning techniques to domain generalization by training models to confuse
domain discriminators while preserving task-relevant features. CrossGrad [48], for example, applies adversarial
perturbations in both input and feature spaces, enabling the model to generalize effectively across diverse environments.
Nevertheless, they simplify the original datasets for convenient pre-training. This comes at the cost of removing a
significant amount of heterogeneity from the datasets, thereby reducing the generalization ability of the method.
Therefore, we aim to design a novel neural network architecture that maximizes the retention of the original dataset’s
full heterogeneity, including user characteristics, sensor positions, activities, and devices, while learning robust and
generalizable representations across datasets from this vast and complex data heterogeneity.
2.3 LLM-based Human Activity Recognition
Recent advancements in LLMs have ignited significant interest in their application to HAR, offering new possibilities
for interpreting sensor data [32, 61] and performing activity recognition tasks. HARGPT [28] demonstrates the
feasibility of using LLMs for zero-shot HAR by directly inputting raw IMU sensor data into LLMs with carefully
designed prompts. The study highlights the effectiveness of chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, enabling LLMs to
analyze sensor data and achieve robust activity recognition without any fine-tuning or domain-specific preprocessing.
By My Eyes [68] introduces a visual prompting approach that transforms IMU sensor data into visual representations,
such as waveforms, which are then used as prompts for multimodal LLMs. This method enhances HAR performance
by overcoming the limitations of textual prompts for long-sequence sensor data, improving efficiency and adaptability
in diverse scenarios. IMUGPT [33] leverages LLMs like ChatGPT to generate diverse activity descriptions, which
are converted into 3D motion sequences via T2M-GPT [69] and further synthesized into realistic IMU data through
physics-based simulation. LanHAR [66] leverages LLMs to generate semantic interpretations of sensor readings and
activity labels, mapping them into a shared language space to mitigate cross-dataset heterogeneity and recognize
unseen activities.
However, these studies are primarily methodological explorations of how LLMs can be adapted to IMU-based
HAR tasks. They emphasize feasibility and the potential of LLMs to interpret sensor data, rather than focusing on
building high-performance or generalizable HAR systems. These approaches often rely on LLMs as the main reasoning
or prediction engine, which experimental evidence suggests yields sub-optimal performance when compared to
specialized deep learning models. In contrast, our work takes a different perspective: rather than treating LLMs
as primary classifiers, we leverage them as auxiliary components to systematically enhance task performance and
cross-dataset generalization of oneHAR. Specifically, LLMs are employed for virtual data synthesis and adaptive
position processing to assist both training and inference phase.

4 CDNet ARCHITECTURE FOR LEARNING GENERALIZABLE REPRESENTATION
4.1 Problem Statement
We construct a universal scenario for IMU-based HAR. Consider an IMUs data collection X ∈ R𝐵×𝑁 ×𝑇 ×C , where X
represents IMUs signals collected from 𝑁 sensors (located at different body positions), each recording C measurement
channels (e.g., accelerometer x, y, z axes) over 𝑇 time steps for a batch of size 𝐵. The source dataset (X𝑠, Y𝑠 ) is
formed by randomly sampling data from arbitrary datasets and sensors, while the remaining data forms the target
dataset (X𝑡 , Y𝑡 ). Our goal is to achieve high activity recognition accuracy on the target dataset X𝑡 , despite substantial
distribution discrepancies not only between X𝑠 and X𝑡 but also among the samples within X𝑡 itself.

4.5 IMU-Text Alignment Block for Activity Recognition
After obtaining the IMU cross-dataset representations, to perform downstream tasks like action recognition, it is
necessary to align them with the corresponding label embeddings in a shared latent space. To achieve this, we utilize a
Text Encoder to process activity labels and an IMU Projector to project the IMU embeddings into the label embedding
space.

aligned embeddings for IMU data and activity labels, forming the foundation for subsequent training objectives.

strategy ensures that the synthetic data complements, rather than overshadows, the real dataset by generating
data in proportion to the under-representation of each activity in the real dataset.

dataset. For example, if jumping samples are 10 times less frequent than walking, the generation process produces
sufficient synthetic jumping samples to achieve a 1:1 ratio between the two activities.

re-implemented and evaluated under exactly the same experimental settings, to ensure a fair and direct comparison.

rate followed a two-phase schedule with 1e-3 for the first phase and 1e-4 for the second phase, using Adam as the
optimizer. For the text encoder, we use the ’bert-base-uncased’ model from Huggingface Transformers, which contains
110M parameters. All parameters of BERT are frozen during training. For LLMs, we employ GPT-4o. For the projector
layer, we use two stacked bidirectional GRU layers with hidden sizes of 18 and 72, respectively. For the IMU projector,
we use a three-layer, 3-kernel 2D convolutional layer, followed by a fully connected layer that projects the output
into a 768-dimensional space. Finally, we report both accuracy and macro F1-score as the primary evaluation metrics.
6.1.4 Data Pre-processing. We perform a series of conventional data pre-processing steps, as described below. First,
all IMU signals are uniformly downsampled to 50 Hz. Second, signals are segmented into fixed-length windows of
120 time steps using a sliding window with 50% overlap. Then the noise in the data is handled using a combination of
smoothing techniques. A moving average filter is used to smooth short-term fluctuations, and a low-pass Butterworth
filter is applied to suppress high-frequency noise. We also ensure that signals from all sensor positions are temporally
aligned using their timestamps, so that data from different body locations capture the same movement phase during
an activity. Finally, we apply layer normalization and position encoding on each sample.
Each dataset was divided into training and test sets using a class-wise stratified random split with a ratio of 70%
for training and 30% for testing. Specifically, the resulting test sets contain 5773 samples for HHAR [51], 1542 for
Pamap2 [44], 7098 for Realworld [54], 2443 for UCI-HAR [45], and 2178 for USC-HAD [70]. Each sample corresponds
to a fixed-length activity segment that integrates IMU data from multiple sensor positions. To further enrich the
diversity and generalizability of the training data, we apply our proposed LLM-driven data synthesis approach to
generate virtual activity samples. These synthetic samples are added exclusively to the training sets. As a result, the
final training set sizes after augmentation are increased to 20860 samples for HHAR [51], 7710 for Pamap2 [44], 24565
for Realworld [54], 10215 for UCI-HAR [45], and 9890 for USC-HAD [70].

6.3 LODO Performance
To evaluate the true generalization capability of each method in a realistic deployment setting, we adopt a Leave-One-
Dataset-Out (LODO) protocol. In each round, one dataset is held out entirely for testing, while the model is trained
on the remaining four datasets. This setting simulates a zero-shot transfer scenario, where the model must perform
activity recognition on a previously unseen domain without any access to its training data.
As shown in Table 3, oneHAR consistently achieves the highest accuracy and macro F1-score across all datasets,
outperforming the previous SOTA (UniMTS) by up to 46.9% in accuracy and 93.0% in F1. Specifically, in terms of
accuracy, oneHAR achieves improvements of 4.1%, 12.1%, 12.4%, 24.3%, and 11.3% on HHAR, PAMAP2, RealWorld,
UCI-HAR, and USC-HAD respectively, compared to the best-performing baseline on each dataset.
These results confirm that oneHAR is not only effective when trained across diverse datasets, but also excels in
zero-shot deployment scenarios. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that despite the significant relative improvements
achieved by our model, the performance of all existing methods—including ours—remains insufficient for reliable
application in real-world zero-shot scenarios.

7 DISCUSSION
We acknowledge several limitations which we leave as future work. First, the activity types covered in our current
evaluation are limited to basic modes of locomotion. This choice restricts the model’s ability to generalize to more
complex or fine-grained human activities, such as object manipulation, social gestures, or multi-tasking behaviors. We
plan to extend the label space and incorporate more diverse activity datasets that go beyond only modes of locomotion
to improve the generalization capacity of oneHAR.

Second, its ability to generalize to entirely unseen scenarios still has room for improvement. In particular, our
performance on zero-shot recognition tasks remains limited, revealing persistent challenges related to domain shifts.
We plan to address this by explicitly modeling domain-specific characteristics - for instance, designing dedicated
generalization modules for user domains (e.g., physical attributes or movement styles). Moreover, we aim to explore
more domain adaptation strategies to enhance the model’s robustness under real-world distribution shifts.
Third, our current use of Large Language Models (LLMs) remains relatively shallow. LLMs encode extensive
prior knowledge about human behavior, contextual interactions, and even digital sensor signals, accumulated from
large-scale textual corpora. However, our model does not incorporate a dedicated mechanism to fully exploit this
potential. Moreover, the reasoning process of LLMs is inherently opaque, making it difficult to interpret why certain
outputs are generated. This lack of transparency raises concerns about potential hallucinations, particularly when
applied to unfamiliar or highly complex activity patterns. So we envision integrating oneHAR into an LLM-powered
intelligent assistant capable of real-time activity analysis, personalized feedback, and interactive guidance. Such
integration would bridge sensing and reasoning, allowing for more adaptive, context-aware, and user-centric HAR
systems. These directions can enable intelligent behavior understanding in healthcare, smart home, and human–AI
interaction scenarios.
8 CONCLUSION
In this paper, we introduced oneHAR, a universal HAR system designed to achieve "one model to fit them all". We
introduce CDNet, an LLM-assisted architecture that captures cross-dataset representations by modeling both temporal
and spatial dependencies of IMU data. Furthermore, we enhance the training process through LLM-driven Data
Synthesis, which generates virtual IMU data using three carefully designed strategies, and optimize inference with
LLM-Assisted Adaptive Position Processing, enabling flexible handling of variable positional c onfigurations. Extensive
experiments on five public datasets demonstrate that oneHAR not only maintains SOTA average performance across
all datasets, but also exhibits the strongest generalization ability in unseen environments, compared with baselines.
