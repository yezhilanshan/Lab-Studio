Human activity is a fundamental component of intelligent interactive systems. Existing human activity recognition (HAR) follows
a closed-ended assumption—defining a fixed set of activity classes and classifying each sensor signal into one of them—yet
real-world human behavior is complex and ever-changing and thus cannot be fully represented by any fixed set. We present a new
paradigm for open-ended human activity understanding (HAU), shifting the goal from closed-set classification to understanding
activities via their meta-motions. To achieve this, we design MOSAIC, a motion decomposition and semantic fusion framework
that transforms raw sensor streams into structured meta-motion representations and subsequently generates fine-grained natural-
language descriptions. Additionally, we propose an LLM-driven training strategy for sensor–language alignment, enabling effective
cross-modal supervision in the absence of real-world training data. Finally, we leverage LLM-assisted reasoning to recognize
complex high-level activities based on the understanding of underlying motions and their composition. Our model demonstrates
strong generalization and performance across 18 public HAR datasets, outperforming the best baseline by up to 19.6% in unseen
scenarios.

Traditionally, HAR follows the closed-ended assumption (CWA) [60]. This assumption defines a finite set of activity
classes in advance, within which the model performs classification by mapping each sensor signal to one of them.
Under this setting, various approaches have been developed. Initially, traditional machine learning approaches such as
Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forests (RF), and Logistic Regression [8, 11, 13, 20, 75] were predominantly
employed. In parallel, early sequence-to-sequence HAR studies further formulated activity recognition as a temporal
sequence modeling problem, learning structured representations from sensor event streams for activities [15, 74]. As
data volume and label diversity increased, deep learning methods demonstrated advantages. These include models
based on convolutional neural networks combined with recurrent neural networks (CNN-RNN) [1, 68? ], Transformer
architectures [12, 49, 72], and graph convolutional networks (GCN) [33, 36, 57]. In practice, real-world human activity
is inherently open-ended [62]. Human activities are complex, continuous, and ever-changing, and thus cannot be fully
represented by any fixed set of pre-defined categories. As shown in Fig. 1, when a person is performing a stretching
exercise, the closed-ended HAR system often classifies it into a coarse and general category such as "standing", simply
because only such a similar label exists in the predefined set. However, such a label provides limited support for
AI applications. For example, in health monitoring or elderly care, knowing that a user is "standing" is less useful
than knowing that "the user raises both arms above the head and bends the upper body backward", which allows the
system to infer whether the user is stretching, losing balance, or preparing to sit down and then give feedback. Even if
"stretching exercise" were explicitly included in the predefined label set, similar cases would still occur, as real-world
activities cannot be exhaustively enumerated. Therefore, we argue that open-ended HAR should shift its perspective
from activity classification to activity understanding— that is, to represent activities through fine-grained motion
descriptions, and recognize activities based on the understanding of themselves. Recently, large language models
(LLMs) [19, 31] have garnered significant attention as a powerful class of language generation systems. Trained on
vast corpora of human language and world knowledge, LLMs possess strong capabilities in reasoning, abstraction,
and generalization. Some studies [26, 64] have further demonstrated that LLMs can directly interpret raw physical
signals. These make LLMs particularly promising for HAU. Existing approaches for LLMs to process sensor data can
be broadly categorized into two paradigms. The first treats sensor signal sequences as textual inputs [26, 64], directly
encoding them as prompts for LLMs. However, this often leads to poor results due to the fundamental differences

support open-ended HAU.
Therefore, we propose a new paradigm of HAR, termed open-ended human activity understanding (HAU). Specifi-
cally, it involves two essential aspects, as illustrated in Fig. 2. (1) Understanding an activity. An activity is essentially
a composition of multiple meta-motions performed by different body parts. Therefore, understanding an activity
means identifying which meta-motions are being executed and uncovering how these motions are organized and
coordinated to form a coherent behavior. (2) Recognizing an activity through such understanding. LLMs provide a
powerful pathway. When an LLM is presented with a comprehensive motion description, it probably can recognize
the most plausible activity being performed.
To this end, we introduce a unified system that transforms low-level sensor signals into high-level activity
understanding. Firstly, we design the Motion Decomposition and Semantic Fusion Framework (MOSAIC), which learns
to capture the structured compositional features of meta-motions from sensor data and translate them into fine-grained
textual descriptions. Built upon MOSAIC, we enable LLM-assisted activity reasoning, where a mobile application
generates motion descriptions with MOSAIC and recognizes activities through an LLM.
To achieve this, we need to address two key challenges:
Challenge I. Modeling the Organization and Coordination of Meta-Motions from Sensor Data. As discussed
earlier, understanding an activity essentially means recognizing how it organizes and coordinates a set of meta-
motions. However, motions occurring across different body parts are often entangled and temporally overlapping,
without clear temporal boundaries. Moreover, they also exhibit spatial correlations and functional dependencies
among limbs; for example, a forearm swing may drive an upper-arm rotation, and a knee lift may induce a foot
raise. Such complex relationships cannot be effectively captured through simple signal processing or handcrafted
segmentation of sensor data. To address this challenge, we introduce the Motion Decomposition and Semantic Fusion

Framework (MOSAIC), which models and describes coherent behaviors through a progressive two-level design: the
physical level and the semantic level. At the physical level, MOSAIC employs a Spatiotemporal Graph Convolutional
Network (ST-GCN) [66] to model the temporal dynamics and spatial dependencies of body motions. At the semantic
level, MOSAIC employs a Semantic Fusion and Decoding Network to bridge the gap between physical motion
representations and the semantic space of LLM. It first employs Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) [56] to encode
contextual information within motion representations. It then applies Multi-Head Cross-Attention (MHCA) [52, 56]
to align representations with a vocabulary space compressed via PCA [6, 22] and derived from a pre-trained LLM.
Finally, Transformer decoder blocks generate fine-grained textual descriptions of motions.
Challenge II. Aligning Sensor-Language modality under the Absence of Real Paired Data. Existing HAR
datasets are typically small-scale and limited to categorical annotations, providing pairs of IMU data and predefined
activity labels (e.g., walking, sitting, running). However, MOSAIC requires large-scale IMU–text pairs, where each
IMU sequence is aligned with a rich natural-language motion description rather than a discrete label. Such datasets
do not exist in current HAR research [27], posing a major challenge for training MOSAIC. To address this limitation,
we construct a large-scale synthetic corpus of IMU–text pairs. For locomotion-related activities, we leverage the
HumanML3D [17] dataset—a large-scale motion-language corpus containing 3D human skeleton sequences paired
with corresponding textual descriptions of actions and gestures. We synthesize IMU signals by deriving tri-axial
acceleration and angular velocity from 3D human skeleton. To further enhance data diversity and coverage, we
additionally generate a collection of static states. Specifically, we prompt an LLM to produce fine-grained textual
descriptions conditioned on activity labels, convert these textual descriptions into 3D skeletal sequences through a
motion generation model, and subsequently transform them into IMU signals. By constructing this large-scale dataset
purely from virtual data, we enable MOSAIC to learn sensor–language alignment at scale, thereby enhancing the
model’s generalization ability across diverse motion patterns.
The major contributions of this work can be summarized as follows.
• We are the first to redefine open-ended HAR as a task of activity understanding rather than activity classification.
We establish a complete pipeline from sensor perception to motion description and ultimately to activity
reasoning, offering a new perspective toward the real-world applicable and generalizable HAR.
• We introduce MOSAIC, a unified framework for open-ended human activity understanding. Unlike conventional
HAR models that rely on closed categorical labels, MOSAIC decomposes complex sensor signals into fine-grained
meta-motions and semantically fuses them into natural-language motion descriptions.
• We construct a large-scale synthetic sensor–language corpus to support cross-modal alignment. Trained on
this dataset, our model achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance, outperforming existing LLM-based HAR
methods in both recognition accuracy and generalization ability.

sensors; HARGPT [26] demonstrates the feasibility of using LLMs for zero-shot HAR; ByMyEyes [69] introduces
a visual prompting approach that transforms sensor data into waveforms, which are then used as prompts for
multimodal LLMs. However, the tokenizers used in LLMs—designed for natural language—are not well-suited for

2.2 LLM-based HAR by Aligning Sensor Signals with Text
Given that LLM tokenizers cannot directly interpret numerical sequences, a natural direction is to transform time-
series signals into representations that LLMs can understand. This has led to a body of work aiming to establish
effective alignment between sensor data and language representations. Some approaches integrate sensing modalities
into MLLMs. For example, OneLLM [18] and Anymal [38] both adopt tailored alignment strategies to train multimodal
encoders and projection layers that map diverse modalities—including sensor signals—into the semantic space of
LLMs. IMU2CLIP [37] aligns inertial measurement unit streams with text and video in a joint representation space.
Other approaches design mechanisms to directly align time series with textual representations. SensorLLM [30]
introduces an alignment module that converts sensor inputs into text about signal trend descriptions. TEST [50]
innovatively introduces a text-prototype-aligned contrast mechanism. Time-LLM [27] proposes a reprogramming
framework that reprograms time-series data using textual prototypes. LLM4TS [9] introduces a two-stage fine-tuning
framework that aligns LLMs with time-series data through multi-scale temporal aggregation. However, current
approaches for aligning sensor signals to language often suffer from coarse and rigid mappings. Such mappings
confine the learning process to shallow pattern matching. As a result, they cause substantial feature loss—particularly
in temporal dynamics, spatial dependencies, and latent semantics—which ultimately limits the model’s generalization
ability and robustness.

to implement it. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the proposed system consists of three key components:

5 LLM-DRIVEN TRAINING STRATEGY FOR SENSOR-LANGUAGE ALIGNMENT
Although we have already proposed a unified framework called MOSAIC for open-ended human activity under-
standing, training it requires large-scale paired data that describes activity behaviors in natural language. However,
existing HAR datasets only provide categorical activity labels rather than descriptive text. To overcome this limitation,
we firstly construct a large-scale synthetic IMU–text corpus (Section 5.1), enabling diverse and linguistically rich
supervision for cross-modal learning. Then, based on this corpus, we develop a two-stage alignment training process
(Section 5.2) that progressively aligns sensor representations with motion descriptions through contrastive learning
and generative fine-tuning.

ST-GCN parameters and train the Semantic Fusion and Decoding Network (SFDN) for autoregressive text generation
with token-level cross-entropy.

Given the fine-grained motion descriptions generated by MOSAIC (Section 4), we further perform high-level activity

sensing data, presenting generated motion descriptions, and displaying the inferred activity results, while the motion

7 EXPERIMENT
7.1 Dataset
7.1.1 3D Skeleton Corpus for Training Dataset. Our training corpus is constructed from two complementary sources:
real motion data from the HumanML3D dataset and synthetic skeleton sequences generated by T2M-GPT.
HumanML3D is a large-scale benchmark for human motion understanding, containing 14,616 motion skeleton
sequences covering a total duration of 28.59 hours. Each sequence is approximately 7.1 seconds long on average and
is paired with at least three precise and diverse textual descriptions, resulting in a total of 44,970 natural language
captions. T2M-GPT is a generative framework that synthesizes human motion from text. Given a natural language
description, T2M-GPT produces a sequence of 3D human skeletons that semantically correspond to the described
activity. These virtual motion-text pairs enrich the corpus with underrepresented or rare activities that are not
frequently observed in real-world datasets. In total, our 3D skeleton corpus comprises 49,873 Sequence–Text pairs,
which together support effective training of the sensor-language alignment.
7.1.2 Downstream Evaluation Datasets. We conduct experiments on the most extensive wearable HAR benchmark.
This benchmark comprises 18 real-world datasets, each capturing diverse activities under varying sensing conditions.
These datasets were collected from a wide range of body locations, including the head, chest, back, arms, wrists, waist,
hips, legs, knees, and ankles. The activities span both coarse-grained actions such as stand, walk, and run, as well as
fine-grained and complex daily behaviors such as tennis right-hand forehand swing, vacuum cleaning, or computer
typing. The 18 datasets used in our evaluation are: Opportunity [42], UCI-HAR [3], MotionSense [35], w-HAR [5],
Shoaib [45], HAR70+ [54], RealWorld [51], TNDA-HAR [67], PAMAP2 [41], USC-HAD [71], Mhealth [4], Harth [34],
UT-Complex [46], Wharf [7], WISDM [59], DSADS [2], UTD-MHAD [10], MMAct [28].
7.2 Experimental Setup
7.2.1 Implementation Details. We implement our framework using PyTorch and train all models on four NVIDIA
A800 GPUs, each equipped with 80GB of memory. The semantic fusion and decoding network (Section 4.2) is initialized
with the publicly available pre-trained weights of LLaMA-7B, serving as the stacked Transformer decoder backbone.
During training, we set the batch size to 16 and adopt the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10−5 and
a weight decay of 0.01. The model is trained for 10 epochs, with gradient clipping (maximum norm of 1.0) applied
to stabilize the optimization process. Mixed-precision training with FP16 is employed to improve computational
efficiency and reduce memory consumption. For the LLM-Assisted Activity Understanding and Reasoning module
(Section 6), we adopt DeepSeek-v3 to perform compositional reasoning over textual motion descriptions generated by
MOSAIC (Section 4). We evaluate performance using both accuracy and F1 score, and all results are averaged over
five independent runs to ensure statistical robustness.
7.2.2 Hyperparameter Settings. For the MOSAIC, we set the input channel dimension according to the IMU feature
size. The model comprises 10 spatiotemporal graph convolutional (ST-GCN) (Section 4.1) blocks, with intermediate
output channel dimensions of 64, 128, 256, and 512. The temporal kernel size is set to 9, and the spatial kernel size is
automatically determined by the adjacency matrix of the underlying human skeleton graph. Batch normalization
is applied to the input data before feeding it into the graph convolution layers. The semantic fusion and decoding
network (Section 4.2) operates on 512-dimensional input embeddings, and the hidden dimension is set to 4096.

on how they integrate sensor signals with language models. (1) HAR Deep Architectures: DeepConvLSTM [47] and
IF-ConvTransformer [72]. (2) Prompt-based LLMs: HARGPT [26]. (3) Time-Series LLMs: CALF [32], TimeLLM [27],
GPT4TS [73], SensorLLM [30], UNITS [14]. (4) MLLMs with Sensor Signals: oneLLM [18], IMU2CLIP [37]. The baseline
details are as follows:
DeepConvLSTM. This method is a classical end-to-end deep architecture for wearable-sensor-based HAR. It first
applies convolutional layers to extract local temporal patterns and then employs LSTM layers to model sequential
dependencies over time. For a fair comparison, we keep its backbone architecture and train it on our large-scale
sensor-language corpus using contrastive learning. During inference, we compute the similarity between the learned
sensor embeddings and candidate label embeddings to obtain the final classification results on all 18 HAR test sets.
IF-ConvTransformer. This method is a Transformer-based HAR architecture. It uses convolutional modules to
enhance inertial feature extraction and Transformer self-attention to capture global temporal dependencies across
the sensor sequence.
OneLLM. This method maps eight different modalities—including IMU signals—into a unified representation space.
We adopt its released pretrained weights and evaluate them on our test sets. Note that oneLLM is not originally trained
Proc. ACM Interact. Mob. Wearable Ubiquitous Technol., Vol. 10, No. 3, Article 168. Publication date: September 2026.
168:14 • Wei et al.
for classification on the IMU modality; instead, it adopts a question-answering task. To ensure a fair comparison, we
apply the same evaluation protocol used for our model to compute classification performance.
IMU2CLIP. This method maps IMU time-series data into the CLIP embedding space by training a cross-modal
encoder, enabling zero-shot activity recognition via similarity matching with text embeddings. Since it is not trained
for classification, we follow the same evaluation protocol as our model to compute classification performance.
SensorLLM. This method aligns sensor signals with textual descriptions of their temporal trends, enabling LLMs to
understand time series. Since the pretrained weights are not publicly available, we follow the official training pipeline
and training dataset settings to reproduce SensorLLM, and then evaluate it on our test sets.
GPT4TS. This method refrains from altering the self-attention and feedforward layers of the residual blocks in a
pretrained language or vision model, and instead fine-tunes the model on a range of representative time-series tasks.
Following their training protocol, we fine-tune the model using the training set of the Mhealth dataset, and evaluate
its generalization performance on the test sets of the 18 real-world HAR datasets.
TimeLLM. It is a reprogramming framework designed to repurpose LLMs for general time series forecasting with the
backbone language models kept intact. Since it is not originally designed for classification, we adapt it to our setting
by training on our large-scale sensor-language corpus using contrastive learning. During inference, we compute the
similarity between the model’s output and all candidate label embeddings to obtain the final classification result. We
evaluate the trained model on all 18 real-world HAR test sets.
UNITS. It’s a unified multi-task time-series model that leverages task tokenization to integrate both predictive and
generative tasks within a single framework. We trained the UNITS on our large-scale sensor-language corpus using
contrastive learning, and computing similarity with label embeddings to obtain classification results on all 18 HAR
test sets.
CALF. This method reduces the distribution gap between text and time-series data via cross-modal fine-tuning,
enabling large language models to better understand temporal signals. We trained the CALF on our large-scale sensor-
language corpus using contrastive learning, and computing similarity with label embeddings to obtain classification
results on all 18 HAR test sets.
HARGPT. This method directly encodes the time-series signals into prompts for activity classification. Following
the original paper, we adopt the provided prompt template and downsample all test data to 10Hz as specified for
evaluation.
7.4 Main Results
7.4.1 Zero-shot Performance. We adopt a highly challenging evaluation protocol: testing the model on entirely
unseen datasets without any fine-tuning. This setting, often referred to as the zero-shot evaluation, is particularly
difficult due to the substantial distribution shifts between the training corpus and the test datasets. As shown in
Table 2, most existing LLM-based HAR methods struggle in this zero-shot scenario. In contrast, our proposed MOSAIC
achieves a remarkable average accuracy of 34.6% and F1 score of 28.2% across 18 diverse test datasets, significantly
outperforming all prior baselines. Compared to the best baseline, MOSAIC achieves up to 19.6% improvement in
accuracy and 19.8% in F1 score. The results clearly demonstrate that our model generalizes far better than all previous
methods, showcasing its superior cross-domain adaptability.
7.4.2 Few-shot Performance. In addition, we further conduct the few-shot setting to fine-tune MOSAIC through a
few real samples. As illustrated in Table 3, we provide 10 real samples per activity to fine-tune the model on each
unseen dataset and then evaluate its accuracy and F1 score. We observe a substantial performance improvement
compared to the zero-shot setting: the average accuracy increases from 34.6% to 54.5%, while the average F1 score
rises from 28.2% to 47.5%. Morever, our model achieves a 12.0% higher accuracy and a 13.1% higher F1 score compared
to the best-performing baseline. This demonstrates our model’s ability to adapt to new environments with minimal
supervision. It is particularly important in real-world applications where annotated data might be scarce or costly to
obtain.

Unlike generic text similarity metrics, ASSE explicitly evaluates whether a generated description captures the key
semantic components of human motion. Specifically, we decompose each description into four semantic categories:
1. Action: the type of motion, such as swipe, wave, clap, throw, swing, sit, or stand.
2. Trajectory: the direction or movement pattern, such as right-to-left, forward, upward, downward, or clockwise.
3. Side: the side of the body involved in the motion, such as left, right, both, or one side.
4. Limb: the body part involved in the motion, such as hand, arm, fist, elbow, or foot.
For each category, we compute the F1 score based on the overlap between the semantic elements extracted from the
generated and ground-truth descriptions. The final ASSE score is computed as a weighted sum of the category-level
F1 scores:

descriptions.

7.6 Ablation Studies
7.6.1 Ablation on the Number of Principal Components. To investigate the effect of dimensionality reduction on
model performance, we perform an ablation study on the number of principal components retained by PCA before
projecting sensor representations into the LLM semantic space. As shown in Figure 7, we experiment with varying
component counts (e.g., 500, 1000, 1500, 2000) and evaluate the downstream HAR performance. Results show that
using too few components leads to substantial information loss, degrading recognition accuracy. On the other hand,
increasing the number of components beyond a certain point yields diminishing returns while incurring additional
computational costs. We find that retaining 1500 components provides the best trade-off between expressiveness and
efficiency, and we use this setting in all other experiments.
7.6.2 Ablation on the Composition of the Training Corpus. We examine how different combinations of training
corpora affect the performance of MOSAIC. Specifically, we compare the performance when using: (1) only real
sensor-text pairs (w/o virtual), and (2) the combined full corpus. As shown in Figure 8, this leads to a clear performance
drop in accuracy, especially under the zero-shot setting. We attribute this degradation to the limited diversity of real
Proc. ACM Interact. Mob. Wearable Ubiquitous Technol., Vol. 10, No. 3, Article 168. Publication date: September 2026.
168:18 • Wei et al.
data, which tends to lack rare, static, or complex activities. As a result, the model struggles to learn a sufficiently
comprehensive and balanced representation of human motion. By contrast, incorporating synthetic samples enhances
the diversity of motion patterns and complements the real data with well-controlled, fine-grained behaviors. This
richer coverage enables MOSAIC to better generalize to unseen scenarios.
7.6.3 Ablation on the Spatiotemporal Graph Convolutional Network. To evaluate the effectiveness of Section 4.1,
we replace the ST-GCN backbone with LSTM. As shown in Figure 8, this replacement leads to a significant drop in
accuracy. The performance degradation demonstrates the necessity of modeling not only the temporal dynamics but
also the spatial structure of human motion signals.
7.6.4 Ablation on the Semantic Fusion and Decoding Network. To evaluate the impact of the Section 4.2, we systemati-
cally remove the multi-head cross-attention layer (w/o MHCA). As shown in Figure 8, this results in a noticeable
performance drop, indicating that simple feature concatenation is insufficient for establishing meaningful semantic
correspondences between sensor data and language. The MHCA module plays a crucial role in injecting language-
relevant information into the sensor representations, enabling better alignment with the LLM’s embedding space and
ultimately improving downstream reasoning and generation performance.

8 DISCUSSION
We acknowledge several limitations that we leave as future work: First, the current evaluation is still partially
constrained by existing HAR benchmarks. Although our goal is open-ended activity understanding, most public
wearable-sensing datasets provide only categorical activity labels rather than fine-grained descriptions of body-part
motions, temporal coordination, or activity semantics. As a result, our large-scale evaluation across 18 datasets still

relies on accuracy and F1 score as the primary quantitative metrics. Future work should build standardized HAU
benchmarks with natural-language annotations, body-part-level motion descriptions, temporal decomposition and
contextual information. Such benchmarks would allow the community to evaluate not only whether a model predicts
the correct activity, but also whether it truly understands how the activity is performed.
Second, our training strategy relies on a synthetic IMU–text corpus to overcome the absence of real-world paired
sensor–language data. However, synthetic IMU signals may still differ from real wearable data due to sensor noise,
device attachment variations, body-shape differences, and diverse personal motion styles. Future research should
collect real-world IMU–language datasets in which sensor streams are paired with fine-grained motion descriptions.
Such data would reduce the simulation-to-reality gap and provide stronger supervision for learning sensor-language
alignment.
Finally, we view MOSAIC as an initial step toward open-ended human activity understanding. Future work can
extend this framework to long-term continuous sensing, multi-person activities, personalized behavior modeling, and
interactive AI agents that reason about human intentions from sensor-grounded motion descriptions.
9 CONCLUSION
We present a new paradigm called open-ended human activity understanding, establishing a complete pipeline
from sensor perception to motion description and activity reasoning. To realize it, we propose MOSAIC (Motion
Decomposition and Semantic Fusion), a unified framework that captures structured meta-motion features from sensor
data and translates them into fine-grained natural-language descriptions. Built upon MOSAIC, we further design
an LLM-driven training strategy for sensor–language alignment, enabling effective cross-modal supervision even
in the absence of real-world paired data. Finally, we integrate MOSAIC and LLM-assisted reasoning into a mobile
application, forming an end-to-end sensing–reasoning–interaction process. Experiments on 18 public HAR datasets
show that our approach achieves SOTA performance among existing LLM-based methods.

Regarding the concern about the real-world applicability of our system, we have designed and conducted an
in-the-wild evaluation. We collected data from 39 participants aged between 18 and 32 years. Each participant wore
smartwatches on both wrists and placed smartphones in their left and right pockets, as shown in Figure 9. The dataset,
detailed in Table 6, includes sensor data collected at a sampling rate of 50Hz, capturing both accelerometer and
gyroscope data. We focused on four types of activities, each with distinct characteristics and challenges, to thoroughly
assess our system’s robustness in real-world conditions:
• Intermittent Sprint Running: To simulate a high-intensity, variable motion scenario, we chose an extreme training
setting involving intermittent sprinting, as shown in Figure 10. Specifically, participants alternated between
slow walking (≤ 6 km/h) and sprinting (≥ 13 km/h) in 21-minute sessions. A total of 18 sessions were recorded,
with more than 300 minutes of data collected in this category. We conducted Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO)
cross-validation, yielding an accuracy of 78.9% and an F1 score of 76.4%. This indicates that our model performs
robustly, even in challenging scenarios involving rapid transitions and high-intensity sprinting.
• Intermittent Sprint Cycling: We also evaluated the system under an extreme cycling scenario, where participants
alternated between slow cycling and sprint cycling. Each session lasted 20 minutes, and a total of 12 sessions
were recorded, amounting to over 250 minutes of data. We conducted LOSO cross-validation, yielding an
accuracy of 60.2% and an F1 score of 58.1%. While the model performs reasonably well, the accuracy and F1
score are lower compared to those for sprint running. We think this may be due to the absence of leg data.
• Strength Training: The strength training scenario focused on various phases, including stretching, warm-up
jogging, and dumbbell exercises. This session lasted between 20-25 minutes. We gathered data from 8 participants
performing these activities. We conducted LOSO cross-validation, yielding an accuracy of 71.2% and an F1 score
of 66.9%. These results demonstrate that the model’s ability to capture fine-grained behaviors helps it better
identify more complex actions.
• Daily Activities (Extended 12-Hour Session): Finally, we also recorded a 12-hour continuous data session that
covered a variety of typical daily activities, such as walking, eating, working, and sleeping. We divided the
12-hour continuous data into 12 sessions, each lasting 1 hour. Each session was further segmented into 10-minute
windows, and the predominant activity within each window was used as the label. We applied Leave-One-
Session-Out cross-validation, yielding an accuracy of 65.2% and an F1 score of 64.9%. These results show that
our model effectively recognizes daily activities even in long-duration, continuous settings.

B.2 The Impact of the DeepSeek Consistency Check.
To quantify the effect of the conservative reasoning constraint, we compare DeepSeek inference with and without the
instruction to output "unknown". All other inference settings are kept unchanged.
As shown in Table 8, the constraint causes 6.5% of the test samples to be classified as unknown. The accuracy and F1
score decrease slightly from 34.9% and 28.7% to 34.6% and 28.2%, respectively. These results indicate that the constraint
does not substantially improve closed-set recognition performance. Instead, it mainly provides a conservative option
that prevents the LLM from forcing unsupported predictions on ambiguous samples, with only a marginal impact on
the overall recognition performance.
