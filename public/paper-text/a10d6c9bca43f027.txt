BCRNet-SNN: Body Channel Response-Aware
Spiking Neural Network for User Recognition
Taewook Kang , Chanwoo Shin , Jongseok Lee , Jae-Jin Lee , Donggyu Sim, Senior Member, IEEE,
and Seong-Eun Kim , Senior Member, IEEE
Abstract—Spiking neural networks (SNNs) have been re-
cently highlighted as an attractive approach for implement-
ing artificial intelligence models in resource-constrained
edge devices for various industrial applications. These
SNNs leverage low-power and wide dynamic range pro-
cessing through biologically inspired event-driven opera-
tions in a massively parallel manner. In this respect, we
propose BCRNet-SNN, an SNN model designed to utilize
an electric body channel response (BCR) as a biometric
feature for user recognition, where each element in the
BCR dataset for 15 subjects is a 1-D vector comprising
380 feature points from the measured envelope by applying
chirp signals to the body. The network parameters for im-
plementing the posttrained BCRNet-SNN are inherited from
the proposed convolutional neural network (CNN) that ex-
tensively extracts BCR-based biometric features (BCRNet),
followed by applying knowledge distillation-based network
lightening process on BCRNet. The performance evalua-
tion results compared to ResNet18 and ResNet6 for the
BCR dataset show that BCRNet achieves a greater than 2%
and 1.4% improvement, respectively, in the average clas-
sification accuracy, while significantly reducing the num-
ber of network parameters to less than 1%. The student
network distilled from the teacher BCRNet (BCRNet-S) re-
quires only 5.1% network parameters than BCRNet, which
can be eligible for implementation in BCRNet-SNN. The pro-
posed structure of BCRNet-SNN to effectively accommo-
date the CNN-to-SNN-converted parameters from BCRNet-S
can achieve up to a maximum accuracy of 98.11%, without
observable performance degradation compared to BCRNet.

I. INTRODUCTION
AS the personal information progressively gains higher
accessibility through digital networks based on wide-
spread mobile Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices, the leakage
and forgery of that information have been emerging as serious
social concerns [1]. In this respect, the current user authentica-
tion systems for accessing private data associated with finance,
healthcare and medical records, and administration have become
increasingly demanding to secure high accuracy and reliabil-
ity [2]. Biometrics inherent in the unique individual biological
features of the fingerprint, face, and iris has been spotlighted as a
promising alternative to conventional recognition methods, such
as identification cards and password-based user authentication.
The biometrics can resolve falsified or misplaced problems that
occur in case of loss or theft and secure the convenience of not
having to remember or carry them [3]. If biometric informa-
tion is mutilated, it cannot function properly and be restored
to its original status. Moreover, information leakage is highly
likely to result in malicious spoofing attacks via forgery and
alterations [4]. Recent studies have presented biometric features
extracted from body channel responses (BCRs) as an effective
candidate for strengthening the security of conventional biomet-
rics. The BCR can be considered a consistent and unclonable
biometric method because individual body channel differences
stem from the intrinsic anatomical structure of the body, such as
tissues, muscles, fat, and bones [5], [6], [7], [8], [9].
This study was inspired by a previous study that proposed
BCR-based biometric features (BBFs) comprising a series of
respective upper and lower envelope vectors of the measured
BCRs by applying up- and down-chirp signals to the body
channel passed through from the left to right forefingers using
capacitive coupling [8]. This achieved a classification accuracy
of up to approximately 95.8% for a dataset, including 2644 data
elements for 15 subjects, using a kernel-based support vector
machine, where each data element comprised a BCR-envelope
vector consisting of 380 feature points [8], [10].
Currently, the performance of classification problems has
been dramatically enhanced by employing deep neural network
(DNN)-based algorithms, which can surmount the inefficient
aspects of the artificial neural networks (ANNs) in learning

processes as the networks become deeper, such as an increase in

values while synaptic weights are updated [11]. Convolutional
neural networks (CNNs) can effectively extract reliable features
based on convolution and pooling processes adaptive to DNN
structures, which show outstanding performance in classifica-
tion applications for vision and image data, such as medical
and autonomous vehicle image analyses [12]. However, because
CNNs require excessive computation and considerable power
consumption to achieve reliable performance, it is impractical
for CNN-based algorithms to operate in resource-limited de-
vices [13]. Consequently, current studies have primarily focused
on reducing network complexity while minimizing performance
loss to be implemented in mobile devices and the edges of IoT
networks [14].
Recently, spiking neural networks (SNNs) have been high-
lighted as a remarkable neural computing paradigm that can
realize ANN for low-power and low-latency data processing
using event-driven and asynchronous spike signals operated in
a massively parallel manner inherited from biological neural
systems [15], [16]. In particular, recent studies on neuromorphic
hardware, such as TrueNorth and Loihi, have demonstrated
that SNNs are highly advantageous in terms of fast infer-
ence and low power consumption [17], [18], [19]. Meanwhile,
because the discontinuous and nondifferential characteristics
of SNN make backpropagation-based training impossible,
many previous studies have presented approximate approaches
for training SNNs, such as spike-based backpropagation,
spike-driven equilibrium propagation, and surrogate gradi-
ent learning [20], [21]. These direct training methods re-
quire computation at each time step for forward and back-
ward propagation, resulting in considerable training time and
computational complexity.
This study presents an efficient alternative for implementing
a reliable posttrained SNN by adopting knowledge distillation
(KD) that can transfer extensively trained knowledge from the
CNN-based teacher network for biometric feature extraction
from the BCR dataset, BCRNet, to the lightweight student
network, BCRNet-S [22]. This can substantially reduce the
network parameter number to approximately 5% of that of
BCRNet while minimizing the training loss and considering the
structural and computational constraints in the CNN-to-SNN
conversion process. We built BCRNet-SNN based on BCRNet-S
using the proposed ANN-to-SNN conversion, including the
replacement of convolution operations with spike transmissions
employing properly scaled ANN parameters. With no observ-
able performance loss compared to BCRNet, BCRNet-SNN
achieved approximately 96.52% average accuracy for 15 subject
classifications.
The rest of this article is organized as follows. Section II
describes the BCR dataset used for evaluating BCRNet-SNN.
Section III presents the proposed methodology, including KD to
BCRNet-S from BCRNet and ANN-to-SNN conversion for con-
version from BCRNet-S to BCRNet-SNN. Section IV presents
the performance evaluation. Finally, Section V concludes this
article.
II. BCR DATASET
The electrical conductivity of the body attributed to the fluid
components enables the body to be a transmission medium for
electric signals, where the channel properties, including signal
pass loss and delay propagation, vary with individual physical
differences [23]. Previous studies presented various applications
utilizing body channels, such as communication systems us-
ing generalized body channel models and energy harvesting
systems using the antenna effects of the body [24]. The aim
of this study is to develop a highly reliable user recognition
system by leveraging nonreplicable biometric features extracted
from the BCR. These features are characterized by variations
in individual intrinsic resistance and capacitance, influenced
by the electrical behaviors of bones, fat, and muscles in the
body [23], [25]. Consequently, any interference during the BCR
measurement process, such as unauthorized signal interception
through physical contact or close proximity, definitively distorts
biometric features within the BCR. This capability enables
BCR-based user recognition systems to easily detect spoofing
attempts and safeguard against signal interception.
Signal transmission within the body can be primarily catego-
rized into capacitive and galvanic coupling [25]. In capacitive
coupling, electric signals are transmitted through current loops
formed between a signal electrode (SE) attached to the body
and a floating ground electrode (GE) coupled to the external
ground on both sides of the signal transmitter (Tx) and receiver
(Rx). In galvanic coupling, both the SE and GE of the respective
Tx and Rx are attached to the body, utilizing it as a waveguide
for signal transmission paths by coupling alternating currents
through the body. Given the BCR measurement environment
between the Tx and Rx, this study primarily focuses on capac-
itive coupling. This approach enables the proposed recognition
system to achieve higher signal-to-noise ratios compared to
galvanic coupling, particularly for longer transmission distances
across the entire human body, including narrow body channels,
such as the arms and legs [8], [25]. Fig. 1(a) and (b) shows
the experimental environment for measuring the BCRs in a

III. PROPOSED BCRNET-SNN MODEL
In this section, we describe the development process of the
SNN structure designed for user recognition through BCR. The
direct training of SNN is challenging because of the discon-
tinuous, nondifferentiable attributes of spike-based neuron ac-
tivation, which complicates the use of traditional backpropaga-
tion methods and requires substantial memory for computation.
To address this, we first developed a high-performance deep
learning structure based on CNN mechanisms and then created
a compact student network from the larger teacher network
by utilizing an offline KD method. This approach effectively
transferred learned features and behaviors from the teacher to the
student network. Finally, we convert the student network into an
SNN model. The following subsections provide comprehensive
details of each procedure.
A. Data Preprocessing

points. These raw features manifest limited distinctive char-
acteristics across the 15 subjects. To maximize intersubject
differences, we implemented a standardization method for each
feature point across the entire range of feature vectors collected
from the 15 subjects. The standardization process is as follows:

B. BCRNet Model
The main objective of this study was to develop a shallow neu-
ral network designed for low-power computing environments,
which is an essential consideration for widespread practical
IoT devices. To this end, minimizing the network parameters
is crucial for preserving a balance between efficiency and per-
formance. Hence, we adopt a simpler yet highly effective CNN
structure [26].
The proposed BCRNet is mainly composed of two 1D-
convolutional layers with a rectified linear unit (ReLU) acti-
vation function, followed by three fully connected layers with
ReLU6 and softmax activation functions. The first convolutional
layer performs temporal convolutions with 16 filters of size
7 × 1, employing a stride of three and zero padding, effectively
reducing the input size by a third and extracting 16 feature maps.
Subsequently,theseoutputsarefedintothesecondconvolutional
layer, which uses eight filters of size 5 × 1 and a stride of
two, resulting in an 8 × 32 feature map. This feature map is
concatenated into a 1 × 256 vector and fed into fully connected
layer with an output size of 128. The ReLU6 activation function
processes this output, capping the maximum at six. ReLU6 is
particularly beneficial in low-power scenarios because it allows
the activated values to be presented with only three bits. The
output is fed into two additional fully connected layers with
output sizes of 64 and 15. The final layer uses a softmax function
forclassification.Fig.4showstheproposedstructureasateacher
network.

D. Knowledge Distillation

transfer of knowledge from the larger, complex teacher network
(BCRNet) to the smaller, efficient student network (BCRNet-S).
This process empowers the student network to maintain the high
performance of the teacher network while drastically reducing
parameter count. Through KD, the student network learns to
generalize crucial data patterns using interclass relationships
gleaned by the teacher network during extensive training on
large datasets. Numerous studies attest to the efficacy of this
approach in significantly boosting the performance of smaller
networks, equipping them to rival their larger counterparts ef-
fectively [22]. Fig. 4 illustrates the KD process along with the
structures of the proposed teacher and student networks. In this
study, KD was leveraged to transfer the knowledge gained by
the pretrained teacher model specialized in time-series datasets
to a student model. Here, “knowledge” is conceptualized as the

E. BCRNet-SNN Model
As shown in Fig. 4, the proposed BCRNet-SNN is con-
verted from BCRNet-S, which inherits the training results from
BCRNet, where the network consists of an integrated-and-fire
(IF) neuron model with an excitatory postsynaptic potential or
inhibitory postsynaptic potential synapse model. The features
between the layers of BCRNet-S are encoded through spike
transmissions from the input to the output neurons of each layer
of BCRNet-SNN in proportion to the values for a given time
window based on rate coding [27]. In addition, the decision for
the candidate subject was made by the output neuron with the
highest spike rate in the neuron group2 (NG2) of the SNN fully
connected layer (SNN-FC-layer). The synapses indicate the
conversions of the connections established by the convolution
filter taps in the student-convolution-layers (Std-conv-layers)
into connections between the input and output neurons in the
SNN layers. Biases in the ANN model were substituted by
bias neurons scaled by the threshold value Vth in the respective
SNN layer, where Vth triggered spike firing when the sum of
the weighted spikes was greater than Vth in the IF neuron. All
connections in the student fully connected layer (Std-FC-layer)
were converted into synapses between the input and output
neurons in the SNN-FC-layer.

A. Experimental Setup
The teacher model (BCRNet) is initially trained using a train-
ing dataset through supervised learning with CE loss. This step
enables the teacher model to capture the fundamental patterns
and attributes essential for the subsequent KD process. Follow-
ing primary training, knowledge transfer to the student network
(BCRNets-S) was facilitated using the KD loss in (6). Through
this process, the student network strives to acquire the teacher’s
advanced knowledge, thereby enhancing its performance. Fi-
nally, the student model was transformed into an SNN model
using the proposed ANN-to-SNN conversion method to achieve
low-power and efficient networks suitable for broader IoT appli-
cations. During the test phase, fivefold cross-validation was uti-
lized to evaluate the generalization ability of the model robustly.

C. Comparisons to Conventional Biometric Modalities
Conventional iris recognition systems offer low false accep-
tance rates (FAR) between 0.0001% and 0.01% and false rejec-
tion rates (FRR) between 0.1% and 0.5% [30]. Compared with
other biometric techniques, the probability of different people’s
irises being identical is extremely low. However, the installation
and maintenance costs of the infrastructure can be high, making
it cost-prohibitive for small businesses or organizations with
limited resources. It generally requires close proximity to the
camera, which can cause discomfort for some users. As the
most practical and well-utilized biometric because of its easy
and intuitive usability, fingerprint patterns offer cost-effective
implementation with a small form factor while ensuring a highly
reliable accuracy, with FAR between 0.01% and 0.1% and FRR
between 0.1% and 1.0% [30]. However, compared to other bio-
metric measures, the innate biometric features of fingerprints are
vulnerable to mutilation or leakage, which can lead to spoofing
attacks through forgery and alteration attempts using artificial
fingerprints [9].
The proposed BCR-based recognition achieved an FAR of
0.02% with a standard deviation of 0.0024, and an FRR of
2.78% with a standard deviation of 0.0385. The high FRR was
attributed to the small number of samples per class, as even a
single error could significantly affect the overall results. The size
of the BCR dataset employed in this study was insufficient to
accurately measure the FARs and FRRs compared with mature
technologies that have been extensively researched for a long
time, such as fingerprint or iris recognition. While the FRR of
BCR evaluated by BCRNet-SNN was slightly higher than those
of fingerprint biometrics, one of the standout benefits of BCR
was its remarkable resistance to replication and reproduction. In
this respect, as a form of multifactor authentication, BCR can
effectively complement fingerprint biometrics by enhancing se-
curitybecauseitcanbemeasuredsimultaneouslywhilescanning
the fingerprint with the same posture. The proposed BCR-based
recognition system can serve as a highly secure and convenient
alternative or supplementary measure that enhances the security
of current biometrics, such as fingerprints for authentication in
allareasoflife,includingfinance,administration,accesscontrol,
welfare, and healthcare.

V. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
A. BCRNet-SNN for User Recognition System
This study introduces BCRNet-SNN, an SNN architecture
leveraging electric BCR for user recognition. BCRNet-SNN
stands out for its low latency, low power consumption, and
wide dynamic range, which are essential for biometric sys-
tems to be embedded in resource-constrained IoT devices.
The network evolves from BCRNet, a CNN model adept at
extracting BCR-based features, and is further refined through
KD to reduce complexity significantly. Our evaluations show
that BCRNet surpasses established models, such as ResNet18
and ResNet6, by over 2% in accuracy, drastically reducing
the network parameters. The distilled BCRNet-S requires only
5.1% of BCRNet’s parameters to be aligned with the SNN
framework. The BCRNet-SNN model achieves an impressive
maximum accuracy of 98.11%, demonstrating no significant
performancedegradationcomparedwithBCRNet. This achieve-
ment highlights the efficiency of our CNN-to-SNN conversion
process, making BCRNet-SNN a benchmark in neural network
design for biometric recognition. This study contributes to ad-
vanced neural network architectures and sets a new standard
for energy-efficient high-performance computing models that

B. Future Study on BCR-Based User Recognition
1) Extended Experiments With an Increased Subject Pool:
Implementing BCR data acquisition methods that consider
various environmental effects, such as interference signals from
neighboring electronic devices and temperature fluctuations af-
fecting skin moisture levels and user clothing, can significantly
enhance the generalizability of BCRNet-SNNs. Long-term data
acquisition experiments are essential to explore factors, such as
health and emotional changes over time, which influence BCR
variations. These experiments should consider these factors as
control variables to ensure the stability of BCR features over
extended periods.
2) Feature Update and Recalibration Methods: The feature
update policy to compensate for physiological or environmental
variations should be tailored to the specific service conditions
of applications, including factors, such as the number of users,
security level, and frequency of use. This approach minimizes
the inconvenience of using a BCR-based recognition system.
The model can adopt an update process that involves refining
the existing model through fine-tuning with updated datasets
derived from the latest measured BCRs, which are then applied
to current recognition processes.
3) Implementation of a Hardware Structure Customized for
BCRNet-SNN: Unlike conventional ANNs, SNNs operate in
an event-based paradigm that requires specialized hardware
specifications. Achieving optimal efficiency in SNN execution
necessitates support for parallel and distributed processing, po-
tentially requiring hardware accelerators or specialized proces-
sors designed for event-based processing. Therefore, developing
efficient algorithms and hardware architectures tailored to SNN
requirements is crucial to fully realizing their potential. In our
study, we explored SNNs as alternatives to traditional neural net-
works to develop a BCR-based recognition system specifically
optimized for resource-constrained edge devices.
4) Scalability for BCRNet-SNN: The BCRNet-SNN frame-
work,optimizedforBCRdatausingashallowBCRNet-Smodel,
may not be directly applicable to larger, more complex datasets,
such as images, speech, or text. However, our framework can
be adapted to such scenarios through modifications. For larger
datasets, the network architecture must be deepened with addi-
tional convolutional and fully connected layers and enhanced
feature extraction techniques, such as attention mechanisms.
Although the specific architecture of the BCRNet-SNN is not
directly scalable, the underlying framework is adaptable. By
training a larger, complex model for the task, applying KD to
create a smaller, efficient student model, and then converting it
to an SNN, scalable and efficient SNN models can be developed
for a wide range of applications. This approach ensures that the
benefits of SNNs, such as their energy efficiency and temporal
processing capabilities, are effectively maintained.
