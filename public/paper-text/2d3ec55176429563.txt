Abstract
Streaming dense video captioning requires real-time pro-
cessing of continuous visual input while determining pre-
cisely when and what to caption. Current approaches
primarily focus on designing complex external memory
mechanisms, failing to leverage Large Multimodal Models’
(LMMs) inherent long-context capabilities. Moreover, exist-
ing methods employing threshold-based caption triggering
face a severe Threshold-Gated Discrepancy (TGD) prob-
lem, a training-inference mismatch arising from data imbal-
ance, where models predominantly predict silence tokens,
requiring thresholds that vary drastically across videos with
extremely narrow effective ranges. We introduce Takusen,
an asynchronous temporal modeling two-agent framework
comprising a Small Multimodal Model (SMM) as an Or-
acle agent and an LMM as a Listener agent. The Or-
acle agent processes sparse video inputs at an acceler-
ated rate to detect event boundaries, while the Listener
agent processes dense inputs to generate accurate captions
when prompted by the Oracle’s signals. This architecture
eliminates threshold dependencies by fundamentally chang-
ing how silence/generation decisions are made, resolving
the TGD problem. To enhance robustness against bound-
ary prediction instabilities, we integrate uniformly dis-
tributed fixed decoding points with Oracle-predicted bound-
aries. Experiments on ActivityNet Captions and YouCook2
datasets demonstrate that Takusen achieves state-of-the-art
performance with a simpler and more efficient design that
balances temporal sensitivity with descriptive accuracy.
1. Introduction
Dense video captioning [22, 23, 61, 70, 82] has become in-
creasingly important with the proliferation of online video
platforms. Unlike conventional dense video captioning
which operates on complete videos, streaming dense video
captioning [3, 84] presents unique challenges: (1) main-
taining vision-language memory while processing con-

tinuous input, and (2) determining when to output cap-
tions in real-time. When investigating existing models for
streaming video understanding, we observed that recent
approaches have focused on designing complex memory
mechanisms [3, 22, 50, 84] and using fixed decoding points
for caption generation [84]. However, these approaches
often overlook the inherent long-context memory capabil-
ities of Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) [12, 13, 17,
18, 32, 36, 47, 54, 55, 76, 78], which have recently seen
rapid advancements. Many designs also frequently invoke
Large Language Models (LLMs) for decoding [70], failing
to leverage LMMs’ inherent single-turn long-context mem-
ory capabilities, and may introduce inefficiencies when pro-
cessing streaming inputs [45, 66].
To enable LMMs to perform streaming dense video
captioning in a single inference pass, a critical challenge
emerges: determining when to remain silent and when to
generate text. This introduces what we identify as the
Threshold-Gated Discrepancy (TGD) problem. The TGD

problem stems from the severe data imbalance [58, 71] in
streaming captioning tasks, where the model must remain
silent for the majority of frames. This imbalance causes
models to favor predicting silence, requiring a threshold
at inference time to override this bias, which introduces
a training-inference mismatch since no threshold exists
during training. Training-inference mismatch is common
in video summarization tasks [37, 81] but does not lead
to TGD. As shown in Figure 2, our preliminary experi-
ments on streaming dense captioning reveal that appropriate
thresholds vary significantly across videos, with extremely
narrow effective ranges; even slight deviations can cause
models to either remain completely silent or generate ex-
cessive captions for every frame. Designing an additional
network to dynamically predict these thresholds is imprac-
tical given their video-specific nature and narrow effective
ranges.
To address these challenges, we propose Takusen, an
asynchronous temporal modeling framework comprising a
Small Multimodal Model (SMM) acting as an Oracle and a
Large Video-Language Model (LMM) functioning as a Lis-
tener. This design effectively leverages the inherent capa-
bilities of VLMs while elegantly solving the TGD problem:
(1) The Oracle Agent processes sparse streaming video in-
puts (sufficient for temporal localization) at an accelerated
rate, focusing on detecting event boundaries. Its speed al-
lows it to “see” further into the future than the Listener
Agent, anticipating event boundaries (both starting and end-
ing timestamps). (2) The Listener Agent receives dense
streaming video inputs to gather sufficient information for
accurate captioning. When the Listener reaches a position
where the Oracle has detected an event boundary, the Or-
acle signals the Listener to either acknowledge a starting
event (e.g., “I will track this event from now on”) or gener-
ate a caption for a completed event. This approach enables
the Listener Agent to confidently remain silent most of the
time, awaiting signals from the Oracle Agent. Crucially,
this eliminates the need for threshold-based decisions dur-
ing both training and inference, resolving the TGD problem
by fundamentally changing how silence/generation deci-
sions are made. To enhance robustness against the Oracle’s
occasional boundary prediction instabilities (where bound-
ary pairs may be excessive, insufficient, or incomplete), we
also incorporate uniformly distributed fixed decoding points

alongside Oracle-predicted boundaries. These fixed points
allow the Listener to generate additional captions at regular
intervals, improving overall captioning quality by comple-
menting the Oracle’s predictions. As shown in Figure 1, the
Oracle Agent identifies temporal event boundaries, while
the Listener Agent is fine-tuned on streaming data to gener-
ate accurate captions. All streaming frames, text prompts,
and responses are handled in a single inference turn by
the video LMM, eliminating the need for complex external
memory mechanisms.
Experiments on standard benchmark datasets including
ActivityNet Captions [23] and Youcook2 [82] demonstrate
that Takusen achieves state-of-the-art performance in
streaming dense video captioning while maintaining a
simpler and more efficient architecture.
In summary, our contributions are:
• We introduce Takusen, an asynchronous streaming
two-agent framework that leverages the complementary
strengths of small and large multimodal models to effec-
tively perform streaming dense video captioning.
• We identify and solve the Threshold-Gated Discrepancy
problem through our Oracle-Listener architecture, elimi-
nating the need for problematic threshold-based caption
triggering.
• Experiments on standard dense video captioning datasets
validate our approach, demonstrating how to leverage
LMMs’ inherent long-context capabilities for streaming
captioning without complex external memory.
2. Related Works
Dense Video Captioning. Dense video captioning local-
izes events and generates descriptions for each segment
[60]. Hierarchical methods enhanced context modeling
and narrative coherence [2], while transformer-based
approaches jointly optimized event localization and
caption generation [61, 83, 86]. Memory-augmented
frameworks extended temporal awareness, improving
caption accuracy [22, 26, 68]. Large-scale pre-training en-
hanced generalization and cross-modal alignment [29, 70].
Streaming solutions address real-time requirements by
processing video incrementally and generating on-the-
fly captions [39, 84]. Cross-attention mechanisms and
adaptive sampling calibrate focus across temporal spans
[5, 7], while progressive strategies refine event proposals
using hierarchical memory and cross-modal retrieval
[22, 50]. Transformer-based dynamic queries improve
event proposal and captioning pipelines [26, 61]. These
developments inform our unified streaming multimodal
large language model for dense event captioning with
minimal overhead.
Streaming Video Understanding. Real-time video an-
alytics has grown important with the rise of untrimmed
streams. JOADAA [11] recognizes actions as they occur
while anticipating future activities. Online detection
strategies reduce latency by identifying action starts in
continuous data [49]. Reinforcement learning enables
dynamic adaptation in online tracking [52], while weakly
supervised approaches reduce annotation requirements [9].
Progressive knowledge distillation improves efficiency at
scale [80]. Transformer-based models like OadTR achieve
low-latency inference through refined attention [62], with
continual learning addressing shifting distributions in
real-world streams [4, 34, 45]. Edge-cloud infrastructures
distribute computation to reduce bandwidth and server
loads [21], while adaptive optimizations handle varying
video content [51]. Surveys highlight challenges in bi-
trate adaptation and resource allocation [24, 72]. FPGA
solutions accelerate processing for UHD content [20, 64],
while selective frame filtering balances accuracy and
cost [30, 38]. Large-scale edge platforms demonstrate
feasible real-time systems [16], with advances in instance
segmentation and few-shot recognition handling occlusions
and novel categories [41, 42, 63, 75]. Volumetric streaming
shows 3D data potential [25], while incremental modeling
maintains performance over time [4, 34, 36, 45]. Integra-
tion of large language models enables richer multimodal
interpretations.
Large Multimodal Models. Large multimodal models
(LMMs) have advanced multimodal learning through uni-
fied representations bridging vision and language [1, 29,
73]. Early transformer-based approaches established cross-
modal alignment [19, 27, 43, 53], later enhanced by pixel-
level techniques and momentum distillation for improved
robustness [28]. Significant generalization gains emerged
from scaling with massive datasets and combining frozen
visual encoders with large language models [1, 29]. Con-
trastive learning on extensive image-text and video-text
pairs has proven essential for extracting rich contextual
embeddings [35, 69], while instruction tuning expands
model applicability across diverse tasks [77, 78]. Tem-
poral modeling in LMMs addresses video-specific chal-
lenges through spatial-temporal token merging, masked
auto-encoding, and sparse attention mechanisms [14, 33].
Long video transformers introduce efficient architectures
capturing extended dependencies without computational
excess [65, 67]. Chain-of-thought approaches enable step-
wise multimodal reasoning for complex analyses [57, 79].
Integrated video and image encoders create shared em-
beddings improving cross-domain transfer [1, 69], while
instruction-tuned LMMs facilitate sophisticated composi-
tional tasks [6, 78]. Recent surveys confirm these models’
transformative impact across multiple domains [48, 56, 77].

3. Method: Takusen
3.1. Preliminaries
Dense Video Captioning. The goal of dense video cap-
tioning is to predict a set of temporally localized captions
{(si, ei, ci)}ne

Streaming Dense Video Captioning. Unlike conventional
dense video captioning which operates on complete videos,
streaming dense video captioning processes video in real-
time as frames become available [84]. Formally, given a
continuous video stream from time t0 up to the current
time tc, denoted as V t0≤t≤tc
∈ R(tc−t0)×H×W ×3

model must predict a set of temporally localized captions
{(si, ei, ci)} where t0 ≤ si < ei ≤ tc for all detected
events. This task presents two major challenges: (1) de-
termining whether an event boundary has been reached at
the current time tc, and (2) generating appropriate captions
for completed events, using only frames observed up to tc
without access to future information.
3.2. Overview
Takusen is an asynchronous temporal modeling frame-
work designed to overcome the limitations of existing
streaming dense video captioning approaches. As shown in
Figure 3, our framework consists of two key components:
an Oracle agent and a Listener agent.
Oracle agent. The Oracle agent is a lightweight small
multimodal model (SMM) responsible for detecting event
boundaries in the video stream. It features a vision encoder
that processes video frames, a language decoder that
generates captions, and sometimes, additional localization
heads designed to predict precise numerical timestamps.
Listener agent. The Listener agent is a large multimodal
model (LMM) that receives streaming video frames and
generates captions based on Oracle messages. It adopts
a modular architecture integrating a pretrained vision en-
coder, a learnable projector, and a pre-trained decoder-only
LLM. The implementation details of both Oracle and
Listener can be found in Section 4.1.
The Oracle agent “sees” further ahead in the video
stream than the Listener, detecting event boundaries and
2802
signaling the Listener when to acknowledge events start or
generate captions for all events within a single-turn infer-
ence. This approach eliminates the need for threshold-based
decision-making, avoiding the TGD problem (introduced in
Section 1) while leveraging the inherent long-context ca-
pabilities of large multimodal models, and eliminating the
need for complex external memory mechanisms. We in-
troduce the asynchronous streaming processing of Oracle
and Listener in Section 3.3 and how they interact during the
event caption decoding in Section 3.4. The training method
is covered in Section 3.5.

3.4. Interactive Streaming Event Decoding
Oracle: Message Preparation. Based on the extracted
features, the Oracle predicts event boundaries {(si, ei)}
where t0 ≤ si < ei ≤ tc(O). This can be accomplished
through: using localization heads for direct regression (if
available) or through text generation with the language
decoder, from which boundaries can be extracted. For
each detected boundary pair (si, ei), the Oracle constructs
prompt messages {(Msi
O , Mei
O )} based on predefined
templates (provided in the supplementary material). These
messages instruct the Listener to: to start tracking an
event at time si (e.g., “Event i starts now.”) and generate a
caption at time ei (e.g., “Event i has ended. Please describe
it.”). This boundary information is passed to the Listener
agent, guiding its captioning behavior without requiring
threshold-based decisions, allowing the Listener to focus
on high-quality caption generation.
Listener: Next Token Prediction with Silence. While the
Oracle may be invoked multiple times, the Listener pro-
cesses the entire video stream in a single, continuous in-
ference pass. Upon acquiring video tokens from vision
encoder at each timestamp, the LLM ingests these tokens
auto-regressively and predicts the next token:
  \max ~P(w_j|w_{0:j-1}, F_L^{t\leq t_{c(L)}})  (1)
where w0:j−1 represents the previous tokens and wj can be
either a text token or a special Silence Token ∅. When
the Listener reaches a timestamp that matches an Oracle-
detected boundary (tc(L) = si or tc(L) = ei), it updates its
context with the corresponding Oracle message:
  w_{0:j-1} := w_{0:j-1} \cup M^{t_{c(L)}}_O  (2)
Listener: Event Caption Decoding. Based on the Oracle
messages and its own processing: At event start times-
tamps (tc(L) = si), the Listener acknowledges tracking
the event (e.g., “Sure, I will track Event i”). At event
end timestamps (tc(L) = ei), the Listener generates a
caption ci = [wj, ..., wj+k−1] describing the event, where
wj+k = ∅ indicates the end of the caption. At all other
timestamps, the Listener predicts the Silence Token ∅ and
continues processing the next frame.
Enhanced Robustness with Fixed Decoding Points. To
address potential instabilities in Oracle boundary predic-
tions, we incorporate Fixed Decoding Points (FDPs) dur-
ing inference. These uniformly distributed points {di}
complement Oracle-detected boundaries by generating ad-
ditional prompts {(Ms=di−1
, Me=di
)} at regular intervals.
When tc = di, these messages are passed to the Listener,
prompting it to generate additional captions that may cap-
ture events missed by the Oracle.
3.5. Model Training
Training Objective. Inspired by [5], Takusen is trained
with a hybrid loss function that balances text generation at
event boundaries with silence maintenance between events:
  L = \frac {1}{N}\sum ^{N-1}_{j=0}(- \mathbb {I}^{[w_j\neq \varnothing ]}\log P_{j}^{w_{j}} - \mathbb {I}^{[w_j=\varnothing ]}\log P_{j}^\varnothing )  (3)
where I is a condition indicator, and Pj denotes the prob-
ability of the j-th token. This loss function encourages the
model to: generate accurate text tokens at event boundaries
(text loss) and maintain silence between events by predict-
ing silence tokens (silence loss). Crucially, unlike previous
approaches that introduce thresholds only during inference,
our training objective directly aligns with the inference
process, eliminating the training-inference mismatch that
characterizes the TGD problem.

4. Experiment
4.1. Experimental Setup
In this section, we detail our experimental setup, including
the dataset and benchmark, evaluation metrics, and imple-
mentation details. We conduct experiments on two widely
used benchmarks for dense video captioning: ActivityNet
Captions [23] and YouCook2 [82].
ActivityNet Captions [23]. ActivityNet Captions dataset
is a large-scale benchmark for dense video captioning that
includes 8,649 training videos and 4,267 validation clips.
Each untrimmed video captures multiple action segments,
with approximately 3.7 distinct events per clip on average.
The videos have an average duration of two minutes,
making this a diverse collection that spans a wide range of
everyday activities. The captions are densely annotated,
providing temporal boundaries for each described event,
which enables more fine-grained video understanding.
YouCook2 [82]. YouCook2 dataset is a challenging
cooking video collection comprising 1,333 training and
456 validation videos from YouTube. Each video is
approximately 5.3 minutes long and contains about 7.8
distinct cooking actions on average. The dataset spans 89
recipes across various cuisines, providing a rich domain for
understanding procedural activity. Each video is annotated
with temporally localized cooking steps and their corre-
sponding natural language descriptions, presenting unique
challenges, as models must recognize fine-grained cook-
ing actions, understand ingredient transformations, and
capture the procedural nature of food preparation processes.
Evaluation Metrics. Following prior work [61, 70, 84],
we adopt standard metrics for dense captioning. Our main
metric is CIDEr and F1 score, computed for each predicted
event whose Intersection-over-Union (IoU) with a ground-
truth segment exceeds thresholds in {0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9},
and then averaged. We also report METEOR using the
same threshold approach and SODAc [8], which aggregates
all event captions within a video for a more comprehensive
evaluation. BLEU-4 [40] and ROUGE-L [31] are also
reported in the ablation study.

4.2. Main Results and Comparisons
Tables 1 and 2 summarize our comparisons with state-of-
the-art models. Despite operating in a streaming setting,
Takusen achieves new state-of-the-art performance across
multiple evaluation metrics on both datasets. On Activi-
tyNet Captions, Takusen achieves a CIDEr score of 43.7,
outperforming Streaming GIT [84] (+2.5) and Streaming
Vid2Seq [84] (+5.9), along with the best SODAc (7.5) and
F1-score (54.0). Similarly on YouCook2, Takusen main-
tains its advantage with a CIDEr score of 40.7, outperform-
ing Streaming GIT and Streaming Vid2Seq (+7.8). When
compared with Video-LLMs (Table 2), Takusen’s CIDEr
score of 43.7 significantly surpasses VideoLLaMA3 [76]
(26.8), TRACE-uni [13] (29.2), and VTG-LLM [12] (20.7),
while achieving the best F1-score (54.0), +8.8 compared to
AVicuna [55]. These results confirm that Takusen not
only outperforms all existing streaming models but also
achieves competitive results against non-streaming models
and dedicated Video-LLMs, despite operating under real-
time constraints. The integration of event-aware processing
and Silence Token Prediction effectively mitigates TGD,
enabling superior temporal alignment and contextual coher-
ence.
4.3. Ablation Studies
To analyze the effectiveness of each component in
Takusen, we conduct extensive ablation studies.
Impact of Core Components. From Table 3, accurate
event boundary prediction by the Oracle agent directly
boosts the quality of captions predicted by Listener, as re-
flected in higher metrics such as CIDEr. The stronger the
Oracle’s F1, the more effectively the Listener can generate
coherent and detailed descriptions. Introducing fixed de-
coding points (FDP) helps the Oracle balance precision and
recall, preventing overly strict or overly loose boundaries.
However, relying solely on the Oracle or FDP in isolation
yields suboptimal results; it is the synergy between the Or-
acle and the Listener that leads to the best overall perfor-
mance. Finally, when event boundaries are assumed to be
perfectly known (the boundary-aware setting), CIDEr rises
significantly, suggesting that further enhancing the Oracle
agent’s accuracy could substantially elevate the system’s
captioning capabilities.

4.4. Qualitative Results
Figure 5 presents qualitative comparisons with ground-truth
annotations and VideoLLaMA3-7B on ActivityNet Cap-
tions. Takusen produces captions that align well with
event boundaries while successfully detecting fine-grained
actions. In contrast, VideoLLaMA3-7B suffers from tem-
poral misalignment, overly brief descriptions, and frag-
mented event continuity. Takusen produces detailed and
temporally accurate captions, maintains event continuity
between actions, and avoids hallucinations. These results
validate the effectiveness of our Oracle-Listener framework
in achieving state-of-the-art streaming captioning with high
precision and temporal alignment. More qualitative results
can be found in Section C.
5. Conclusion
We present Takusen, an asynchronous temporal model-
ing two-agent framework for streaming dense video cap-
tioning. Our Oracle-Listener architecture pairs a small
multimodal model (Oracle agent) with a large multimodal
model (Listener agent), eliminating threshold-based trigger-
ing and resolving the Threshold-Gated Discrepancy prob-
lem. This approach enables efficient event boundary de-
tection and high-quality caption generation without com-
plex external memory. Experiments on ActivityNet Cap-
tions and YouCook2 show Takusen outperforms exist-
ing streaming methods while achieving competitive results
compared to full-video approaches, all with a simpler and
more efficient architecture.
