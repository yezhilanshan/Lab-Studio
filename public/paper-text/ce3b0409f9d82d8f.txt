Abstract—This paper presents a novel multimodal framework
for Human Activity Recognition (HAR) in homes that combines
Channel State Information (CSI) from WiFi access points with
visual data from camera images. This approach introduces a
dual-stream classification pipeline that leverages a Transformer-
based deep learning model for processing CSI features and
a computer vision model based on a fine-tuned Convolutional
Neural Network (CNN) for image-based activity classification.
To enable autonomous decision-making, an agentic AI mod-
ule based on the Falcon Large Language Model (LLM) has
been incorporated, which fuses predictions from both modalities
through a reasoning-driven mechanism. This integrated system is
capable of recognizing activities such as walking, sitting, standing,
and falling, and is particularly effective in scenarios requiring
real-time monitoring and emergency detection. Agentic AI-based
fusion is particularly important because false negatives can be
risky. Experimental results demonstrate that the fusion of RF and
visual cues significantly improves recognition accuracy compared
to unimodal approaches, offering a robust solution for intelligent,
non-intrusive HAR systems.
Index Terms—Human Activity Recognition, Channel State In-
formation, Computer Vision Models, Agentic AI, Fall Detection,
Multimodal Fusion, Deep Learning.
I. INTRODUCTION
Human Activity Recognition (HAR) has emerged as a
crucial component in a wide range of applications, including
healthcare monitoring, intelligent surveillance systems, smart
homes, and ambient assisted living [13]. Traditional HAR
techniques are mainly based on wearable sensors, such as
accelerometers and gyroscopes, or manual input, which can
be intrusive, inconvenient, and often require user compli-
ance. These limitations have driven the shift towards passive
sensing technologies that offer non-contact, continuous, and
environment-sensitive monitoring.
Among passive modalities, WiFi-based Channel State Infor-
mation (CSI) and camera-based image analysis have gained
significant traction [26]. CSI captures fine-grained signal
variations across multiple antennas and subcarriers, offering
temporal and spatial sensitivity to human movements and

environmental changes [9]. It enables activity recognition even
in non-line-of-sight (NLOS) scenarios, making it suitable for
deployment in complex indoor environments. For instance, by
processing amplitude and phase in the complex number rep-
resentation of the CSI, sensing systems can detect extremely
subtle changes such as the millimeter-level movement of a
chest during breathing [16]. In parallel, camera-based systems
leverage rich visual semantics to identify posture, motion, and
context, benefiting from recent advances in deep convolutional
neural networks (CNNs) and large-scale image datasets [21].
This paper presents a multimodal HAR framework that
integrates both CSI and image data to enhance recognition
performance. Specifically, we utilize a Large Language Model
(LLM)-inspired neural architecture for extracting and classi-
fying features from CSI signals, and an Image Classification
Model based on a fine-tuned convolutional neural network
for classifying human activities from video frames. To in-
telligently combine the outputs of both modalities, a Falcon
LLM model is introduced for implementing the agentic AI
server/module to perform the reasoned decision fusion, evalu-
ating both predictions in context to produce a final judgment.
Our results demonstrate that the combination of comple-
mentary sensory modalities leads to improved accuracy and
robustness in HAR, particularly in critical scenarios such as
fall detection. The proposed agentic fusion approach facilitates
autonomous decision making, opening pathways for real-world
deployment in safety-critical environments.
II. RELATED WORK
Human Activity Recognition (HAR) has been extensively
studied across multiple sensing domains, particularly in com-
puter vision and wireless signal processing. Early progress
in visual activity recognition was driven by convolutional
neural networks (CNNs), with architectures such as ResNet
and EfficientNet demonstrating strong capability in extracting
spatial patterns from RGB images [11], [22]. More recently,
Vision Transformers (ViT) introduced attention-based mecha-
nisms that improved the modeling of long-range dependencies
within visual scenes, enabling stronger recognition perfor-
mance across a wide range of activities [8]. Despite these
advances, camera-based HAR remains sensitive to lighting
conditions, occlusion, and privacy limitations.
Parallel to computer vision, WiFi Channel State Information
(CSI) has emerged as a promising modality for passive and
privacy-preserving activity recognition. Early works such as
WiSee and CARM revealed that subtle changes in WiFi
propagation patterns can be mapped to human movements,
enabling gesture-level recognition using amplitude and phase
variations [1], [25]. Later systems like Widar and DenseSense
showed that CSI captures rich temporal and frequency-domain
information and can support robust recognition even in non-
line-of-sight indoor environments [26]. Most of these systems
rely on feature-level fusion or late-stage prediction fusion, but
generally lack an explicit reasoning mechanism to reconcile
conflicting signals or contextualize predictions.
The rise of Large Language Models (LLMs) and agentic
AI has introduced new opportunities for multimodal reasoning
and model orchestration. Models such as LLaMA [23] and
Falcon [2] have shown strong generalization capabilities and
the ability to perform tool-augmented reasoning and decision-
making. Techniques like chain-of-thought prompting and tool-
use frameworks, including Toolformer, highlight the potential
of LLMs to act as intelligent controllers capable of integrating
information from multiple sources [28], [19]. These develop-
ments open the door for LLMs to function as agents that can
evaluate, fuse, and reason over predictions from specialized
models.
The system presented in this work draws from all these
research directions. It combines a Transformer-based CSI
classifier, a CNN-based vision classifier, and an LLM-powered
agent to fuse both outputs through natural-language reasoning.
The framework also incorporates timestamp-aligned multi-
modal data and real-time emergency detection via Twilio,
enabling actionable responses such as fall detection. Compared
to prior multimodal HAR systems, the agentic AI approach
offers greater interpretability, flexibility, and robustness by
leveraging the reasoning capabilities of LLMs to make the
final decision.
III. METHODOLOGY
A. Data Acquisition
The dataset creation process for this Human Activity Recog-
nition (HAR) project involves generating a synchronized,
multimodal dataset that integrates visual frames from video
recordings with simulated Channel State Information (CSI)
values. The pipeline begins by organizing a directory of
activity-labeled videos depicting actions such as walking,
sitting, standing, and falling. Each video is processed using
OpenCV [6] to extract frames at a fixed rate (e.g., 5 frames per
second), where each frame acts as a visual representation of
the ongoing activity. To maintain temporal coherence between
modalities, a timestamp is generated for every extracted frame
using a simulated real-time base clock.
Fig. 1. Workflow of the multimodal Human Activity Recognition system
using CSI and visual data.
The methodology and workflow are illustrated in Figure 1.
Parallel to frame extraction, synthetic CSI data are generated
for each activity. This is achieved by creating complex-valued
matrices that simulate multipath wireless propagation across a
predefined number of subcarriers and antennas, specifically, 30
subcarriers and 3 antennas, similar to real-world 802.11n WiFi
hardware setups [9]. Activity-specific variability is introduced
into the simulated signal by injecting Gaussian noise based
on the expected motion intensity of each activity class. For
instance, high-mobility actions such as falling exhibit stronger
variations in CSI patterns compared to static actions like sitting
or standing [27].
From each CSI sample, relevant features are extracted,
including amplitude, phase, Channel Frequency Response
(CFR), Channel Impulse Response (CIR), Signal-to-Noise
Ratio (SNR), and Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI).
CIR is derived by applying the inverse Fast Fourier Transform
to the CFR, capturing the temporal characteristics of multipath
components, which is an essential factor for modeling human
activity in RF-based sensing systems [1].
Each extracted video frame is then synchronized with
the corresponding CSI feature vector based on timestamp
alignment and assigned the same activity label. The resulting
sample consists of the frame path, a flattened image array,
all CSI-derived features, the activity label, and its timestamp.
The entire dataset is constructed as a pandas DataFrame and
subsequently divided into training and testing subsets using a
stratified random sampling strategy to ensure balanced class
distribution.
This synchronized multimodal dataset enables training of
complementary AI models: a Computer Vision Model for
processing the image modality and a Transformer-based Deep
Learning Model for interpreting CSI features. These models
can be fused at decision time using a reasoning-capable large
language model (LLM), Falcon [17], allowing the system
to make final activity inferences. This architecture supports
safety-critical applications like fall detection, where both
modalities must concur on the prediction before the system
autonomously sends emergency alerts via SMS or other ac-
tions.
B. Feature Extraction
1) CSI Features
In wireless communication, Channel State Information
(CSI) represents detailed properties of the wireless propaga-
tion channel between a transmitter and receiver. It provides
complex-valued measurements, which capture both amplitude
and phase across multiple subcarriers and antennas, typically
using Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing (OFDM)
systems such as WiFi [9]. Because CSI captures fine-grained
temporal and spatial channel variations, it is highly sensitive
to environmental dynamics and human motion, making it a
compelling modality for Human Activity Recognition (HAR)
[1], [27].
In the dataset generation pipeline, synthetic CSI data are
simulated to emulate the effects of various human activi-
ties, including walking, sitting, standing, and falling. This
is achieved by constructing complex-valued matrices where
both real and imaginary components are drawn from normal
distributions. The activity-specific noise that is added is higher
for dynamic activities, such as falling and walking, and lower
for static ones like sitting or standing. This methodology
reflects empirical observations from real-world CSI hardware
where signal distortions intensify with movement.
For each simulated CSI matrix, a set of key features
comprising amplitude, phase, Channel Frequency Response
(CFR), Channel Impulse Response (CIR), Signal-to-Noise
Ratio (SNR), and Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI) is
extracted to represent the signal’s frequency- and time-domain
characteristics.
1) Amplitude: magnitude of complex CSI values across
subcarriers/antennas [27].
2) Phase: angle of complex CSI values; sensitive to motion.
3) CFR: flattened complex CSI matrix [9].
4) CIR: IFFT of CFR capturing multipath timing [1].
5) SNR: mean-to-std ratio of amplitude [27].
6) RSSI: average amplitude [27].
All features are concatenated into a one-dimensional vector,
forming the input representation for training machine learning
models. The spatial dimension (across antennas and subcar-
riers) and temporal characteristics (via CIR and timestamp-
ing) are both preserved, enabling robust learning of activity
patterns. These features are further synchronized with cor-
responding visual frames, forming a multimodal dataset that
integrates RF sensing with vision. Such joint representations
are particularly effective in scenarios where visual signals
alone may be unreliable, such as low lighting or occlusion.
2) Image Features
In the context of Human Activity Recognition (HAR),
images provide rich and intuitive visual information about a
subject’s posture, movement, and surrounding environment. In
this system, image frames are extracted from activity videos
and paired with simulated CSI data to form a synchronized
multimodal dataset.
The image feature extraction process begins with video
frame sampling. Each input video corresponds to a specific
activity, in this work, walking, sitting, standing, or falling, and
frames are extracted at a specified frame rate, in our work,
5 frames per second. These frames represent the temporal
evolution of human activities. For each sampled frame, the
following steps are applied to prepare the image data for
learning and inference.
1) Frame Extraction and Saving: The video is read using
OpenCV, and frames are sampled at fixed intervals. Each
frame is saved as an image file (e.g., JPEG) with a
timestamp and file path recorded, preserving temporal
context for synchronization with the CSI features.
2) Color Conversion and Flattening (dataset stage):
The extracted BGR image is converted to RGB for
consistency with vision frameworks. For bookkeeping,
a flattened 1D vector is stored (training uses structured
tensors) [7].
3) Preprocessing for Model Inference: Before CNN in-
ference:
a) Resize: image to 224 × 224 pixels to match pre-
trained backbones (e.g., ResNet, ViT) [8], [11].
b) Normalization: scale to [0, 1] and normalize with
ImageNet statistics [7].
c) Tensor Conversion: convert to (C×H×W) tensor
for model input.
4) Feature Extraction via Vision Backbone:
Pretrained/fine-tuned CNNs (EfficientNet, ResNet,
MobileNet, ViT) extract high-level features such as:
a) Edges/Contours/Shapes: for body orientation
[20].
b) Pose/Silhouette: for sitting vs. falling discrimina-
tion.
c) Context: scene cues, e.g., proximity to ground.
The classification head outputs logits over activities.
5) Prediction and Interpretation: Softmax converts logits
to probabilities; the top class is the predicted activity,
later fused with CSI-based inference for agentic decision
making.
By transforming raw video into structured, high-
dimensional image features, the model accurately recognizes
human activities [11], [22]. When combined with CSI in a
multimodal framework, robustness improves, especially under
occlusion or low lighting.
C. Transformer-based Deep Learning Model for CSI-based
Classification
Training begins by extracting CSI features (amplitude,
phase, CFR, CIR, SNR, RSSI). CIR is computed via IFFT
of CFR to capture multipath effects [27]. Multidimensional
complex-valued CSI is flattened into a numerical vector for
modeling. Inputs are z-score normalized; activity labels (sit-
ting, standing, walking, falling) are integer-encoded.
A Transformer classifier embeds each scalar feature, adds
positional encodings to preserve structure, and applies multi-
head self-attention across layers [24]. Mean pooling aggregates
sequence information, followed by layer normalization and a
Fig. 2. Twilio Interface SMS notification received by the emergency contact.
linear head producing class probabilities. The model is trained
with cross-entropy loss and AdamW optimization [14].
D. Computer Vision Model for HAR Using Images
A CNN-based pipeline recognizes activities from RGB
frames. Labeled frames are loaded via a custom PyTorch
Dataset, resized to 224 × 224, normalized, and batched.
Transfer learning uses EfficientNet-B0 [22] with its final layer
replaced to match four classes. The model is fine-tuned using
cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimizer.
E. Integration with LLM Model
The agentic HAR server fuses CSI and vision predictions
continuously. At each step, an image sample is selected and
its timestamp-matched CSI sample is retrieved. CSI features
(amplitude, phase, CFR, CIR, SNR, RSSI) feed the Trans-
former; the associated image feeds the EfficientNet [22]. If
both predict “falling,” an emergency SMS is triggered via
Twilio, reflecting cross-modal agreement for high-confidence
action [5]. Figure 2 illustrates the alerting behavior.
For reasoning and emergency detection, we incorporate
Falcon-RW-1B, a lightweight causal LLM. Pretrained on Re-
finedWeb, Falcon offers a speed/accuracy tradeoff suitable for
resource-constrained settings [12], [17]. The model processes
structured prompts containing both modalities’ predictions,
evaluates semantic agreement, and, when critical conditions
are met, triggers automated responses. This enables the system
to move beyond raw classification into agentic AI for proactive
decision-making. The LLM performs learned reasoning. It can
potentially infer a more logical final activity based on the lin-
guistic structure and semantic relationship between ”walking”
and ”standing,” offering a much more nuanced output than a
fixed rule. The LLM is also capable of handling ambiguity. It
is conceptually better at weighting different pieces of evidence
to produce a reasoned text output, as opposed to a fixed
priority system. The LLM makes the methodology scalable,
for instance, in case one wanted to add a third sensor, such as
an accelerometer, later.
IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
A. Dataset
The dataset targets multimodal learning for behavior recog-
nition [1]. Activity-labeled videos (walking, sitting, standing,
falling) are sampled uniformly (typically 5 fps) to extract
frames. A synthetic timing mechanism generates timestamps
[10]. For each frame, a synthetic CSI matrix (e.g., 30 subcarri-
ers, 3 antennas) is generated with activity-dependent Gaussian
noise. Features (amplitude, phase, CFR, CIR, SNR, RSSI) are
computed [1], [9] and concatenated.
Frames are saved as JPEG (RGB), and bookkeeping stores
flattened RGB vectors with timestamps [20]. Each sample
contains: timestamp, label, image path, flattened RGB, and
CSI features. Samples populate a Pandas DataFrame [15].
Stratified splits create balanced train/test sets; artifacts are
saved in CSV/pickle formats. The dataset captures spatial–
temporal (vision) and electromagnetic (CSI) evidence for
robust HAR [4].
B. Model Training
1) Deep Learning Model for CSI Training: Mini-batch
training minimizes classification error; performance is
examined via accuracy and confusion matrices [1].
2) Computer Vision Model for HAR using Images
Training: Supervised fine-tuning with Adam and cross-
entropy loss. Batch-wise metrics (loss/accuracy) track
convergence; the trained model is serialized for infer-
ence.

V. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The confusion matrices for the CSI and image classifications
are shown in Fig. 3, corresponding to accuracies of 92.81 %
for video and 65.27 % for CSI classifications. Diagonal dom-
inance indicates effective class discrimination. CSI confusion
is highest between Sitting and Standing (both low-motion
states). Occasional false negatives in falls are concerning, but
the visual model rarely misclassifies falls, supporting vision’s
dominance for safety-critical detection. This motivates fusing
CSI with vision.
Combining complementary modalities improves accuracy
and robustness, particularly for fall detection. CSI can capture
subtle motions invisible to cameras; vision dominates under
clear conditions but may fail under occlusion or low light.
Requiring agreement between both modalities yields high-
confidence, actionable decisions suitable for safety-critical
deployment.
A. Performance Analysis
The experimental results demonstrate complementary
strengths of both modalities. The vision model achieves
92.81% accuracy compared to CSI’s 65.27%, primarily due
to rich spatial information in visual frames. However, CSI
exhibits unique advantages in capturing motion dynamics in-
visible to cameras, particularly under occlusion or low lighting
[25], [30].

Confusion matrix analysis reveals distinct patterns: the
vision model excels in fall detection with minimal false
negatives, which is critical for safety applications. The CSI
model struggles with low-motion states (sitting vs. standing)
but shows better sensitivity to dynamic activities where RF
signal disturbances are pronounced [27].
The multimodal fusion strategy leverages these complemen-
tary characteristics. By requiring cross-modal agreement for
critical events, the system reduces false positives while main-
taining high sensitivity [18]. The agentic AI fusion mechanism
enables context-aware decision-making beyond simple voting,
evaluating semantic consistency and confidence levels through
the Falcon LLM [5].
Performance improvements are greatest for transitional ac-
tivities and fall events, where corroborating CSI evidence
enhances reliability. The system demonstrates real-time feasi-
bility with acceptable latency (1-2 seconds) for fall detection,
while the lightweight Falcon-RW-1B enables edge deployment
for privacy-preserving inference [29].
B. Comparative Analysis
To validate the effectiveness of the proposed agentic fu-
sion framework, a comparative performance analysis was
conducted between unimodal classifiers, conventional fusion
baselines, and the proposed LLM-based fusion approach [3].
Majority voting selects the final activity label based on agree-
ment between modalities, while weighted averaging combines
softmax probabilities using fixed modality weights. Although
Fig. 3. Confusion matrices for CSI and video classification.
these approaches provide modest improvements over unimodal
classification, prior studies have shown that such heuristic
fusion methods lack contextual reasoning capabilities and are
unable to robustly resolve conflicting predictions under uncer-
tainty [28]. Falling activities are modeled with higher signal
variability, reflected by an increased standard deviation, as falls
induce abrupt body motion and rapid multipath changes that
cause large fluctuations in CSI amplitude and phase [26].
In contrast, the proposed agentic fusion model leverages
a large language model (Falcon) to perform reasoning-based
decision fusion [19]. Instead of aggregating numeric confi-
dences, the LLM interprets semantic relationships between
predicted activities and applies task-aware decision logic,
drawing on recent advances in chain-of-thought reasoning and
tool-augmented language models [2].
The results in Table II demonstrate that while the classi-
fication based on the vision-only model achieves outstanding
baseline performance, fusing the CSI model through agentic
LLM-based rationality would lead to steady improvements
across all metrics. Especially, the proposed fusion approach
significantly improves recall, which is the most significant
metric for safety-sensitive applications such as fall detection
[4], [26], [28].
C. Limitations
The simulated CSI data is for demonstration and preliminary
testing purposes rather than a faithful representation of real-
world phenomena. The core of the simulation relies on gener-
ating a complex CSI matrix where the real and imaginary parts
are entirely random. The only difference between activities like
’falling’ and ’sitting’ is the standard deviation of the additional
random noise that is applied. The model does not account

VI. CONCLUSION
This work presented a multimodal framework for Human
Activity Recognition (HAR) that integrates Channel State
Information (CSI) obtained from WiFi signals and visual data
extracted from camera frames. A Transformer-based Deep
Learning model was employed for the classification of CSI-
derived features, while a Deep learning model, based on
a fine-tuned convolutional neural network, was utilized for
image-based activity recognition. An agentic AI reasoning
component, implemented using the Falcon LLM model, was
introduced to perform decision-level fusion by evaluating and
integrating the predictions from both models.
Future work includes extending the framework to incorpo-
rate additional sensing modalities such as audio or thermal
data, adopting sequential modeling techniques for continuous
activity tracking, and optimizing the system for deployment
on edge devices to support real-time, on-device inference in
resource-constrained settings.
