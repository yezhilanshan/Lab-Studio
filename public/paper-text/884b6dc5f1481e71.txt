1 Introduction
Mobile gaming has become a dominant form of digital entertain-
ment, accounting for nearly 57% of the global video game revenue[1].
Players engage in mobile games for various reasons, including re-
laxation, social interaction, and competition. However, along with
the rapid growth of mobile gaming, concerns have emerged regard-
ing its impact on players’ emotions and behaviors. Many players
experience negative emotions such as frustration, disappointment,
and anger due to in-game failures, network issues, or competitive
pressure. If left unregulated, these emotions may lead to problem-
atic behaviors such as excessive gaming, cyberbullying, and even
real-world emotional distress. Existing research has demonstrated
that players who enter a "rage loop" often struggle to disengage
from the game [4]. For instance, after consecutive failures, some
players refuse to quit and instead prolong their play sessions in
an attempt to "take revenge," thereby exacerbating negative emo-
tional cycles. This compulsive engagement not only affects mental
well-being but also contributes to gaming addiction.
Current interventions for in-game toxicity primarily rely on re-
active measures such as chat filters, along with block, mute, and
report functions.[69, 78] While these tools can shield the commu-
nity from overt hostility, they have critical limitations. First, they
are reactive, addressing toxic behavior only after an angry outburst
has already occurred, rather than preventing the underlying emo-
tional escalation. Second, these methods are designed to protect
others, but they do not help calm the frustrated player; in fact, being
blocked or reported may even aggravate their negative feelings.
Other approaches, like generic, time-based reminders to “take a
break,”[57]are often emotion-agnostic, failing to engage with the
player’s actual mental state and the specific in-game events that
trigger their frustration.
Our work introduces a proactive and non-disruptive interven-
tion framework tailored for fast-paced games where pausing is
not a viable option. We address the core challenge of designing
an intervention that is perceived as supportive rather than intru-
sive. To achieve this, we propose a system that leverages a Large
Language Model (LLM) to provide timely, personalized support
upon detecting the onset of player frustration. Unlike previous sys-
tems, our LLM utilizes real-time game events to generate concise,
customized messages delivered via an unobtrusive on-screen over-
lay. This context-aware approach ensures the feedback is directly
relevant to the player’s situation, offering empathetic guidance
over generic advice to help players regulate their emotions more
effectively.
Real-time emotion detection is essential for proactive interven-
tions. Traditional methods, such as camera-based facial analysis
[22, 27] and speech analysis [34, 63], face challenges in mobile envi-
ronments due to lighting variability, occlusion, and face significant
privacy risks. Recent studies have explored ultrasonic sensing to
address these issues. Wearable acoustic systems such as EarIO [38]
and EyeEcho [37] demonstrate that ultrasonic signals can track
fine-grained facial movements in real time. However, these systems
require earable or glasses-mounted hardware and assume stable
head–sensor geometry, making them unsuitable for mobile gaming,
where users frequently change posture and do not wear additional
devices. Moreover, these systems focus on expression classification
or geometric motion tracking, rather than modeling continuous
valence–arousal dynamics. Recent mobile-based acoustic systems
like ExpresSense [24], UFace [68], and EARFace [79] recognize dis-
crete facial expressions but are less suited for tracking rapid, subtle
emotional transitions that characterize gameplay. They also do
not address practical challenges unique to mobile gaming—such
as two-handed screen interactions, which generate severe acoustic
artifacts and corrupt the facial echo signal.
We present RageSense, which bridges these gaps by enabling
continuous valence–arousal estimation directly from a standard
smartphone, requiring no additional hardware, and by introduc-
ing an IMU-informed filtering mechanism to suppress acoustic
corruption caused by intense touch interactions. This system inte-
grates near-ultrasonic emotion detection with large language model
(LLM)-generated interventions to support emotion regulation in
mobile gaming. The key contributions of this work include:
• Continuous Valence-Arousal Estimation for Game Environ-
ments: We introduce a novel approach to emotion recogni-
tion in mobile gaming by being the first (to our best knowl-
edge) to map acoustic spectrograms to continuous valence-
arousal space. This approach is enhanced by custom ultra-
sonic signals that reduce interference from smartphone arti-
facts, as well as the integration of IMU data to detect exces-
sive finger movements, ensuring better spectrogram quality
and more accurate valence-arousal estimation.
• Personalized, Context-Aware Interventions Based on Large
Language Models: Our system integrates emotion detection
with large language model (LLM)-powered interventions,
providing context-sensitive, personalized feedback based
on real-time game content and the player’s emotional state.
This tailored approach fosters more empathetic and effective
emotional regulation during gameplay.
• Comprehensive In-Field Study Evaluation: We conducted
an in-field study with 53 participants over three weeks to
evaluate the effectiveness of our emotion regulation system.
Compared to baseline methods, our system demonstrated

significant improvements in emotional regulation, provid-
ing clear evidence of its effectiveness in real-world gaming
scenarios.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. We first
review related work and present a formative study on player needs

2 Background and Related Work
We first summarize related work about facial emotion recogni-
tion methods, as well as smartphone-based emotion recognition
techniques. Then, we summarize existing research about user en-
gagement and emotions in the context of video games.
2.1 Negative Emotion in Games
Emotion recognition in gaming has become an increasingly impor-
tant area of research as it holds the potential to create more adaptive,
personalized gaming experiences[12, 28, 39, 62, 71]. The goal is to
detect players’ emotional states in real-time, allowing games to
respond dynamically and maintain engagement by adjusting diffi-
culty, providing feedback, or offering interventions[31, 33, 67, 83].
Traditional emotion recognition methods have predominantly re-
lied on either physiological signals or behavioral cues, such as facial
expressions and voice analysis. These methods, however, have their
own set of challenges, particularly when applied to interactive
gaming contexts[10, 15, 66].
Early research focused primarily on physiological sensing, such
as heart rate, skin conductance, and EEG, to monitor emotions.
These physiological signals provide valuable insights into arousal
levels and emotional states, as they are directly tied to the auto-
nomic nervous system. Studies have demonstrated the ability to
predict player emotions, such as frustration, excitement, or stress,
using these signals. For instance, Yannakakis et al[39]. developed
a system that integrated physiological data from sensors to detect
discrete emotional states like frustration and enjoyment during
gameplay. Similarly, Mishra et al[43]. used facial emotion recog-
nition techniques to detect the emotional states of players during
video game interactions.
2.2 Emotion Regulation and In-Game
Interventions
Given the increasing emotional nature of video games, recently,
more and more research efforts have shown that video games would
affect the emotional experience of players [7, 19]. Video games
offer players diverse emotional experiences, ranging from positive
emotions, such as happiness and satisfaction, to intense negative
emotions, like sadness and anger.
Many studies also proposed to understand the types of sensa-
tions elicited by these game products[13, 14, 17, 40, 50, 53]. These
explorations are based mainly on two approaches: model-based and
model-free. The first group uses a top-down approach, building
the model on a theoretical framework that maps the players’ affect.
For example, a model can be based on the game emotion theory
[32, 64], or a general emotion theory [48], such as the definition
of a set of parameters that describes the players’ behavior. In [54],
the researchers studied the players’ satisfaction with the game re-
wards (Looting System) in Massive Multiplayer Online (MMO) and
Multiplayer Online Battle Arena (MOBA) games through a set of
simulations. The second approach (bottom-up) infers the players’
emotions mapping them without the use of any particular theoreti-
cal framework. This type of players’ analysis can be performed in
two ways: studying the players’ behaviors in video games, or using
physiological information to infer players’ emotions.
Many techniques are developed to analyze and detect the emo-
tions of players. Mishra et al. [43] proposed a Hidden Markov Model
(HMM) based real-time player emotional recognition system using
recorded videos to capture player’s facial emotions. Granato et al.
[18] proposed a hardware and software setup to collect users’ phys-
iological data (e.g., EMG, EDA) and then asses players’ emotions
during video games. Another study [41] explored emotion recogni-
tion and inhibitory control for adolescent players during violent
games.
2.3 Ubiquitious Sensing for Emotion Detection
It is widely accepted that emotional changes are accompanied by
both behavioral reactions (e.g., facial expressions, speech) and phys-
iological responses (e.g., heart rates) [21, 23, 29, 52, 60, 65, 72, 77, 81].
However, directly measuring physiological signals with dedicated
sensors such as EEG is often impractical for everyday contexts like
gaming [6, 16, 25, 49]. Consequently, with smartphones becoming
central to social interaction and entertainment, researchers have
turned to their built-in sensors, observing that smartphone usage
is closely associated with user affective experiences. For instance,
prior work has inferred user’s mood from social media interaction
patterns [76] or predicted emotions using data from motion, touch,
and eye-tracking sensors during social network use [55]. Among
these, motion sensors are particularly effective due to their ubiq-
uity, low cost, and the richness of the movement data they capture,
making them a widely used vehicle for detecting emotions.
Facial expressions are a primary indicator of emotion, but tradi-
tional camera-based methods face challenges like lighting changes
and occlusion. To overcome these, acoustic sensing offers a promis-
ing alternative. Systems such as EarIO [38] and EyeEcho [37] intro-
duce wearable acoustic sensing that can continuously track facial
expressions. EarIO, deployed in earables, and EyeEcho, mounted
on glasses, emit acoustic signals towards the face to capture subtle
skin deformations, providing continuous real-time tracking. Kar et
al. [24] introduced ExpresSense, a system that uses near-ultrasound
signals from smartphones to detect facial expressions without a
camera. Similarly, Wang et al. [68] developed UFace, an acoustic
system recognizing seven typical facial expressions. These systems
show the feasibility of acoustic sensing for facial expression recog-
nition, focusing on discrete emotion classification.
In contrast, our work focuses on modeling emotion in the con-
tinuous dimensions of valence and arousal, better capturing the
dynamic nature of emotions in gaming. Discrete labels oversimplify
evolving emotional states, while continuous modeling reflects how
CHI ’26, April 13–17, 2026, Barcelona, Spain Liu et al.
emotions change over time. The valence-arousal model offers a fine-
grained representation of emotional experiences, enabling richer
descriptions beyond static categories. Russell [56] introduced this
framework, forming the foundation for affective modeling. Previ-
ous work [11, 45, 47, 70, 82] shows the value of continuous emotion
prediction across modalities. Our work extends this by applying the
valence-arousal model to mobile gaming and integrating acoustic
sensing for real-time emotion tracking and adaptive interventions.
3 Formative Study
To deeply understand players’ negative emotional experiences dur-
ing mobile gameplay and to identify challenges in implementing
an effective intervention system, we conducted focus group dis-
cussions and semi-structured interviews with typical mobile game
players. The goal of this qualitative study was to: 1) identify com-
mon triggers and manifestations of negative emotions, especially
frustration and rage, during gameplay; 2) explore players’ percep-
tions of their own emotional reactions and existing coping mecha-
nisms; and 3) understand practical challenges players perceive in
adopting technology-driven emotion-regulation interventions.
3.1 Participants and Methodology
We recruited 24 active mobile gamers (M=24.2, SD=2) from a local
university community. Participants played for at least one hour
daily for entertainment. Crucially, eligibility was determined through
a screening survey in which participants indicated the frequency of
intense negative emotions experienced during gaming over the past
month and provided valence–arousal (1–5) ratings and brief descrip-
tions of their most recent episode. Only individuals who reported
experiencing intense frustration or rage at least once per week were
enrolled, ensuring alignment with our target demographic.
Our methodology employed a two-stage qualitative approach.
We first conducted two 60-minute focus groups to uncover shared
experiences and common themes surrounding frustrating game-
play. These were followed by individual semi-structured interviews
to probe deeper into personal emotional responses and to gather
specific perceptions regarding the acceptance, usability, and privacy
concerns of potential technology-based interventions. All sessions
were moderated by two experienced researchers and audio-recorded
for subsequent thematic analysis.
3.2 Understanding Negative Emotions
Intervention in Mobile Gaming
DQ1: How to design an emotion recognition system that players
would actually trust and adopt?
Our formative study revealed a key tension: while the vast major-
ity of players (95.8%) were open to receiving in-game emotional
support, their acceptance hinged on addressing three key concerns:
data privacy(20/24), with users opposing the storage of personally
identifiable data like facial or audio recordings ; emotional trajec-
tory(18/24): Participants questioned whether the system could cap-
ture the dynamic nature of their in-game emotions in real-time; and
performance overhead(15/24), fearing that sensing would consume
critical system resources and introduce lag .
Elaborating on these concerns, players were skeptical of systems
that reduce complex emotions to static labels. They argued that the
timing of an intervention is critical. For instance, one participant
vividly expressed this concern: "What if the system tries to ’help’ me
based on my anger from the last lost team fight, right when we’ve just
won a new one? That would be incredibly annoying." Similarly, the
concern about performance overhead was a deal-breaker. Another
participant stated unequivocally, "If this system makes my phone hot
or the game lag, I’ll turn it off immediately. Smooth gameplay comes
first." These insights established two core design requirements: the
system must not only track the temporal dynamics of emotion
but also operate with negligible performance impact. This directly
motivated our choice to combine lightweight acoustic sensing with
a continuous valence-arousal model, as this approach is well-suited
for tracking real-time emotional shifts without compromising game
performance.
DQ2: How to deliver an intervention that feels genuinely helpful,
not just another distraction?
Our second design principle guides the intervention’s content and
delivery. A significant 87.5% of participants stressed the need for
personalization, a need driven by specific concerns about how a
generic system could fail. Their concerns centered on three potential
failures: improper timing (17/24), contextual mismatch (14/24), and
a patronizing tone (10/24).
These concerns were vividly illustrated by participants. On im-
proper timing, one player said, "If I’m concentrating on a gacha
pull and the system distracts me, I would get really angry." The issue
of contextual mismatch was equally critical, as another explained,
"When we lost a team fight because a teammate didn’t show up, the
system telling me to ’watch the minimap’ would just amplify my
frustration." Finally, a patronizing tone could undermine an expert
player’s experience: "I have over a thousand hours on this cham-
pion. For the system to tell me ’Don’t forget to use your ultimate’
after a complex tactical mistake feels like it’s treating me like a com-
plete novice." These examples highlight a critical requirement: an
effective system must understand the cause of an emotion, not just
its presence. This principle led us to explore solutions capable of
deep contextual understanding, making Large Language Models
(LLMs) a compelling choice. By interpreting emotional data along-
side in-game events, LLMs empower our system to handle these
subtleties—delivering feedback that is timely, relevant, and respect-
ful—thereby transforming a potential annoyance into meaningful
support.

Sense’s emotion detection and intervention. system.
Emotion Estimation. Traditional approaches, like visual-based

methods, while powerful, face privacy concerns and may require
cumbersome equipment. Touchscreen behavior models, though in-
tuitive, can be heavily influenced by the game type or the player’s
hand interactions, making it difficult to consistently map to emotion-
related signals like valence and arousal. To address these challenges,

4 RageSense Design
4.1 Acoustic Sensing-based Emotion Estimation
In this section, we propose an emotion recognition system using
acoustic sensing with the built-in speakers and microphones of
smartphones. The main technical challenges are recognizing facial
expressions in gaming environments, minimizing the interference
of acoustic sensing on the gameplay, and mapping acoustic fea-
tures to continuous valence and arousal values. To address these
challenges, we have designed three key modules: finger motion
removal, an acoustic sensing pipeline, and a neural network for
valence-arousal value estimation, shown in Figure 4.

4.1.1 IMU-Gated Motion Removal. In highly engaging or emo-
tionally charged gaming scenarios, players often exhibit intense
hand and finger movements, such as rapid screen tapping or swip-
ing, which can introduce significant motion-induced noise in the
acoustic sensing signals. These movements can interfere with the
accuracy of emotion detection based solely on acoustic features.
To address this challenge, we integrate a motion-aware approach
that leverages data from the Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) sen-
sors, including accelerometers and gyroscopes, to identify the echo
frames corrupted by aggressive taps/swipes.
Large-scale hand gestures, such as aggressive swiping or tapping,
produce distinct patterns in the IMU data, which can be used to
differentiate between minor and significant hand operations. To en-
sure accurate emotion detection, our system distinguishes between
two scenarios based on IMU data:
• Significant Hand Operations: where the IMU amplitude ex-
ceeds a predefined threshold, the system temporarily sus-
pends acoustic sensing to avoid distortion from motion noise.
CHI ’26, April 13–17, 2026, Barcelona, Spain Liu et al.
Figure 4: VA-based emotion estimation pipeline.
• Minor Hand Operations: when the IMU amplitude remains
low, indicating minimal interference with the acoustic sig-
nals, the system continues to rely on the acoustic features
for emotion detection.
To detect and isolate significant hand movements, we employ an
energy-based approach to analyze the vibrations generated by hand
operations. Specifically, we combine accelerometer and gyroscope
magnitudes to produce a unified vibration magnitude. Next, the
short-time energy is calculated by accumulating the squared values
of the combined signal within a sliding window. A threshold, set to
the mean plus 0.25 times the standard deviation of the energy, is
used to identify the onset of high-energy segments. As illustrated
in Figure 3, this approach effectively isolates segments of intense
hand movement during mobile gameplay, thereby preventing their
interference with the subsequent acoustic feature extraction
For passive sensing applications, such as finger tapping, we set
the window length to 0.1 seconds, which covers the duration of
typical passive vibration signals. After segmenting the vibration
signals, they are normalized relative to their maximum amplitude
to account for variations in the intensity of taps. This ensures that
the system can effectively differentiate between noise generated by
minor operations and genuine signals associated with significant
emotional responses.
4.1.2 Acoustic Signal Processing.
Frequency Modulation Artifacts Denoising. In near-ultrasonic Fre-
quency Modulated Continuous Wave (FMCW) systems, particularly
in mobile gaming contexts, the modulation process can introduce
low-frequency variations or gaps due to non-linearities in the fre-
quency sweep. These can result in audible low-frequency noise,
impacting the user experience, especially during gaming sessions.
To address these, we applied a frequency modulation artifact denois-
ing technique that smooths the FMCW signal. This involves using
a raised cosine window to smooth the beginning and end of the
signal, reducing abrupt changes. The method works by multiplying
the original signal with the raised cosine function at both ends,
ensuring smoother transitions and minimizing audible noise.
Interference Cancellation. In the dynamic setting of mobile gam-
ing, we leverage the smartphone’s own speaker to emit ultrasonic
signals, transforming it into an active sensor for capturing facial
expressions. The received signal, however, contains both the de-
sired expression-related features and strong reflections from static
objects, such as tables and chairs. To suppress these static reflec-
tions, we compute the difference between consecutive spectrograms,
which cancels out the stationary background and isolates subtle
facial signals. In addition, since both the speaker and microphone
are integrated within the same device, part of the emitted signal can
directly couple into the microphone, introducing interference with
a fixed delay. Inspired by the interference cancellation technique in
works [36–38], we estimate this constant delay using Time Align-
ment and subtract the known artifact during signal processing,
effectively eliminating the direct-path interference and focusing on
reflective signals from the user’s face.
4.1.3 Feature Extraction. Our feature extraction pipeline, illus-
trated in Figure 5, transforms subtle physical deformations of the
user’s face into a digital representation. The process begins when
the smartphone speaker emits an inaudible ultrasonic chirp signal
(TX). As the signal reflects off the user’s face, its echo patterns
are modulated by facial muscle movements, and these echoes are
captured by the phone’s dual microphones (RX ch1, RX ch2), cre-
ating a continuous two-channel data stream encoding the user’s
expression.
To decode this information, we compute the cross-correlation
between the transmitted (TX) and received (RX) signals. This step
reveals the time-varying signature of the facial echoes, converting
raw audio into structured correlation sequences. However, body
movements or hand gestures can introduce transient noise, re-
ducing signal stability. We apply a Gaussian filter to smooth the
correlation data, suppressing fluctuations while preserving facial
motion patterns.
Finally, we segment and stack these cleaned correlation sequences
frame-by-frame, creating a 2D matrix, termed a spectrogram. Each
row represents the acoustic response from a single chirp, forming
a spectrogram of the facial expression over time. This spectrogram
serves as the final input for our deep learning model.

4.1.4 Valence and Arousal Estimation. To map the acoustic spectro-
grams into continuous emotional states, we designed the valence-
arousal Estimation Network, a lightweight hybrid architecture opti-
mized for real-time mobile deployment. As illustrated in Figure 4, a
pipeline of specialized encoders is employed to map the acoustic pat-
terns in the spectrograms into the two-dimensional valence-arousal
space.
The process begins with a Spatial Feature Encoder (ResNet-18)
that efficiently scans the spectrogram to extract foundational local
patterns, which serve as the essential building blocks of an expres-
sion. These features are subsequently processed by a Contextual
Relations Encoder, a Sparse Transformer, which models long-range
dependencies to form a holistic contextual representation with an
efficient complexity of (𝑂(𝑁 log 𝑁)). To capture the temporal dy-
namics of emotional expression, the contextualized features are
then fed into a Temporal Dynamic Encoder (LSTM). The final hid-
den state from the LSTM is mapped by a Projection Head to predict
continuous values for valence and arousal.
To handle the continuous data stream, the system operates on a
sliding-window basis. We chose a 5-second window with a 2-second
stride following prior valence-arousal-regression studies [75, 84],
which showed that a 5-second temporal context is necessary to
capture a complete emotional fluctuation. To further enhance the
stability of the real-time output, we introduce a standard temporal
smoothness loss during training. Following common practice, we
penalize rapid frame-to-frame changes in the predicted valence and
arousal using a Huber-style difference loss, which is less sensitive
to outliers than a simple squared penalty.

4.2 LLM-powered Negative Emotion
Intervention
4.2.1 Adaptive Intervention Trigger Algorithm. As detailed in Ap-
pendix C, the algorithm processes real-time ultrasound-based affec-
tive signals to detect emotional shifts and provide context-aware
interventions during gameplay. It runs continuously while the ses-
sion is active. First, an ultrasound spectrogram is acquired, and the
system estimates the instantaneous valence 𝑉 (𝑡) and arousal 𝐴(𝑡)
using a Transformer+LSTM model. These values are appended to a
circular buffer. To smooth fluctuations, an exponential moving av-
erage (EMA) is applied to both valence and arousal. Once the buffer
is filled, dynamic thresholds are computed to detect significant
negative emotional shifts.
The thresholds are based on initial values from "anger/frustration"
instances in our dataset (𝑉0 = −0.453 ± 0.181, 𝐴0 = 0.480 ± 0.276).
A 60-second sliding window continuously calculates the player’s
current mean and standard deviation. The adaptive thresholds for
valence 𝑇𝑉 and arousal 𝑇𝐴 are set as:
𝑇𝑉 = min(𝑉0 − 𝑘𝜎𝑉 , 𝜇𝑉 − 𝑘𝜎𝑉 ), 𝑇𝐴 = max(𝐴0 + 𝑘𝜎𝐴, 𝜇𝐴 + 𝑘𝜎𝐴)
(4)
The sensitivity parameter 𝑘 linearly decays from 1.2 to 0.8 dur-
ing the session, minimizing false alarms while adapting to recent
emotional baselines. A six-player pilot study confirmed this range
reduces false alarms and avoids missing true frustration events. An
CHI ’26, April 13–17, 2026, Barcelona, Spain Liu et al.
intervention is triggered when valence falls below 𝑇𝑉 and arousal
exceeds 𝑇𝐴.
The algorithm uses the CUSUM change-point detection method[35],
tracking gradual shifts in valence-arousal. If a change-point is de-
tected, the system checks if the smoothed values exceed dynamic
thresholds. If met, screenshots of the current game state are cap-
tured for the intervention context.
Next, the system constructs a prompt for the cloud-based LLM,
combining valence-arousal history and captured images. The LLM
generates a personalized intervention message; if not, a default
message is provided. Finally, the intervention is displayed as a semi-
transparent pop-up for 5 seconds, ensuring minimal disruption.
Our intervention message is an in-app UI component displayed
through an Android OS-level overlay window. This mechanism
allows RageSense to present messages above the game without
accessing or modifying any game code.
4.2.2 LLM-based Content Generation. The intervention module of
the RageSense system uses a Large Language Model (LLM) to gen-
erate personalized, context-aware messages in response to detected
negative emotions. The LLM integrates two main input sources: real-
time gameplay screenshots and emotional data (valence-arousal
values). Game screenshots capture key aspects of the player’s cur-
rent situation, such as game status, challenges, and performance.
This visual data is used to generate targeted recommendations.
Meanwhile, valence-arousal data encodes the emotional state, offer-
ing direct insight into the player’s emotional condition and trends,
enhancing the intervention’s precision.
The prompt guides the LLM to deliver personalized, context-
aware responses by considering the player’s emotional state and
gameplay scenario. It prioritizes empathy, tailoring responses for
comfort in negative emotions and motivation for heightened emo-
tions. The LLM is encouraged to use humor where appropriate,
ensuring engaging and emotionally aligned responses. Responses
are concise (under 25 characters) and based on real-time game
context to enhance relevance and the player’s experience.
5 Study 1: Evaluation of Acoustic-Based
Emotion Estimation in Games
To validate our ultrasonic emotion detection, we invited the 24
participants from our formative study to a follow-up in-lab exper-
iment. Twenty individuals (13 males, seven females; aged 18- 35)
accepted the invitation, while four declined. Real-time interven-
tions can alter players’ natural emotional trajectories and suppress
the strong negative affect required for reliable ground-truth label-
ing. To avoid such confounds and ensure unbiased valence-arousal
measurements, Study 1 used an offline version of the system with-
out interventions. After briefing each participant on the study’s
procedures and obtaining their informed consent, we collected a
total of 35 hours of audio data, including 7 hours of emotional
expressions captured during gameplay.
5.1 Procedure
Game Types. To elicit a diverse range of emotional responses
within a limited timeframe, participants played three different
types of games: Fast-pace Game (MOBA-style): A fast-paced mul-
tiplayer online battle arena (MOBA) game was selected due to its
highly competitive nature, where win/loss outcomes significantly
influence emotions. Moderate-pace Game (Strategy Simulation):
A moderate-paced strategy simulation game was chosen to create
a more thoughtful, planning-oriented experience. While it lacks
the immediate intensity of action games, it still evokes emotional
responses through the tension of managing resources, building
strategies, and overcoming challenges. High-intensity Game (Battle
Royale): A battle royale game was included to provide moments of
high emotional engagement. With its unpredictable pacing, play-
ers experience adrenaline rushes during combat and heightened
emotional states as the match progresses towards its final stages,
fostering a sense of anxiety and excitement.
Experiment Procedure. Each participant completed 5 gaming ses-
sions with a total duration of approximately 90–120 minutes, each
lasting 15–25 minutes with breaks scheduled in between. During
the experiments, participants’ facial expressions were recorded us-
ing the smartphone’s front camera, and these videos served as the
ground truth source for ultrasonic spectrogram inputs.A prelimi-
nary version of the RageSense application ran continuously in the
background while participants were playing the game. The app
recorded IMU data and ultrasonic-derived features.
Annotation Process and Reliability. Building on the established
principle that individuals are the most reliable annotators of their
own emotions [59, 80], we adopted a self-annotation methodol-
ogy. We invited the same 20 participants from our study to label
their own facial expression videos, which were recorded using the
smartphone’s front camera during gameplay. To prepare them for
this task, all participants completed a systematic training program
structured in three stages: (1) theoretical instruction on the valence-
arousal dimensional emotion model; (2) guided practice, where they
learned to map valence-arousal scores to example video clips with
expert reference ratings; and (3) a calibration phase, where they
performed trial annotations with immediate feedback until their
ratings achieved a consistent and reliable level.
We employed a 9-point Likert scale for valence-arousal annota-
tion, a method widely adopted in prior studies [26, 42, 51]. Videos
were sampled at 3 fps, and each frame was rated by 10 randomly
selected participant-annotators, with the final value determined
through majority voting. Ratings were then linearly normalized
from [−4, 4] to [−1, 1]. The ground truth for each ultrasonic spec-
trogram was obtained by averaging the valence-arousal labels of
its time-aligned video frames.
Annotation reliability was evaluated using Krippendorff’s 𝛼,
yielding 𝛼 = 0.689 for valence and 𝛼 = 0.661 for arousal. According
to Krippendorff’s guidelines, valence annotations are acceptable
for exploratory studies, while arousal shows slightly lower agree-
ment but remains consistent with existing valence-arousal datasets
(typically 𝛼 = 0.4–0.65). [9, 61].

5.3 Valence and Arousal Estimation
Performance
In this section, we evaluate and present the accuracy of the user-
independent model and impact of different model architectures.
5.3.1 Leave-one-subject-out Cross Validation. Maintaining consis-
tent performance across different users has been a long-standing
challenge for most ubiquitous sensing systems. As shown in Fig-
ure 6, we employed a leave-one-subject-out cross-validation strategy
to evaluate the correlation coefficients (CCC) for valence (CCC𝑉 ),
arousal (CCC𝐴), and the combined PVA (Pearson’s Variance Aver-
age) metrics. The results show that while individual subjects exhibit
varying performance, the system consistently achieves a high cor-
relation across subjects, with most subjects demonstrating CCC
values above 0.5. Notably, the average performance for CCC𝑉 and
CCC𝐴 remained consistently strong, suggesting that the system is
robust and adaptable to different users.
A deeper analysis of the outlier cases, highlighted in Figure 6, re-
veals two core challenges impacting system performance. The first
is a physical challenge at the signal level. For P1 and P16, intense
gameplay engagement resulted in significant head movements, in-
troducing substantial motion artifacts. These artifacts corrupted
the facial signal, obscuring subtle expressions and undermining the
integrity of the feature extraction process, which in turn degraded
prediction accuracy. In contrast to this data-level challenge, P9’s
case exposes the problem of subjectivity in emotion annotation.
The participant consistently maintained a serious expression with
minimal affective cues. However, this objective "poker face" was
subjectively misinterpreted by human annotators as a negative
emotion (e.g., frustration), introducing a systemic bias into the
ground-truth labels. Consequently, although our model correctly
captured the absence of facial activity, this discrepancy with the
flawed ground truth artificially deflated the final correlation scores.
Together, these cases highlight a dual dilemma for models in nat-
uralistic settings: they must achieve physical robustness against
signal-level noise while also depending on semantic accuracy at
the label-level for effective learning.
These results suggest that, despite variations in individual player
behavior, the system demonstrates robustness and adaptability
across different users, confirming the model’s effectiveness for
practical applications.
5.3.2 Leave-One-Game-Out Cross-Validation. To evaluate the gen-
eralization capability of our system across different game scenarios,
we employed a leave-one-game-out cross-validation strategy on
a per-participant basis. For each of the five games played by our
20 participants, we iteratively designated one game as the test set
while using the remaining four for training. As illustrated in Fig-
ure 6, the results underscore the system’s high performance, with
the CCC𝑉 , CCC𝐴, and PVA metrics generally exceeding 0.6 across
all game scenarios.
These findings provide strong evidence that the model main-
tains robust and reliable predictive power across varied gaming
contexts. This consistent and strong performance highlights the sys-
tem’s adaptability and signals its significant potential for practical
applications in diverse interactive environments.
5.3.3 Impact of model architecture. To better map spectrogram
features to valence and arousal values, we compared several net-
work architectures, including CNN-based models like MobileNet,
transformer-based models, and hybrid models like MobileNet +
LSTM and Transformer + LSTM. We also implemented an Efficient-
Net model using facial images for comparison with state-of-the-art
visual emotion recognition methods.
As shown in Table 1, our Sparse Transformer + LSTM model
outperforms all other models, achieving CCC scores of 0.6115 for
valence, 0.6215 for arousal, and a PVA of 0.6165. The Transformer +
LSTM hybrid also showed strong performance, with CCC scores of
0.5771 for valence, 0.5542 for arousal, and a PVA of 0.5657. These
results emphasize the benefit of combining transformer encoders
for feature extraction and LSTM decoders for temporal modeling.
Overall, these results confirm that the architecture choice is
crucial for emotion estimation, with our Sparse Transformer +
LSTM model effectively capturing subtle emotional changes and
performing almost as well as video-based SOTA methods[58].

5.5 Robustness Evaluation
To validate the robustness of RageSense in real-world application
scenarios, we recruited 5 participants out of 20 participants for the

robustness study, with each completing 10 sessions. To ensure fair
model training and evaluation, the collected data were balanced
across different emotional states. We then conducted performance
tests under varying holding angles and body postures.
5.5.1 Impact of Holding Angles. Different from PC games, mobile
games need the user to hold the smartphone all the time. Through
a group survey, we found that 35-55° is the most common and com-
fortable angle range to hold the smartphone horizontally, such that
the line of sight is perpendicular to the smartphone screen. How-
ever, different users may have various preferences for smartphone-
holding angles during mobile gameplay, as shown in Fig. 7, which
includes three smartphone angles (35°, 45°, and 55°) facing the user’s
face. Therefore, we implemented the addition experiment (N=5)
to examine how the smartphone orientation would affect the es-
timation performance. During the registration phase for training,
the subjects recorded the echoes with the smartphone held at a
default angle (𝜃= 45°). We then captured the reflected echoes with
different smartphone angles from 35° to 55° to simulate the varying
smartphone holding behaviors in daily gameplay.
From Fig. 7, it is observed that the accuracy is highest at a 55°
holding angle, while the accuracy for the 35° and 45° angles is similar
and slightly lower. This result is contrary to our initial expectations
of a steady decline in accuracy with increasing angle. The higher
accuracy at 55° may be due to better alignment of the face with the
smartphone microphone’s coverage area compared to the 35° and
45° angles, where the face region is more displaced.
5.5.2 Impact of Body Postures. As the sound wave intensity de-
creases in inverse proportion to the squared distance between two
objects, as well as due to multi-path channel fading, the reflected
RageSense: Leveraging Acoustic Sensing and LLM-Based Intervention for Emotion Regulation in Mobile Gaming CHI ’26, April 13–17, 2026, Barcelona, Spain
signal quality may vary depending on the specific locations. In pre-
vious evaluations with the default experimental setting, we asked
participants to sit and keep the smartphone 20 cm away from the
face. To investigate the impacts of different user body postures on
recognition performance, we implemented another robustness tests
for different postures. In the experiment, we asked five participants
to perform the experiments in two common postures, including
sitting on a chair, and reclining on the bed.
The experimental results reveal that body posture significantly
influences system performance. Specifically, the sitting posture
yields superior performance across all metrics, achieving a con-
cordance correlation coefficient (CCC) of 0.611 for valence, 0.621
for arousal, and a combined PVA score of 0.616. In contrast, the
reclining posture leads to noticeable degradation, with CCC values
of 0.492 (valence), 0.588 (arousal), and a PVA score of 0.540. This
performance drop may be attributed to less stable hand-holding
behavior and increased variability in the relative positioning be-
tween the smartphone and the user’s face when reclining. Unlike
the seated posture—where users often stabilize their arms against a
table, maintaining a consistent and optimal sensing distance—the
reclining position may introduce subtle posture shifts due to gravi-
tational effects on the arm, leading to degraded signal quality and
recognition accuracy. These findings suggest that user posture plays
a non-trivial role in wireless sensing applications and must be con-
sidered when designing robust and generalizable affect recognition
systems.
5.5.3 Robustness to Game Audio. A key consideration is the sys-
tem’s robustness to the game’s own audio, as both the ultrasonic
probe signal (18–22 kHz) and the audible game sounds (<10 kHz)
are emitted simultaneously from the same speaker. Our design
leverages this clear spectral separation. We empirically validated
this by measuring the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) of the received
echo, observing only a negligible 0.34 dB drop when game audio
was active. This confirms that concurrent game sound has virtually
no impact on sensing performance.
5.5.4 Robustness to Dynamic, Noisy Environments, and User Char-
acteristics. We expanded the robustness evaluation to include dy-
namic and noisy environments, as well as various user characteris-
tics.The results are summarized in Table 3. We invited 5 participants
from Study 1 to participate in a supplementary session for environ-
mental robustness validation.
Noisy Environment. We evaluated RageSense in a high-noise
indoor public environment (i.e. cateteria, 78.42 dB) characterized by
strong non-stationary ambient noise (e.g., human chatter, utensil
impacts, and broadcast noise). Although this noise is outside the
ultrasonic band, its harmonic components partially overlap with
the sidelobes of the transmitted near-ultrasonic signal, weakening
the quality of the received echoes. As a result, the system showed
a mild performance drop (CCC𝑉 ↓8.81%, CCC𝐴 ↓8.08%), but the
accuracy remained within an acceptable range, indicating that the
core ultrasonic features were still preserved despite heavy acoustic
interference.
Dynamic Environment. we conducted tests in an outdoor walk-
way with frequent pedestrian movement and varying ambient noise
levels (70.11 dB). This environment contained transient occlusions
and secondary reflections from moving bodies, which could poten-
tially disturb the non-line-of-sight echo path. However, because
the primary acoustic path—between the smartphone and the user’s
face—is substantially stronger than reflections from surrounding
motion, performance degradation remained limited (CCC𝑉 ↓2.39%,
CCC𝐴 ↓4.92%). These results demonstrate that RageSense maintains
stable accuracy even under typical everyday human movement.
Skin Tones. We included participants with medium-to-dark skin
tones (N=3). Compared to lighter-skin participants in the controlled
study, we observed a moderate decrease in valence-arousal estima-
tion concordance (CCC𝑉 ↓9.03%, CCC𝐴 ↓14.35%). While we did
not collect sufficient data to identify the underlying cause, we note
that two of these participants had substantial facial hair, which may
partially obscure fine-grained geometric changes captured through
ultrasonic reflections. We note that the sample imbalance reflects
our local demographic pool.
Accessories and Hair Styles. We examined robustness to com-
mon user attributes, including eyewear and hair configuration. Per-
formance remained consistent across users with glasses (N=13) and
without glasses (N=7), indicating that minor multipath reflections
from eyeglass frames had limited impact. Similarly, both long-hair
(N=9) and short-hair (N=11) participants showed negligible per-
formance differences (PVA 0.6118–0.6221). These results suggest
that typical variations in accessories and hair styles exert minimal
influence on the primary smartphone-to-face echo path.
These robustness experiments, while based on modest sample
sizes, indicate that RageSense maintains reasonable performance
across noisy/dynamic environments and diverse user characteris-
tics, supporting its applicability in real-world mobile gaming sce-
narios.
5.6 Discussion
The results demonstrate the significant effectiveness of acoustic-
based emotion detection in gaming scenarios. First, the system
performed exceptionally well in tracking emotional fluctuations
within a single gaming session, proving the feasibility of ultrasonic-
based emotion detection in dynamic gaming environments. We
chose to allow the system to learn across different games to adapt to
changes in user emotions and external disturbances. This approach
enables the system to maintain stable performance across multiple
sessions, and despite some fluctuations, it still effectively captures
emotional changes.
However, although the system excels at filtering hand move-
ments, variations in smartphone holding angles can still lead to
inaccurate predictions, highlighting the challenges of emotion de-
tection in real-world environments. Despite the influence of hand
posture on detection, the system was still able to effectively iden-
tify intense emotional fluctuations, as both valence and arousal
are time-varying. To further improve the system’s robustness and
practicality, the subsequent Study 2 will explore how to enhance

6 Real-time System Implementation
Study 1 showed the effectiveness of the proposed approach in esti-
mating user’s emotion status, which is the foundation of emotion
intervention.
6.1 Prototype Application
To assess the practicality of real-time emotion estimation and ex-
plore the effectiveness of in-game interventions, we developed a
prototype mobile application for the Android OS (Appendix A).
The app operates in the background, emitting inaudible ultrasound
waves and analyzing the characteristics of the reflected echoes to
predict the user’s emotional state, including valence and arousal
levels. When significant fluctuations in these emotional parameters
are detected, the app captures a screenshot of the current game
content and sends it, along with historical valence and arousal
data, to a cloud server. The cloud server then uses a large language
model (LLM) API to interpret both the game content and the user’s
emotional state, generating a personalized intervention message
that is sent back to the mobile app. This message is displayed as a
brief, semi-transparent pop-up, which is strategically positioned
away from core gameplay controls and interactive elements. It au-
tomatically disappears after 5 seconds to avoid interfering with
the fast-paced nature of gameplay. This ensures that users receive
targeted emotional regulation suggestions without disrupting the
intensity or flow of their game.
To benchmark the performance of our emotion detection sys-
tem, we also created two baseline applications: one that triggers
interventions based on predefined contextual cues, and another
that triggers interventions at random intervals. This comparison
allows us to evaluate the relative effectiveness of context-aware
interventions versus arbitrary ones.

7 Study 2: In-field Study with real-time
Intervention In Mobile Gaming
To evaluate the effectiveness of RageSense’s emotion-aware, LLM-
based intervention system, we conducted a field study with 53 new
participants. Given the relatively small sample size, we utilized a
Latin Square design to ensure each participant experienced all three
intervention models. The order of conditions was counterbalanced
across participants to mitigate potential sequence effects.
7.1 Intervention Conditions
Throughout the gameplay, RageSense’s emotion detection module
continuously monitored participants’ emotional states. The key
distinction between the intervention models was the nature of the
intervention triggered when a participant’s negative emotional
indicators exceeded a predefined threshold:
Control condition (Pre-defined Text-Based Intervention). : In this
condition, intervention requests were triggered based on predicted
valence-arousal values derived from the emotion detection model.
The system selected intervention messages randomly from a prede-
fined text library, offering non-adaptive guidance. The messages
were delivered in the participant’s preferred format (either text or
voice).
Proposed condition (LLM-based Personalized Intervention). : This
condition also used predicted valence-arousal values from the emo-
tion detection model to trigger interventions. However, unlike the
Control Condition, the intervention messages were generated dy-
namically by a large language model (LLM). The system processed
real-time game screenshots and emotional data to generate per-
sonalized, contextually relevant suggestions or supportive mes-
sages. The intervention was adjusted dynamically based on the
participant’s emotional state, aiming to provide more tailored and
context-aware interventions.
Baseline condition (Random Timing-based Intervention). : In this
condition, interventions were triggered at random intervals, ir-
respective of the participant’s emotional state. The intervention
messages were randomly selected from the predefined text library,
offering non-adaptive and uncontextualized guidance. This condi-
tion served as the baseline for comparison.
7.2 Metric
The field study spanned over three weeks, with each participant
completing multiple sessions of gameplay. As the study took place
in the field, the duration of each session varied depending on the
participant’s schedule, but it was required that each session lasted
at least one hour. To evaluate the effectiveness of the interventions,
two primary types of data were collected:
Emotional Change Metrics: The primary measure was the imme-
diate impact of interventions on players’ emotional states. With
user consent, we accessed users’ valence-arousal values during
their interaction with the app, along with in-game recording data,
to assess the effectiveness of the intervention. Emotion data from
one minute before and one minute after each intervention was ex-
tracted and changes in valence (pleasantness) and arousal (tension
level) were computed. The effectiveness of the intervention was
assessed by comparing the average post-intervention emotional
improvement (increase in valence and decrease in arousal) for each
participant across all intervention instances.
Subjective Questionnaire Responses: Participants completed a post-
experiment survey assessing their perceptions of the intervention.
The survey included satisfaction ratings (on a five-point Likert
scale), the perceived helpfulness and contextual relevance of the
intervention, and general acceptability (e.g., whether they found
the interventions intrusive). The experimental group provided ad-
ditional feedback on the LLM-generated messages, including their
perceived naturalness and empathetic quality, while the control
group was asked about their impressions of the pre-defined mes-
sages.
7.3 Procedure and Participants
The study was conducted over a three-week period, with partici-
pants completing one session per week. A call for new participants
who had not been involved in Study 1 was posted in the university
community, where participants were selected based on the follow-
ing criteria: they must own an Android smartphone and have prior
experience with mobile gaming. Consistent with the demographic
distribution of MOBA players—where young adults (18–30 years
old) constitute the dominant player segment according to prior
market reports[2, 3] and game analytics[20]. Our recruitment fo-
cused on university students. A total of 85 participants responded
to the call, from which 15 participants discontinued their partici-
pation, 10 encountered software compatibility issues, and 7 raised
privacy concerns. In the end, 53 participants completed the study
and provided feedback.
To ensure the robustness of the results, we employed a Latin
Square design to mitigate order effects. Each participant was ran-
domly assigned to a specific order of intervention conditions (Con-
trol, Baseline, or Proposed), with each intervention being experi-
enced in a different week. The order of interventions was coun-
terbalanced across participants, ensuring that each intervention
model was tested evenly throughout the study period. Participants
were free to schedule their gameplay sessions at their convenience,
ensuring a more naturalistic and flexible in-field testing environ-
ment. The study has been approved by the Internal Review Board
of [the institute name is hidden for double-blinded review].

8 Discussion and Limitation
8.1 Beyond the Individual:The Social Context of
Emotion
A key limitation of RageSense is its current focus on individual
emotion, which may not fully capture the rich social context of
multiplayer gaming[44]. This can occasionally lead to interventions
that are contextually inappropriate. A promising direction for fu-
ture work is to extend the system to foster interpersonal emotion
regulation (iER) skills, which are vital for teamwork. We envision
evolving RageSense from a reactive "regulator" to a proactive social-
emotional learning (SEL) "partner". Such a system would analyze
team-level emotional data to facilitate supportive interactions, for
instance, by suggesting an encouraging message when a teammate
is struggling. This is particularly valuable in common scenarios like
friends playing together, shifting the goal from merely mitigating
individual rage to actively fostering more cohesive and positive
team dynamics, ultimately enhancing the shared social experience
8.2 Not All Game Contexts Are Equal: Matching
Interventions to Gameplay
While personalized interventions were highly effective in reduc-
ing emotional distress in Simulation Strategy games, their impact
RageSense: Leveraging Acoustic Sensing and LLM-Based Intervention for Emotion Regulation in Mobile Gaming CHI ’26, April 13–17, 2026, Barcelona, Spain
was less pronounced in fast-paced, team-based games like MOBA
(Multiplayer Online Battle Arena), where emotional fluctuations
were less noticeable. This suggests that the effectiveness of emo-
tional interventions may vary significantly depending on the pacing
and structure of the game. In fast-paced team games, emotional
changes might be subtler or harder to mitigate through interven-
tions. As seen in figure 9, there was little change in playtime after
personalized interventions in such games. For these types of games,
exploring alternative intervention modalities, such as subtle tactile
or audio cues instead of intrusive text, may be more effective. Fur-
ther testing across a broader range of game genres is necessary to
refine the intervention system for greater applicability.
8.3 Short-Term Emotional Effects and
Long-Term Observations
While RageSense reliably reduces negative affect within a game-
play session, our three-week field deployment did not reveal clear
evidence of cumulative longer-term reductions in rage levels. To
assess whether rage incidents decreased as participants continued
using the system, we examined the intervention trigger frequency
as a proxy for rage events and computed the Pearson correlation be-
tween chronological session order and the number of interventions.
The correlation was negligible (Pearson 𝑟 = −0.13), suggesting
that intervention frequency did not exhibit any meaningful linear
decline throughout the study period. These findings indicate that
RageSense provides acute, moment-to-moment emotional stabiliza-
tion, but such short-term benefits did not aggregate into measurable
long-term behavioral change within three weeks. These observa-
tions highlight an important area for future investigation: whether
longer-term use could cultivate more durable emotional regulation
skills beyond immediate gameplay. Longer-term, context-controlled
studies will be needed to evaluate these broader effects.
8.4 Privacy Risks and Implications
Although RageSense avoids visual capture, the handling of affective
and privacy information warrants careful consideration. Below, we
reflect on the privacy risk and implications of our design choices.
8.4.1 Minimization of Affective Data Exposure. Although valence
-arousal traces are low-dimensional, they represent sensitive affec-
tive information that may implicitly reflect a user’s stress level,
frustration, or emotional stability. To reduce the exposure of such
data, RageSense performs all valence–arousal inference locally on
the smartphone, thereby preventing raw ultrasonic reflections or
intermediate spectrogram features from leaving the device. Only
when a notable emotional fluctuation is detected, a short 5-second
valence-arousal history and five obfuscated gameplay screenshots
(which are anonymized through the app’s built-in Optical Character
Recognition (OCR) function that locates text regions and pixelates
potentially sensitive strings—e.g., player usernames/IDs, teammate
names, and chat messages—thereby reducing identifiability and
cross-session linkage) are transmitted for intervention generation.
While this design substantially limits the amount of affective data
shared, we acknowledge that even minimal emotional traces can
carry inference risks, and further reductions remain an important
direction for future work.
8.4.2 Identifiability and Characteristics of Ultrasonic Reflections.
Prior work [46] has shown that rich skeletal trajectories or fine-
grained motion patterns can serve as signatures for user re- identi-
fication. Ragesense does capture fine-grained variations in facial
micro-movements and device interaction behaviors. However, these
reflections encode only non-semantic perturbations in facial ge-
ometry and do not contain reconstructible facial appearance or
body skeletal trajectories. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that any
physiological or behavioral signal may carry residual identifiability
risk, and a fuller investigation remains a direction for future work.
8.4.3 Cloud-Assisted Interventions and Practical Trade-Offs. Our
current design relies on a cloud-based LLM to generate personalized
interventions. While this requires network connectivity, the mech-
anism is strictly non-blocking: if the connectivity or cloud server
is unavailable, gameplay proceeds uninterrupted. As lightweight
multimodal on-device models [30, 73, 74] continue to mature, fully
local intervention generation may become feasible, further reduc-
ing data transmission latency and tightening the system’s privacy
boundary.
9 Conclusion
This paper presents RageSense, a system for detecting and regu-
lating negative emotions in mobile gaming using near-ultrasonic
acoustic sensing. Unlike traditional methods, RageSense avoids vi-
sual and audio-based surveillance, offering a non-intrusive, accurate
solution for real-time emotion estimation. Paired with personal-
ized, context-aware interventions powered by a large language
model (LLM), it effectively reduces negative emotion such as rage,
frustration and enhances the gaming experience, particularly in
Simulation Strategy games.
